# The intro: what starts a record, and what that owes the rest of it

Research first, then the tables. Everything below is a named source or a
measurement of this program; anything neither is marked `[chosen]`.

The questions this document answers are the owner's:

> If X starts the song and X is going to come back, do we want to start with X
> stripped down? If Y starts, what does it do to the other instruments? Does
> the starting instrument have a solo later? If it is drums, do we let the
> drums be the focal point?

Three of those four have sourced answers and one of them has a partial one.

---

## 1. What this program did before this, measured

`node tools/roll.ts --sweep lofi 1 20 --map` reads the record back out of its
own MIDI file and reports who is playing in each bar (twenty seeds a genre, at
the genre's own length):

| | lofi | dungeon synth |
|---|---|---|
| parts in bar one | 2.3 | 1.1 |
| bars before anything else arrives | 7.3 | 12.8 |
| bar at which every part is finally in | 19.1 (39% into the record) | 38.8 (40%) |
| **the opening is heard with room round it again** | **14%** | **80%** |
| who opens | `keys+drums`, every record | `drone`, every record |

Read the bold line for lofi: **what the record opens with is, six times in
seven, never heard with room round it again.** The intro is a way in and
nothing else — it makes no promise the record keeps. Dungeon synth scores
well on the same measure for a structural reason rather than a musical one:
its opener is a drone that is in nearly every section anyway.

And two more facts fall out of the same table. The opening is *always the
same parts*, because the intro takes the first N of the genre's entry order
and nothing else is ever considered. And at 82 bpm a lofi intro of eight bars
runs **23 seconds**, which is a 1985 intro (§2).

## 2. An intro is short, and the point of it is the moment it ends

> Intros that averaged more than 20 seconds in the mid-80s are now only about
> 5 seconds long — a 78 percent drop… "If people can skip so easily and at no
> cost, you have to do something to grab their attention."
> — Hubert Léveillé Gauvin, "Drawing listener attention in popular music:
> Testing five musical features arising from the theory of attention economy",
> *Musicae Scientiae* 22(3), 2018, 291–304, reported in ScienceDaily,
> 4 April 2017 (303 top-10 singles, 1986–2015; tempo up ~8%, time to the
> title down ~18%)

> I can't remember hearing an intro that was too short, but I've heard many
> that were too long… simple strummed chords should last no more than 10
> seconds, tops.
> — Gary Ewer, "Song Intros: Making them Relevant and Enticing",
> secretsofsongwriting.com

And what the shortness is *for*:

> In some songs an introductory passage precedes the establishment of the main
> rhythm… **The moment of the rhythmic hook in these records comes when the
> introduction finally ends and the main rhythm 'kicks in'.**
> — Gary Burns, "A typology of 'hooks' in popular records", *Popular Music*
> 6/1 (1987), pp. 7–8

**What this constrains.** An intro is measured in SECONDS, not bars: eight
bars is four seconds at 240 bpm and twenty-three at 82. The genre states a
ceiling and the length is drawn from the lengths that fit under it. And the
thing the intro is building is not itself — it is the bar the rest of the
record starts on.

---

## 3. What opens the record has to be material to it

> Make the intro relevant to the rest of the song. There needs to be something
> about that intro that sounds **material to the main sound and message**.
> — Ewer, ibid.

> An intro… establish[es] the tempo and basic rhythmic structure, establish[es]
> the key, establish[es] the mood.
> — ibid.

> Consider an intro melody that is different from your verse and chorus
> melodies… it **can then be used to help take the end of a chorus to the
> beginning of the next verse, or serve as an outro**.
> — ibid.

The typology of what an intro can be, which is the shape a genre table wants:

> **the chord progression** of your song one or more times through and then
> starting the vocals… **instrumental melodies on top of those chord
> progressions**… or launch **directly into your chorus**.
> — "Five Different Types of Introductions You Can Use In Songwriting",
> planetarygroup.com

**What this constrains, and it is the owner's first question.** *If X starts
the record and X comes back, start with X stripped down* — yes, and the
sources say why: the intro is the same material with something withheld, and
what it withholds is what arrives when the intro ends. So an intro is not a
different section, it is the record's own idea with the tune, or the beat, or
both, held back.

**And the owner's third question — does the opener come back?** Ewer's intro
melody *returns as a link and as the outro*, and a record that opens on a
thing and never lets that thing be heard alone again has spent its opening.
The first attempt at this was a shed order that protected the opening so it
would be the last part dropped. **Measured, it did nothing at all** (§7), and
what the measurement showed was the real obstacle: this program's floor is
`fewest` — three parts — so nothing after bar one is ever heard with room
round it. What the opening needed was not protection but somewhere to be
alone, which is the break (§5).

## 4. If the drums start, the drums are the point

> Many records begin with the strong establishment of a rhythm. **Use of solo
> drums, solo bass, or drums and bass in duet at the start of a record will
> attract especially great attention to rhythm because there is little or no
> melody or harmony to attend to, and no lyrics.** Records which begin this
> way include the Strangeloves' 'I Want Candy', … the Rolling Stones' 'Honky
> Tonk Women', … Michael Jackson's 'Billie Jean', … Dolly Parton's '9 to 5'.
> — Burns 1987, pp. 6–7

> In other cases, the establishment of a rhythm may be combined with the
> establishment of **a riff, that is, a repetitive melodic hook** … In these
> cases the rhythmic modulation is somewhat less noticeable because the
> listener must also pay attention to melody.
> — ibid., p. 7

**What this constrains, and it is the owner's fourth question.** Yes — and
the source is stronger than the question: a drum intro only works *because
there is nothing else to attend to*. So a rhythm-led intro is drums, or drums
and bass, and **nothing else**; and it is the one intro this program must not
thin, because thinning the drums in an intro whose whole subject is the drums
takes away the thing being introduced. Before this, lofi opened `keys + drums`
with the drums thinned — which is neither a rhythm intro nor a chord intro,
and attends to nothing in particular.

---

## 5. The break: the one place a record goes below its floor

> A breakdown is "a section of a song in which various instruments have solo
> parts (breaks)"… the **stripping away of other instruments and vocals**
> helps create intense contrast… breakdowns **usually precede or follow
> heightened musical climaxes**… it replaced the bridge typically found in
> such a record **after the second chorus**.
> — en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breakdown_(music) (which also records Tom Moulton's
> disco break, "a rhythm-only section of the recording")

**What this constrains.** One section of a record — the last quiet one before
the peak, or the first after it, which is where a bridge sits — drops below
the arrangement's floor and carries **what the record opened with, and
nothing else**. That is the answer to the owner's third question: the opening
part does get its moment, and the moment is a documented section type rather
than an invention. It is the only place this program is allowed under
`fewest`, and the reason it is allowed there is that without it no part is
ever alone after bar one.

## 6. What goes into the program

| | rule | source |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | An intro's length is drawn from the lengths that fit under a ceiling **in seconds**, which the genre states | Léveillé Gauvin 2018; Ewer |
| 2 | An intro is one of three KINDS, drawn: **rhythm** (drums, or drums and bass, and nothing else), **bed** (the foundation without the tune), **hook** (the tune from bar one) | Burns 1987; planetarygroup's typology |
| 3 | A **rhythm** intro is not thinned — the drums are the subject | Burns 1987 |
| 4 | Every other intro IS thinned, and withholds the tune, so that the tune's arrival is what the intro was for | Burns 1987 ("kicks in"); Ewer |
| 5 | One section — where a bridge sits, before the peak or just after it — is a **break**: below the floor, carrying what the record opened with and nothing else | Wikipedia, "Breakdown (music)"; Moulton's disco break |
| 6 | A genre whose drums are not its subject says so and takes the rhythm intro out of its own pool | dungeon synth's own research: "primarily beatless" |

Weights are `[chosen]` inside what the sources describe: they name the kinds
and rank nothing, so the default leans on the bed — this program's own
existing behaviour — with a rhythm intro one time in four and a hook intro
one time in eight.

## 7. What was tried and measured at nothing

**A shed order that protects the opening.** Ewer's line about the intro
returning suggested a cheap rule: move whatever opened the record to the end
of the shed order, so it is the last part dropped. It was built, and then
measured over sixty seeds with the rule on and off:

| | rule on | rule off |
|---|---|---|
| the opening is in the outro | 100% | 100% |
| the opening is present in a section | 97% | 97% |
| the opening is in the record's thinnest section | 82% | 82% |

Identical, because a section here sheds ONE part at a time and the first name
in the shed order is never an opener in either genre. A knob that does nothing
is this program's cardinal sin, so it is not a knob: the code carries the note
and the field does not exist. The break is what the idea actually needed.

## 8. What is still not done

**The intro's own material.** Rule 2's *hook* intro starts the tune from bar
one, but no intro here writes a melody of its own — Ewer's "intro melody that
is different from your verse and chorus melodies" is a fourth kind this
program has no builder for. Named here so it is not mistaken for an oversight.

**A break that is a real solo.** The break carries the record's openers — up
to two parts. A break of ONE part, with a line written for being alone rather
than a line written for a texture that happens to have thinned, is a further
step: the material stage would have to know that a part is exposed there.

## 9. What it came to, measured

Same tool, twenty seeds each, at the genre's own length
(`node tools/roll.ts --sweep <genre> 1 20 --map`):

| | lofi before → after | dungeon synth before → after |
|---|---|---|
| bars before the opening is joined | 7.3 → **6.3** | 12.8 → **9.5** |
| bar at which everyone is in | 39% → **39%** of the record | 40% → **37%** |
| **the opening is heard with room round it again** | 14% → **34%** | 80% → **78%** |
| ways in used across the twenty | one | one → two |

Dungeon synth was already at 80% because its opener is a drone that is in
nearly every section and its floor is low; the intro kinds cost it two points
and bought it a second way in. Lofi is the change this was for: **one record
in seven used to return to its opening, and now one in three does** — every
one of those is a break, and a record without a bridge still has nowhere to
put one.
