# The story, the sections, and every way a loop can change

Deep research on six questions: how a loop can be altered (inside and outside itself),
what sections actually exist (a section is not a letter), what an outro *is* and why
having intros without them is incoherent, whether our sections express the point of the
song, how songs tell a story — characters, conflict, resolution — and why a motif should
arrive incomplete the first time.

This is the third research pass (after `LOOP_TO_SONG.md` and
`DEEP_RESEARCH_ARRANGEMENT.md`) and it goes past production craft into music theory and
narrative theory, because the questions asked are not production questions.

---

## 1. Why we say a song tells a story

Not a metaphor — there is a formal theory of it. Byron Almén, *A Theory of Musical
Narrative* (Indiana UP), defines musical narrativity as **"articulating the dynamics of
conflict and coordinating events into an interpretive whole."** Three parts of it map
directly onto what an engine can build:

**The characters are MOTIVES, and they are literally called agents.** In Almén's
analysis, "motives a and b function as anthropomorphised syntactic units, or **musical
agents**, within the meaningfully unfolding temporal process of a narrative trajectory."
The protagonist of a song is its motif. That is the character.

**The conflict is between motives — and between a motive and its harmonic ground.**
"Motives a and b constitute **oppositional elements**, and the narrative program consists
of various attempts to bring the two motives into a more harmonious relationship." So a
song's B section is not merely "different music" — it is the *antagonist*. The reason ABA
is a form and not a playlist is that A and B are in conflict, and the return of A is the
outcome of that conflict.

**The resolution is synthesis plus arrival.** "A **synthesis of both motives**, supported
by rhythmic, harmonic, melodic and registral elements, has taken place, and the arrival
of **tonic harmony** indicates the resolution of the narrative conflict." Resolution is
two things at once: the motives reconciled, and the harmony coming home.

Almén's other key term is **markedness and rank** — narrative works by one element being
*marked* (unusual, foregrounded) against a normative background, and by that ranking
being **revalued over time**. What was subordinate becomes dominant, or vice versa. That
is the engine-legible definition of a story: *the hierarchy between the voices changes*.

### What this means for us, concretely

Our engine currently has one protagonist (the lead) and no antagonist. Each form letter
gets its own cell, which gives *contrast*, but the letters have no **relationship** —
B is not derived from A, does not oppose it, and A's return is not a *consequence* of B.
Three things follow:

- **B should be derived from A by opposition**, not generated independently: inverted
  contour, the complementary register, the rhythm displaced, the mode darkened.
- **The return of A should be revalued** — A after B is not A before B. It should come
  back higher, fuller, re-orchestrated, or harmonically resolved in a way it wasn't.
- **Resolution should be audible as arrival on the tonic** at the final return, and our
  progressions currently loop indifferently — nothing ever *lands*.

## 2. Why a motif should arrive incomplete

The instinct in the question is exactly right and it has a name: **motivic allusion** —
"a theme that emerges in **fragments long before it fully unfolds** — like a shadow of
the future."

Film-scoring practice states the rule as: foreshadowing should be "a subtle play with
expectation, **never a spoiler**." The theme is present early, but something is withheld,
so that the full statement is an *event*.

What gets withheld, in practice:
- **Notes** — only the head of the motif, or its skeleton without the ornament
- **Register** — heard low and quiet before it is heard high and open
- **Instrument** — heard on a background voice before the lead takes it
- **Harmony** — heard over an unresolved or ambiguous chord before it is heard over the
  tonic
- **Completion** — the phrase is cut off before its answer

And `009` already gave us the production-side version of the same device, which I quoted
two research passes ago and never built: the collaborator *"took the main notes from my
chorus melody and played them on different synths during the intro and in the background
of the verses."* Same motif, reduced, elsewhere, early.

**The corollary the question draws is the important one:** if the first statement is
already complete, every later statement can only be repetition. The full arrival has
nothing to reveal. So the *first* appearance should be partial **by design**, and the
peak is where the motif is finally whole — which is exactly the peak-first idea, but
applied to the MOTIF rather than to the arrangement density.

## 3. Every way a loop can be altered

Two categories, as asked: changes to the material itself, and changes to the frame
around it.

### A. INSIDE the loop — the material changes

**Classical motivic transformation** (the canonical set, confirmed across theory
sources):

| Technique | What it does |
|---|---|
| Repetition | State it again unchanged |
| **Sequence** | Restate at a different scale degree, stepwise up or down |
| **Inversion** | Flip the intervals — every rise becomes a fall |
| **Retrograde** | Play it backwards |
| Retrograde inversion | Backwards *and* upside down |
| **Augmentation** | Stretch the durations — the motif becomes grander, slower |
| **Diminution** | Compress the durations — the motif becomes urgent |
| **Fragmentation** | Take a recognisable piece and develop that alone |
| **Extension** | Add a tail to the phrase |
| **Truncation** | Cut the phrase short |
| **Modulation** | Same shape, new key |
| Ornamentation | Add passing tones, turns, grace notes |
| Reharmonisation | Same melody, different chords underneath |

**Thematic transformation** deserves its own entry because it is *not* variation. Wikipedia
on the technique: "a basic theme is reprised throughout a work, but it undergoes constant
transformations and disguises and is **made to appear in several contrasting roles**" —
and critically, "the transformed theme **has a life of its own and is no longer a sibling
to the original**." Liszt perfected it; Wagner's leitmotifs and essentially all modern
film scoring depend on it. This is how *one* motif becomes the heroic version, the
tragic version, the tender version. It is the single most valuable unimplemented idea for
this engine, because it produces genuine sectional contrast **from one source**, which is
the thing we keep failing to do — either generating unrelated material or repeating.

**Rhythmic / performance alterations** (no pitch change, so no harmonic risk):
displacement (shift the figure by a beat), swing/straight, subdivision change,
syncopation, ghost notes, accent pattern, articulation (staccato/legato), velocity
contour, humanised timing.

**Registral / textural**: octave transposition, voicing (close/open/drop-2), inversion of
the chord, doubling at the 3rd/6th/octave, thinning to skeleton, density of ornament.

### B. OUTSIDE the loop — the frame changes

- **Which voices play** (the subtractive arrangement — our main lever)
- **Expression** — level, filter, dynamics (`005`'s third and fourth legal moves)
- **Timbre / re-orchestration** — the same notes on a different instrument. This is the
  cheapest big change we do not use, and it is exactly what a return needs
- **Harmonic rhythm** — same chords, half or double the rate
- **Progression rotation** — start the loop on a different chord, so the same cycle
  begins off-tonic (this alone makes a verse feel unresolved against a chorus)
- **Key change / modulation** for a section
- **Metric position** — begin the loop on beat 3, or a bar early (elision)
- **Loop length against the grid** — coprime layering (5 or 6 against 8)
- **Time — automation across the section**: filter opening, reverb depth, level swell
- **Silence** — the empty, the drop-out, the stop

## 4. Every section a song can have

A section is a **function**, not a letter. The complete working vocabulary:

**Core**
| Section | Function |
|---|---|
| **Verse** | Narrative; the words change each time; lower energy than the chorus |
| **Pre-chorus** | Lifts and prepares; often a dominant pedal or rising line |
| **Chorus** | "The main pinnacle, the main focus point… usually the dynamic peak"; content stays the same each time |
| **Post-chorus** | Follows the chorus, distinct from it and from the verse (*Smells Like Teen Spirit*) |
| **Bridge / Middle 8** | Contrast in the last third; goes elsewhere tonally — often a key change, which buys "two for one" (a shift in and a shift back) |
| **Refrain** | A short hook tagged to each verse rather than a full section (*Blowin' in the Wind*) |

**Frame**
| Section | Function |
|---|---|
| **Intro** | Three kinds: the **vamp** (the verse minus its melody — most typical), a **distinct riff** heard only there, or something deliberately unrelated |
| **Outro / Coda** | See §5 |

**Interior**
| Section | Function |
|---|---|
| **Instrumental break / Solo** | A voice takes the foreground; the tune rests |
| **Interlude** | Connective, low-information passage between big sections |
| **Breakdown** | Strip to a core element; the hole the payoff falls out of |
| **Build / Riser** | Accumulating tension into an arrival |
| **Drop** | The release the build was for |
| **Vamp** | A short figure repeated freely, often under a solo or as an intro |
| **Tag** | A repeated closing phrase, jazz/gospel — a way *out* |
| **Turnaround** | A short harmonic figure that leads back to the top of the form |
| **Transition** | No melody, unstable harmony, odd phrasing — the "in transit" feeling |
| **False ending** | The song appears to stop and returns |
| **Elision** | One section's last bar *is* the next one's first — the seam is removed |

We currently have: intro, build, drop/low, moment/peak, out. That is five functions out
of twenty, and only one of them (intro) is genuinely characterised.

## 5. Outros — and why intros without them are incoherent

An intro is the answer to "how does this world begin"; an outro is the answer to "how
does it end." Having one without the other means the song has an entrance and then simply
*stops being*. Our "out" is just the lowest-energy block at the end — it is a fade of
activity, not an ending.

The real vocabulary (typical length **8–16 bars**, 2–4 phrases):

| Ending | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Fade out** | Volume recedes while the loop continues — "a sense of continuation or nostalgia" |
| **Cold ending / hard stop** | Stops abruptly on a final chord. Decisive |
| **Coda** | New concluding material that "often **restates or transforms earlier themes**… reinforcing the main idea and leaving a lasting impression" |
| **Tag / vamp-out** | A short phrase repeated to close — jazz and gospel |
| **Reprise** | "Bring back a musical motif from the song's **intro** or verse, but play it **differently** — slower, or on a different instrument" |
| **Ritardando ending** | Slow down into the close; often strip to one voice plus the tune |
| **Button** | A single decisive final hit |

**The reprise is the one that answers the question directly.** If the intro states the
motif incompletely, the outro can state it once more — transformed, on another
instrument, slower — and the song closes the circle it opened. That is what makes an
intro and an outro the *same idea* rather than two unrelated blocks, and it is why having
intros but no outros felt wrong.

## 6. Are our sections expressing the point of the song?

Honestly: **no, not yet.** They express *contrast* and they express *energy*. They do not
express a *point*, because nothing in the engine has a point of view. Specifically:

1. **There is no antagonist.** B is independently generated, not opposed to A. No
   conflict, so nothing to resolve.
2. **Returns are not revalued.** A-after-B currently plays a later loop of A's own cell —
   development, which is good — but it is not *higher, fuller, or resolved*. Almén's
   "revaluation of rank" never happens.
3. **The motif arrives complete.** Every statement is full, so nothing is ever revealed.
4. **Nothing lands.** The progression loops indifferently to the end; there is no final
   arrival on the tonic, no cadence, no closure.
5. **Only one voice is a character.** The others are functions (bass, chords, kit), not
   agents. A duet, a hand-off, an answer that becomes the lead — none of that exists.

---

## What to build, in order

1. **Foreshadow the motif** — the intro and the verse background carry a *reduced* form
   (head only, low register, another instrument). The peak is its first complete
   statement.
2. **Derive B from A by opposition** — inversion, complementary register, displaced
   rhythm, darker mode. Thematic transformation, not independent generation.
3. **Revalue the return** — A after B comes back re-orchestrated, up an octave, or fuller.
   The same music, but ranked differently.
4. **Give the song an ending** — pick from the real vocabulary per genre/seed: reprise the
   intro's motif transformed, a coda, a tag, a cold stop, a ritardando. Not just "quiet
   block at the end."
5. **Land it** — make the last chorus/coda arrive on the tonic, with a real cadence.
6. **Characterise the interior sections** — solo, breakdown, interlude, false ending,
   turnaround are all distinct functions we currently collapse into "lower energy."
