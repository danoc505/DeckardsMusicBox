| Life.2.p1 | in a short while he contracted malign diphtheria | The Greek says "little by little" (κατ’ ὀλίγον) the disease grew on him, not "in a short while"; and its name is κυνάγχη ("dog-choke"), the ancient name for a severe inflammation of the throat. | I.6.9.p4 | the Primal Good and the Primal Beauty have the one dwelling-place | The Greek puts this as another way of speaking: "or he will place the Good and the first beautiful in the same" (ἢ ἐν τῷ αὐτῷ ... θήσεται), "but in any case the beautiful is there": a choice left open, not a conclusion. |
| I.7.1.p2 | it can but remain unmoved | The Greek says the Good is "at rest" (ἐν ἡσύχῳ), "in quiet": not "unmoved" in Aristotle's sense, though the meaning is close. |
| I.7.1.p3 | try all you will to separate the light from the sun, or the sun from its light, for ever the light is in the sun | The Greek says "even if you wish to cut it off on one side, the light is towards the sun" (πρὸς τὸν ἥλιον): the light still stretches back to its source. "Or the sun from its light" is not in the Greek, and the light is "towards" the sun, not "in" it. |
| I.7.2.p1 | But the Universe outside ; how is it aligned towards the Good ? | The Greek asks "and all the other things, how are they towards it?" (τὰ δ’ ἄλλα πάντα): it does not speak of a universe outside. |
| I.7.2.p1 | are no more than images of the Ideal-Form | The Greek says that what each thing shares in are "images of being and of the one, and the form likewise" (εἴδωλα ὄντος καὶ ἑνός, καὶ τὸ εἶδος ὡσαύτως): images of being and the one, not of the Ideal-Form. |
| I.7.3.p1 | the misfortune is still life, a life of a definite character | The Greek says only that life is evil there too, "since it is not life alone" (ὅτι μὴ ζωὴ μόνον): it is life with something added, the punishment; "a life of a definite character" is MacKenna's. |
| I.7.3.p2 | feel itself at home | The Greek says the soul "will be able to receive both" (ἀμφοτέρων δεκτική), life and death: it can be in either state, not that it feels at home in both. |
| I.8.1.p2 | are nearer to Non-Being | The Greek says the worse are "rather a privation" (στέρησις μᾶλλον), the lack of a form, Aristotle's word; MacKenna's "nearer to Non-Being" says something else. Where the question comes back, he has "utter Lack" for the same word. |
| I.8.2.p2 | And the First Act is the Act of The Good stationary within Itself, and the First Existence is the self-contained Existence of The Good | In the Greek it is intellect that "is the first activity of that one and its first being" (πρώτη ἐνέργεια ἐκείνου καὶ πρώτη οὐσία), while the Good "stays in itself": the Good does not itself act or exist as intellect does. MacKenna gives the first activity and the first existence to the Good. |
| I.8.2.p3 | circles around the Intellectual-Principle | The Greek says the soul "dances" round it (χορεύουσα), as a chorus round an altar: Plotinus' image for the soul's ordered life about what it loves. |
| I.8.3.p1 | of an utterly different order from Authentic-Being | The Greek says non-being here is "only other than being" (ἕτερον μόνον τοῦ ὄντος), Plato's sense of non-being in the Sophist; MacKenna makes it "utterly different". |
| I.8.3.p1 | there is no question here of movement or position with regard to Being | The Greek says this non-being is not like "motion and rest, which are about being" (ὡς κίνησις καὶ στάσις ἡ περὶ τὸ ὄν): two of the Sophist's greatest kinds, which are "not being" only because they are other than it. MacKenna's "position" is rest (στάσις). |
| I.8.3.p2 | that part is all lawless void | The Greek says only "that too is all of these" (καὶ αὐτὸ πάντα ταῦτα): the whole list of what evil is. "Lawless void" is MacKenna's. |
| I.8.3.p3 | become a cause of evil by consecration to this Absolute | The Greek says such a thing is evil "by looking towards it" (βλέπον πρὸς αὐτό), or "makes things such" (ποιητικόν): there is no "consecration". |
| I.8.4.p1 | Soul, on the contrary, since not every Soul is evil, is not an evil Kind | The Greek has two claims: the soul "in itself is not evil" (καθ’ ἑαυτὴν μὲν οὐ κακή), and "nor is every soul evil"; MacKenna leaves out "in itself" and makes the second the reason for a conclusion of his own. |
| I.8.4.p1 | all the states, foreign to the true nature | The Greek calls them "involuntary affections" (ἀκούσια παθήματα): the soul does not choose them. |
| I.8.4.p1 | not by their true value but by the mere test of like and dislike | The Greek says the false opinions make the soul "think evil and good the things it flees and pursues": it mistakes what it avoids and seeks for evil and good. There is no "true value" or "like and dislike". |
| I.8.5.p3 | the Evil which holds men down binds them against their will | The Greek speaks of the evils (ἃ δ’ ἂν ἀνθρώπους κατάσχῃ) "that take hold of men", in the plural; it means the evils, not "the Evil". |
| I.8.6.p1 | with justice and piety in the light of philosophy | The Greek, quoting Plato, says "just and holy with wisdom" (μετὰ φρονήσεως): practical wisdom, not philosophy. |
| I.8.6.p2 | The Good in the Supernal | The Greek says only "that Good" (ἐκείνῳ τῷ ἀγαθῷ). |
| I.8.6.p2 | the good thing by which Matter is brought to order | The Greek says virtue is "a good, which makes us master matter" (ὃ κρατεῖν τῆς ὕλης ποιεῖ): mastering, not ordering. |
| I.8.6.p2 | established by practical proof | The Greek says "shown by induction" (τῇ ἐπαγωγῇ δεδειγμένον), by going through the cases one by one: Aristotle's argument in the Categories. |
| I.8.7.p2 | Since you possess only a derivative being | The Greek of the Timaeus says "since you have come into being" (ἐπείπερ ἐγένεσθε); MacKenna's words of introduction, "in which the Father of Gods addresses the Divinities of the lower sphere", are his own explanation. |
| I.8.8.p1 | not in the concrete signification but in the presence or absence of just such forms | The Greek says "fillings, emptyings, and not fillings simply, but fillings with such and such things" (πληρώσεις τοιῶνδε): being full of this food or that. There is nothing about concrete significations or the absence of forms. |
| I.8.8.p3 | the incoming Idea should have been able to conquer the Matter | The Greek says only "but one should master it, someone might say" (ἀλλὰ κρατεῖν ἔδει): it is we, or the reason in us, who should master the body's desires; "the incoming Idea" is MacKenna's. |
| I.8.8.p3 | in times of stress, we are not the same | The Greek speaks of being "full" and "empty" (πλήρεις, κενοί), filled with this or with that: our desires and thoughts change with what we have eaten and drunk, not with stress and peace. |
| I.8.9.p1 | by means of the philosophic habit | The Greek says "by intellect itself and by wisdom" (νῷ αὐτῷ καὶ φρονήσει), and gives the reason MacKenna leaves out: "for it knows itself". |
| I.8.9.p2 | an ugly appearance in Matter | The Greek has "an ugly face" (αἰσχρόν τι πρόσωπον), an example taken from life. |
| I.8.10.p1 | give it Quality and in its very Evil it would almost be a Form | The Greek says matter is evil not by having a quality, "so that perhaps it is not evil as being a form" (ἵνα μὴ ᾖ ἴσως κακὴ εἶδος οὖσα), but as a nature contrary to form; MacKenna's "give it Quality" and "almost" are his reading. |
| I.8.11.p1 | This objection may be answered by applying the principle to the case of Evil in the Soul | In the Greek the objection goes on: if evil is privation, it is in the soul, as its vice, and "nothing outside"; there is no answer until the next paragraph ("But if privation..."). MacKenna makes the objector's conclusion into an answer. |
| I.8.11.p2 | it has much good in it | The Greek says the soul "has something good" (ἔχουσά τι ἀγαθόν), "a trace of intellect": the argument needs only some good, not much. |
| I.8.13.p2 | where, fallen from all our resemblance to the Divine, we lie in gloom and mud | The Greek says "sinking into it, one will have fallen into dark mud" (εἰς βόρβορον σκοτεινόν): "fallen from all our resemblance to the Divine" is MacKenna's gloss on "the place of unlikeness". |
| I.8.13.p2 | And the death of Soul is twofold | The Greek says only "its death": "while still in the body" to be sunk in matter, "and when it has gone out" to lie there. The two times are there, but "twofold" is MacKenna's. |
| I.8.14.p1 | as hasty to compromises | The Greek says the evil soul is "rash in its assents" (προπετῆ εἰς συγκαταθέσεις): quick to agree to impressions, the Stoics' word for judgement, not "compromises". |
| I.8.14.p1 | burned away by every heat | The Greek says such weak things are "easily carried about" (ῥᾳδίαν τὴν φοράν) by winds and the heat of the sun: carried about, not burned away. |
| I.8.14.p3 | for the Soul cannot lift up this foreign thing close by, since the evil of it makes it invisible | The Greek says the source of light "does not bear with" matter (οὐ γὰρ ἀνέχεται αὐτήν), though matter is present, "because matter does not see" it, by its badness: it is matter that cannot see, not matter that is invisible, and nothing is "lifted up". |
| I.8.15.p2 | within that place of its choice | The Greek says the soul must be "with intellect alone, settled in it" (ἐν αὐτῷ ἱδρυμένην): "that place of its choice" is MacKenna's. |
| I.8.15.p3 | not destitute of Images of the Good and Beautiful | The Greek has "images of the beautiful" (εἰδώλοις τοῦ καλοῦ): "the Good" is added. |
| I.9.1.p1 | For wheresoever it go, it will be in some definite condition | The Greek says "it will go out having something" (ἔχουσά τι), something of the body, "if it goes out at all": the reason for the opening command, not a condition it will be in. |
| I.9.1.p2 | movements which it is unlawful to indulge | The Greek says only "but one must do nothing" (δεῖ δὲ μηδὲν πράττειν): nothing is done that would come from these feelings. There is no law in the Greek. |
| I.9.1.p2 | That is not likely in the Sage | The Greek's σπουδαῖος is "the good man", serious about virtue, as elsewhere in the Workshop (see I.4); MacKenna's "Sage" makes him a Stoic figure. |
| II.1.1.p1 | however true an explanation, is utterly inadequate | The Greek says only that this would "perhaps" (ἴσως) be true, but would give no clarity (σαφήνεια): the complaint is that it explains nothing, not that it is "utterly inadequate". |
| II.1.1.p1 | The Will of God is able to cope with the ceaseless flux | In the Greek this is part of what "someone will perhaps claim" (μήποτε ... ἀξιώσει), a view Plotinus puts forward to test; MacKenna states it as fact. |
| II.1.1.p3 | by such communication | The Greek says "even in this way and in this manner" (κἂν οὕτω καὶ τοῦτον τὸν τρόπον); there is no word for "communication". |
| II.1.2.p2 | no need, then, to call on a soul or on any perdurable conjunction | The Greek says he would need nothing from the soul "except" (ἢ) that the two be always together to make up a living being: the lasting union with soul is the one thing still needed, where MacKenna has it not needed either. |
| II.1.3.p2 | co-operates by its upward tendency as earth by its lingering below | The Greek says only that fire is "sharp and quick" because it does not stay down here, as earth is because it does not stay above; there is no word for "co-operates". |
| II.1.3.p3 | occur in some of the superiors from which the celestial fire depends | The Greek says "if it had something else" (εἰ ἄλλου τινὸς ἔχοι), some other element besides fire in the heaven, which might flow away too; there are no "superiors" on which the fire depends. |
| II.1.4.p1 | docile to the purposes of the stars | The Greek says the fire there "suits the nature of the stars" (τῇ τῶν ἄστρων πρόσφορον φύσει); there is nothing about their "purposes". |
| II.1.4.p2 | moving in its marvellous might | The Greek says the soul is "set" or "lies" (κειμένην) next to the best things, with a wonderful power; it is not said to move. |
| II.1.5.p2 | whose region does not in fact extend so low as to the moon | The Greek says the opposite: the things under the heaven are not parts of it, "or else the heaven would not reach (only) as far as the moon" (ἢ οὐ μέχρι σελήνης ὁ οὐρανός). The heaven ends at the moon; what is below it is not the heaven. |
| II.1.5.p3 | bringing with it some effluence from the Divine Reason | The Greek says the higher soul comes when the body is already formed, "helping a little, from reasoning" (μικρὰ ἐκ λογισμοῦ), towards its being; there is no "effluence" and no "Divine Reason", only our own reasoning. |
| II.1.6.p1 | Plato accepts it as indicated by all the appearances | The Greek says Plato judged the view by "the likely" (τῷ εἰκότι), the "likely account" of the Timaeus, not by "all the appearances". The appearances come next, and lead the other way. |
| II.1.6.p1 | derive from them the impression of illumination | The Greek names sight and "the grasp of touch" (τὴν τῆς ἁφῆς ἀντίληψιν): to both senses the stars seem made of fire. MacKenna leaves out touch. |
| II.1.6.p3 | It is still not proven that fire is necessary to the visibility of earth | The Greek is a question to be looked into (ἐπισκεπτέον, "we must look at whether"), and its point is the next sentence: if each element needed the others, nothing would have a being of its own. |
| II.1.6.p5 | there are living beings of fire among the Celestials | The Greek says "among the daemons" (δαιμόνων): MacKenna's "Celestials" makes them gods of the heaven. |
| II.1.7.p3 | extinguished by the currents of air opposed to it | The Greek says the flame is quenched when it "meets more air" (ἐντυχοῦσαν πλείονι ἀέρι); nothing is said of currents opposing it. |
| II.1.7.p3 | constitutes also the still higher heavenly bodies | The Greek says "the rest of the heaven" (τὸν δ’ ἄλλον οὐρανόν), the heaven between the stars, is of this light too, and is not seen; there are no "still higher heavenly bodies". |
| II.1.8.p1 | no match for what it would be opposing in those higher regions | The Greek compares that fire with the fire down here: it is "lesser, and not as strong as the things here" (ἰσχύον τε οὐκ ἴσα τοῖς ἐνθάδε), since flame dies down as it rises (chapter 7). |
| II.1.8.p2 | a Principle not dwelling in the higher regions | The Greek says only "another nature presiding over them" (ἄλλης ἐπιστατούσης φύσεως); where it dwells is MacKenna's gloss. |
| II.2.1.p1 | being self-centred it is not of unlimited extension (and consequently must move ceaselessly to be omnipresent) | A hard place. The Greek says only "or is it in itself, not being continuous?" (ἢ ἔστιν ἐν αὑτῇ οὐ συνεχὴς οὖσα;); MacKenna's "unlimited extension" and his words in brackets are his reading of it. |
| II.2.1.p3 | its straight line would finally fling it outside the universe | The Greek says only that fire "will be scattered" (σκεδασθήσεται) in a straight line; "outside the universe" is MacKenna's (Plotinus says next that there is no place beyond). |
| II.2.1.p3 | itself its own sphere | The Greek says "itself its own place" (αὐτὸ αὑτοῦ τόπος). |
| II.2.1.p4 | a living thing whose nature binds it within a body | The Greek says "a body that is alive and in its natural state" (ζῶντι καὶ κατὰ φύσιν δὲ ἔχοντι σώματι); nothing binds a living thing within a body. |
| II.2.1.p4 | the custom the All-Soul has established | The Greek says nature is "what has been ordered by the whole soul" (τὸ ὑπὸ ψυχῆς τῆς πάσης ταχθέν): an ordering, not a custom. |
| II.2.1.p4 | dwelt wholly and solely in the realm in which every member is at rest | The Greek "as being only there where each thing stays" (ὡς ἐκεῖ οὔσης μόνον, οὗ ἕκαστον μένει) means a place in the heaven, not the intelligible realm; MacKenna reads "there" (ἐκεῖ) as Plotinus' "There", the world above. |
| II.2.2.p1 | (which it desires to possess alone) | A hard place: the Greek says only "because it does not have it alone" (ὅτι μὴ μόνον ἔχει), which may mean that it has other things besides the soul, or that it does not have the soul in one place only. MacKenna's words in brackets are his. |
| II.2.2.p3 | what in us is of this circling nature (the soul) | The Greek says "what is spherical in us" (τὸ σφαιροειδὲς ἡμῶν), which is earthy: the head, which the Timaeus (44d) makes spherical in imitation of the All. It is a part of the body, not the soul. |
| II.2.2.p3 | the Spirit which dwells with the Soul does thus circle about the divinity | The Greek πνεῦμα here is the "breath", the fine body around the soul that the later Platonists call its vehicle; MacKenna's capital "Spirit" can be read as something higher. |
| II.2.2.p3 | to each a revolution around their common centre | The Greek gives each star a motion "around their own centre" (τὴν περὶ τὸ κέντρον αὐτῶν), each turning in its own place, as in the Timaeus (40a-b); not around a common centre. |
| II.2.3.p1 | another phase possesses sensation, while yet another includes the Reason | A hard place: MacKenna counts three powers. The Greek "the one whose nature is to perceive and the one that receives the reason that forms opinions" (ἡ δὲ αἰσθάνεσθαι πεφυκυῖα καὶ ἡ λόγον δοξαστικὸν δεχομένη) goes with one singular verb and "itself" (ἑαυτήν), and the chapter goes on with two: the lower power woven through the All, and the perceiving power above. |
| II.2.3.p1 | this upward tension | The Greek says "its turning" (ἡ δὲ ἐπιστροφὴ αὐτῆς), Plotinus' word for the soul's turning back to what is above it. |
| II.3.1.p2 | so that they must be supposed to change their plans as they stand at their zeniths or are declining | The Greek says "on the centres" (ἐπὶ κέντρων), the astrologers' four cardinal points of the sky: where a star rises, culminates, sets, and is lowest under the earth. The zenith is only one of them. |
| II.3.1.p3 | so that, after all, they possess no definite nature but vary according to their angles of aspect | The Greek says only "as if they were not themselves" (ὥσπερ οὐχ αὑτῶν ὄντας), but one kind when they see and another when they do not; "no definite nature" and "angles of aspect" are MacKenna's. |
| II.3.2.p1 | emanations merely corporeal cannot differ greatly from star to star | The Greek says the outflow from each is "the same" (τῆς ἀπορροῆς ἑκάστων τῆς αὐτῆς οὔσης), not that it cannot differ greatly. |
| II.3.2.p3 | suppose the stars to have life and mind and to be effective by deliberate purpose | The Greek says only "being ensouled" (ἔμψυχα ὄντα), acting "by choice"; "mind" is MacKenna's. |
| II.3.3.p3 | Like the birds of augury, the living beings of the heavens, having no lot or part with us, may serve incidentally to foreshow the future | The Greek puts it as a condition: their work towards us is incidental "if, as with birds, signifying is incidental" to them (εἰ ὥσπερ ὄρνισι κατὰ συμβεβηκὸς τὸ σημαίνειν). MacKenna makes it a statement. |
| II.3.5.p1 | When the cold planet, we are told, is in opposition to the cold, both become menacing | The Greek says the cold one in opposition "to the hot" (τὸν ψυχρὸν τῷ θερμῷ), which is why Plotinus says they ought rather to blend. |
| II.3.5.p1 | as if the first one could be darkened by night | The Greek says "the other" (τοῦ ἑτέρου), the fiery star that is glad at night; MacKenna makes it the first. |
| II.3.5.p2 | the reason alleged is that the force of Mars is all-sufficient since it contains more fire than it needs | A hard place: the Greek says only "for that one's is enough, being more fiery than towards that one" (αὐταρκεῖ γὰρ τὸ ἐκείνου πυρωδεστέρου ἢ πρὸς ἐκεῖνον ὄντος), which is probably damaged; "than it needs" is MacKenna's reading, and the modern version's. He names Mars where the Greek says "the fiery one". |
| II.3.5.p4 | it is to be the inciter of the animal spirits | The Greek says the gall had to "rouse spirit" (θυμὸν ἐγείρειν), the part of the soul that is angry; "animal spirits" is MacKenna's. |
| II.3.5.p4 | but all the members will be in sympathy with the entire animal frame to which they belong | The Greek says all are in sympathy "through what is without reason in them" (τῷ ἀλόγῳ αὐτῶν): the sympathy of the All works in its irrational life, not in reason. |
| II.3.6.p1 | And who could accept the fancy that their happiness comes from their seeing each other in this or that relative position and not from their own settled nature ? | The Greek says "and that there is no limit (πέρας) for them": no end to their desire. Nothing is said of their settled nature. |
| II.3.6.p2 | such a one, they see, has risen by so many degrees, representing so many of the periods of its upward path | The Greek says that as many degrees as each sign rises in, "so many years" (ἐνιαυτοὺς τοσούτους) of the rising: the astrologers' way of reckoning the length of a life. "Periods" is MacKenna's. |
| II.3.7.p1 | Obviously, unless the particular is included under some general principle of order, there can be no signification. | The Greek says "for there would be no signifying in an ordered way if each thing did not come about" in order (οὐ γὰρ ἂν ἐσημαίνετο τεταγμένως μὴ ἑκάστων γινομένων): the order is in the events, not a general principle above them. |
| II.3.7.p2 | but more markedly so since in this case the parts are not merely members but themselves Alls, members of the loftier Kind | The Greek says only that they are "not only parts but also wholes, and greater" (μὴ μόνον μέρη, ἀλλὰ καὶ ὅλα καὶ μείζω); "members of the loftier Kind" is MacKenna's. |
| II.3.7.p3 | all is one scheme of differentiation, starting from the Firsts and working itself out in a continuous progression of Kinds | The Greek says only "for something else comes from these too, and next, by nature, another" (ἄλλο τι καὶ ἐκ τούτων καὶ ἐφεξῆς κατὰ φύσιν ἄλλο); the Firsts and the Kinds are MacKenna's. |
| II.3.8.p1 | alike in its direct course (its tendence towards the divine ?) and in its divagation (its activity towards the lower ?) | The Greek says "whether it goes straight or is thrown into disorder" (κἂν εὐθυποροῖ καὶ ταράττοιτο αὖ): the soul's acting rightly or wrongly, which justice then follows. MacKenna's guesses in brackets are his. |
| II.3.8.p1 | it is the cause of all by its possession of the Thought of the First Principle | The Greek says soul makes all things "having the standing of a principle" (ἀρχῆς ἔχουσα λόγον): it is itself a principle. Nothing is said of the First Principle or its thought. |
| II.3.8.p2 | For our part, nature keeps us upon the work of the Soul | The Greek says "we do the works of soul according to nature" (ψυχῆς ἔργα κατὰ φύσιν ποιοῦμεν): nature is how we do them, not what keeps us on them. |
| II.3.9.p1 | To Plato the Spindle represents the co-operation of the moving and the stable elements of the kosmic circuit | The Greek says the spindle is "what wanders and what does not wander" (τό τε πλανώμενον καὶ τὸ ἀπλανὲς) in the revolution: the planets and the fixed stars. "Co-operation" is MacKenna's. |
| II.3.9.p2 | The " We " is the actual resultant of a Being whose nature includes, with certain sensibilities, the power of governing them. | The Greek says "what we truly are" (ὅπερ ἐσμὲν κατ’ ἀλήθειαν ἡμεῖς), to whom nature gave the power to master the passions. For MacKenna the "We" is a "resultant"; for Plotinus it is the true self, not the composite. |
| II.3.9.p2 | virtue the unconquerable | The Greek ἀδέσποτον is "with no master over it", Plato's word: the herald of the myth of Er says that "virtue has no master", and each will have more or less of it as he honours it (Republic X, 617e). |
| II.3.9.p3 | no longer profiting, merely, by the significance of the sidereal system | The Greek says that for one who lives in destiny the stars "do not only signify" (οὐ μόνον σημαίνει) for him: they act on him as well. Nothing is said of profiting. |
| II.3.9.p4 | body is the agent while, at the same time, it becomes the vehicle through which is transmitted something of the star's will | The Greek says the body gives, a part to a part, "while the star's choice and its soul that truly is look to the best" (προαιρέσεως τοῦ ἄστρου καὶ ψυχῆς τῆς ὄντως αὐτοῦ πρὸς τὸ ἄριστον βλεπούσης). Nothing of the star's will is passed on through the body: the star's will is turned the other way. |
| II.3.9.p5 | But (with every allowance to the lower forces) all follows either upon that Highest or rather upon the Beings about It — we may think of the Divine as a fire | A hard place: the Greek says "the other things follow on it, or rather not on it, but on those around it" (παρακολουθεῖ δ’ αὐτῷ τὰ ἄλλα, μᾶλλον δ’ οὐκ αὐτῷ, ἀλλὰ τοῖς περὶ αὐτόν). "It" is masculine, most likely the star, not "that Highest"; and the fire is only an image of how heat spreads, not "the Divine". |
| II.3.9.p5 | " is a mighty spirit and its ways are subdivine." | The Greek is δαίμων μέγας, "a great daemon", and the passions in it are "daemonic" (δαιμόνια): Plato's words for Love in the Symposium (202d-e), a great daemon between gods and mortals. "Spirit" and "subdivine" hide the quotation. |
| II.3.10.p1 | for it could never touch body except under stress of a powerful inner impulse | The Greek says it would not come into a body "unless it had a great capacity to be affected" (μὴ μέγα τι παθητικὸν ἔχουσα): something passive in it, not an impulse. |
| II.3.11.p1 | and ambition ... in love . . . | MacKenna's dots mark a gap; Volkmann's text has none here: "what lies in the love of honour and is concerned with beauty" (τὸ τιμῆς ἐν ἔρωτι ὂν καὶ περὶ τὸ καλὸν ἔχον) makes a desire for what only seems beautiful. |

| III.2.7.p2 | it is not abstract Spirit | The Greek says souls are "not souls only, but already living things" (οὐ ψυχαὶ μόνον, ἀλλὰ ζῷα ἤδη): souls joined to bodies. "Abstract Spirit" is MacKenna's. |
| III.2.7.p2 | to presuppose it | The Greek says "to bring it into being" (ὑφιστάναι), to make it subsist. |
| III.2.7.p3 | And even granting that those less noble members are not in themselves admirable | The Greek says it is neither holy nor pious "to grant that these things are not well (ἐνδόντας τὸ μὴ καλῶς ταῦτα ἔχειν) and to find fault with the work": the granting is the impiety. MacKenna has Plotinus grant it. |
| III.2.8.p1 | they have not trained themselves in self-defence | The Greek says they "are not good, and have not prepared themselves not to suffer" (οὐκ εἰσὶν ἀγαθοὶ οὐδὲ παρεσκεύασαν αὑτοὺς μὴ παθεῖν): what they lack is goodness, not training in self-defence. |
| III.2.8.p2 | in the disaster to their human quality | The Greek says their first penalty is to be "wolves and ill-starred men" (λύκοις εἶναι καὶ κακοδαίμοσιν ἀνθρώποις): men with an evil daemon, unhappy, whose wickedness is itself their misfortune. |
| III.2.8.p3 | This at once brings us outside the gymnasium with its fun for boys | The Greek says "such things are not a wrestling school, for there it is play" (ἀλλ’ οὐ παλαίστρα τὰ τοιαῦτα: παιδιὰ γὰρ ἐκεῖ): life is not the wrestling school, but war. |
| III.2.8.p3 | Not even a God would have the right to deal a blow for the unwarlike | The Greek says "it was not for a god to fight for the unwarlike himself" (οὐ θεὸν ἔδει ὑπὲρ τῶν ἀπολέμων αὐτὸν μάχεσθαι): what is fitting, not what is a god's right. |
| III.2.8.p4 | Yet death would be better for us | The Greek says death is better "for them" (αὐτοῖς), those who will not do what the gods command; MacKenna takes it to us all. |
| III.2.8.p4 | the triumph of weaklings would not be just | The Greek says only "this is just, and that is not" (τοῦτο γὰρ δίκαιον, καὶ οὐκ ἐκεῖνο): it is just that the cowardly be ruled, and the other way would not be; "the triumph of weaklings" is MacKenna's reading of "the other". |
| III.2.9.p1 | It would not be just, because Providence cannot be a something reducing us to nothingness | The Greek begins "For providence must not be such that we are nothing" (οὐ γὰρ δὴ οὕτως τὴν πρόνοιαν εἶναι δεῖ, ὥστε μηδὲν ἡμᾶς εἶναι); "It would not be just" is MacKenna's link to the last line of chapter 8. |
| III.2.9.p1 | is to annihilate the Universe | The Greek says that providence, if it were all, "would not exist either: for of what would it still be?" (οὐδ’ ἂν εἴη: τίνος γὰρ ἂν ἔτι εἴη;): providence itself would vanish, having nothing to provide for. |
| III.2.9.p2 | their prayers should bring others to sacrifice themselves for their sakes | The Greek says it is not a lawful prayer for the bad "to ask that others be their saviours, having thrown themselves away" (ἑαυτοὺς προεμένους): it is the bad who have given themselves up, and nothing is said of others sacrificing themselves. |
| III.2.9.p2 | nor do they take any steps to improve themselves | The Greek says they never took care that good rulers should come to be "so that they themselves might be cared for" (ὅπως αὐτοὶ εἶεν ἐπιμελούμενοι); nothing is said of improving themselves. |
| III.2.9.p3 | to increase the dominance of moral value | The Greek says "making virtue the stronger" (ἐπικρατεστέραν ἀρετὴν ποιοῦν). |
| III.2.9.p3 | wisdom, intelligence, executive power and right doing | The Greek has "wisdom, intellect, craft (τέχνης) and justice (δικαιοσύνης)": craft, not executive power, and justice, not right doing. |
| III.2.10.p1 | Men are no doubt involuntary sinners in the sense that they do not actually desire to sin | The Greek says they are unwilling "because error is unwilling" (ὅτι ἁμαρτία ἀκούσιον), Socrates' saying that no one errs willingly; nothing is said of desire. |
| III.2.10.p2 | exists only in the sense of a universal relationship | The Greek says the necessity is "not from outside, but in that it holds altogether" (ἀλλ’ ὅτι πάντως): the deed, once done by the doer, is done of necessity; nothing is said of a relationship. |
| III.2.10.p2 | Now Men are Principles, or, at least, they are moved | The Greek says "men too are principles" (ἀρχαὶ δὲ καὶ ἄνθρωποι), without hedging; "at any rate" (γοῦν) gives the proof, that they are moved by their own nature. |
| III.2.11.p1 | it cannot desire all to be good | The Greek says reason does "not wish (οὐ βουλόμενος) all things to be good": it is reason's choice, not something it cannot do. |
| III.2.11.p1 | celestial spirits, the intermediate order | The Greek says "daemons, a second nature" (δαίμονας, δευτέραν φύσιν). |
| III.2.12.p1 | retaining, that is, the hostility to partition | The Greek says reason is "unlike in its parts" (ἀνόμοιος τοῖς μέρεσιν), made of different reasons; MacKenna makes it averse to being divided, the opposite of the point. |
| III.2.13.p1 | Those that have money will be made poor | The Greek says reason makes them poor "if they used wealth badly" (εἰ κακῶς ἐχρήσαντο πλούτῳ), not all who have money. |
| III.2.13.p1 | a man that wrongs a woman will become a woman, to be wronged | The Greek says "raped" (βιασάμενος ... ἵνα βιασθῇ): violence, not a wrong in general. |
| III.2.13.p3 | produce as one thing the desirable and the just | The Greek says "the beautiful (τὸ καλόν) and the just": the beautiful, not the desirable. |
| III.2.14.p1 | displays the Divine Intelligence to a degree in which no arranging by reason could express it | The Greek says the natures of single things come to be "in a more intellectual way than by the ordering of reasoning" (νοερώτερον ἢ κατὰ λογισμοῦ διάταξιν); "the Divine Intelligence" is MacKenna's. |
| III.2.14.p1 | In that case, things of the Intellectual realm and things of the realm of sense must remain one unbroken identity for ever | The Greek speaks of the eternal things "among the intelligibles and among the things of sense" (ἔν τε νοητοῖς ἔν τε αἰσθητοῖς), the intelligible world and the everlasting heavens, which are always the same; it does not say the two realms would be one. |
| III.2.14.p2 | with stars radiant not only in the ears and on the brow | The Greek says "on the face" (κατὰ τὸ πρόσωπον) and on the breast; the ears are MacKenna's. |
| III.2.14.p2 | wrought in the furnaces of craftsmanship | The Greek says "made by the art of Hephaestus" (τέχνῃ Ἡφαίστου), the smith god. |
| III.2.15.p1 | The Divine Reason is the beginning and the end | The Greek says "reason, then, is the principle, and all things are reason" (ἀρχὴ οὖν λόγος καὶ πάντα λόγος); "divine" and "the end" are MacKenna's. |
| III.2.15.p2 | the necessity of this bandit war | The Greek says "the undeclared war" (τοῦ πολέμου τοῦ ἀκηρύκτου), a war without heralds, which no truce ends; nothing is said of bandits. |
| III.2.15.p4 | the other kind of man is himself a futility | The Greek says "the other man is a toy" (ὁ δ’ ἄλλος ἄνθρωπος παίγνιον), Plato's plaything of the gods; "futility" loses the image that the rest of the chapter plays on. |
| III.2.15.p4 | comes to look on life with their eyes | The Greek says that a serious man who plays along and suffers such things "has fallen into a children's game, having put off the mask he wore" (τὸ περὶ αὑτὸν ἀποθέμενος πρόσωπον); "to look on life with their eyes" is MacKenna's. |
| III.2.16.p2 | Life, then, aims at pattern as does the pantomimic dancer with his set movements | The Greek says "its activity is artful (τεχνική), as the dancer moving would be": the dancer's art moves him, not a pattern set before him; and nothing is said of mime or of symbolising life. |
| III.2.16.p3 | (By an imperfect communication) it sets up a conflict of part against part | The words in brackets are MacKenna's: the Greek says reason "set the parts against one another and made them in need" (ἀντιθεὶς δὲ ἀλλήλοις τὰ μέρη καὶ ποιήσας ἐνδεᾶ). |
| III.2.17.p1 | the partial thing (a human being, for example) | The bracket is MacKenna's: the Greek speaks of the longing of any part for the whole (ἡ ἔφεσις τοῦ μέρους πρὸς τὸ ὅλον). |
| III.2.17.p2 | and the Reason-Principle does not excuse them | The Greek says reason "makes us not pardon such things" (ποιεῖ δὲ ὁ λόγος μηδὲ συγγνώμονας ἐπὶ τοῖς τοιούτοις εἶναι): what reason makes is our refusal to pardon. |
| III.2.17.p4 | accepting from the author its entire role | The Greek says the soul takes "all the rest apart from itself and its own deeds" (τὰ ἄλλα πάντα χωρὶς ἑαυτῆς καὶ τῶν ἔργων αὑτῆς): the poet gives everything except the soul and what it does; MacKenna makes the whole role given and laid over its character. |
| III.2.17.p4 | robes of state or rags | The Greek has "saffron robes" (τοὺς κροκωτούς), the yellow gowns of women's and Dionysus' parts on the stage, and rags. |
| III.2.17.p5 | All is just and good in the Universe | The Greek says that in the whole there is "the fitting and the beautiful" (τὸ πρέπον καὶ τὸ καλόν); "just" is MacKenna's. |
| III.2.17.p6 | and the corresponding moral type is often serviceable | The Greek, in words set off as a saying, says "there is often need of such a man too" (δεῖ δὲ καὶ ἀνθρώπου τοιούτου πολλάκις): of the man himself, not of a moral type. |
| III.2.18.p1 | its expression may take any one of three main forms | The Greek says only that we must bear in mind "the second and the third" (τὰ δεύτερα καὶ τὰ τρίτα), and that soul does not always act with the same parts; the "three main forms" are MacKenna's. |
| III.2.18.p2 | We may perhaps think of actors having the right to add something to the poet's words | The Greek doubts whether there is any need "to bring in actors who will say something other than the poet's words" (μὴ γὰρ οὐδὲν δεῖ ἐπεισάγειν τοιούτους ὑποκριτάς): the thought is put in question, not granted as a right. |
| III.2.18.p3 | when the All is itself a Soul | The Greek says "the All belonging to a soul" (τοῦ παντὸς ψυχῆς τινος ὄντος): the All is a soul's, not itself a soul. |
| III.3.1.p1 | does not engender them but encompasses them | The Greek says the whole reason does not beget them "but is the whole with them" (ἀλλ’ ὁ πᾶς ἐστι μετὰ τούτων): it is a whole of which they are parts, not a container round them; "this Universal Idea" is MacKenna's. |
| III.3.1.p1 | its parts (i.e. events good and evil) are expressions of these Soul-parts | The Greek says the reasons are the activity of a whole soul, "and their parts of its parts" (τῶν δὲ μερῶν τὰ μέρη); the gloss "events good and evil" is MacKenna's. |
| III.3.1.p1 | show malice and angry envy | The Greek says the horses "contend for victory" (φιλονεικῶσι) and "rage with jealousy" (ζήλῳ θυμῶνται): rivalry, not malice. |
| III.3.2.p1 | The Leading-Principle holds all the threads while the minor agents, the individuals, serve according to their own capacities | The Greek says the leading principle "weaves all together" (συμπλέκει) while "the things on either side" (τῶν ἐφ̓ ἑκάτερα) come together by nature; "the minor agents, the individuals" and "their own capacities" are MacKenna's. |
| III.3.2.p1 | so as to make it probable that the final event may be success | The Greek says "so that what results from them may have room to be placed well" (ἔχῃ χώραν τοῦ τεθῆναι εὖ): the plan leaves a place for every outcome; it does not make success probable. |
| III.3.3.p1 | Your personality does not come from outside into the universal scheme | The Greek says your part is not "an episode" (ἐπεισόδιον) in the All, an added scene, "but you have been counted in, such as you are" (ἠρίθμησαι ὁ τοιόσδε). |
| III.3.3.p2 | Reason acquits plant and animal and, their maker | The Greek asks why "we reasonably blame" (εὐλόγως αἰτιώμεθα) neither the things nor their maker; MacKenna makes reason itself the judge. |
| III.3.3.p2 | universal equality there cannot be | The Greek says "it was not right that all things should be equal" (οὐ γὰρ πάντα ἴσα ἔδει): a thing of what ought to be, not only of what can be. |
| III.3.3.p3 | the Reason-Principle of this Universe follows upon a phase of the Soul | The Greek says this reason "follows on another soul" (ἀκόλουθος ... ψυχῇ ἄλλῃ), a soul other than the reason itself, not "a phase" of one. |
| III.3.3.p4 | In sum nothing can secure to a thing of process the quality of the prior order | The Greek says the reverse inference fails: "if what has come to be is of this kind, it does not follow that what is before it is of this kind too" (οὐ τοίνυν, εἰ τοιοῦτον τὸ γενόμενον, καὶ τὸ πρὸ αὐτοῦ τοιοῦτον). It defends the cause from the faults of the effect. |
| III.3.3.p4 | the debt is the heavier | The Greek says "we must welcome it all the more" (ἔτι μᾶλλον ἀποδεκτέον): gratitude, not a debt. |
| III.3.4.p1 | subject to reproach and punishment than the mere animals | The Greek says only that "there would be no cause for censure" (ἀπῆν ἂν αἰτία ἡ κατὰ τὴν ἐπιτίμησιν); "punishment" is MacKenna's. |
| III.3.4.p1 | cannot be dependent upon the World of Sense | The Greek says "those are not cut off from these" (οὐ γὰρ ἀπήρτηται ἐκεῖνα τούτων): the higher things are not detached from the lower, which is why the free principle is within providence. MacKenna turns it into a question of which depends on which. |
| III.3.4.p2 | So this Principle is not the only effective force in all men | The Greek asks two questions, "then is it not the only one in all?" and "but why not the only one?", and answers that "in some it is the only one" (ἐφ̓ ὧν δὲ μόνη); MacKenna's "There is no reason why it should not be" answers a question the Greek does not put. |
| III.3.4.p3 | drive him down the troubled paths | The Greek says "as if to throw him into muddy water" (οἷον εἰς θολερὸν ἐμβάλλειν): a body whose mixture clouds the soul, like silt stirred up in water. |
| III.3.4.p4 | the Soul which later will shine out again is for the present at a feebler power | The Greek says the reason has become dim, "as when a soul has become weaker" (οἷον ψυχῆς ἀσθενεστέρας γενομένης), and will "shine out" (ἐκλάμψει) later; it is a comparison, not a statement about the soul now. |
| III.3.4.p5 | how does the higher fall to it | The Greek asks "how did it go wrong" (πῶς ἐσφάλη), of the worse itself; nothing is said of the higher falling to it. |
| III.3.4.p5 | it is not that the greater and prior suffers any diminution of its own nature | The Greek says "it did not lessen something that was" (οὐκ ὄντος ἠλάττωσεν): the less was not made less from something greater, but was less from the start; "the greater and prior" is MacKenna's. |
| III.3.5.p1 | the personal voice, life and history of the living form | The Greek says "one voice of the animal, as it were, and one life and way of living" (μία τοῦ ζῴου οἷον φωνὴ καὶ ζωὴ καὶ βίος); the stress is on "one", and "personal" is MacKenna's. |
| III.3.5.p2 | Divine Mind and unmingled Soul | The Greek says "intellect and pure soul" (νοῦς γὰρ καὶ ψυχὴ καθαρά); "Divine Mind" is MacKenna's. |
| III.3.5.p2 | the Reason-Principle of providence is god-serving | The Greek says it is "dear to god" (θεοφιλής), loved by god, not serving god. |
| III.3.5.p3 | In the total, then, the right rules | The Greek says "virtue rules everywhere" (πανταχοῦ ἀρετὴν κρατεῖν), and what went wrong is changed and set right. |
| III.3.5.p4 | consults the desire of the agent alone | The Greek says what follows is strung on "according to the will of those who acted" (κατὰ τὴν τῶν πραξάντων), the same word as providence's will; "alone" is MacKenna's. |
| III.3.5.p5 | two handsome people of loose character and two living honourably | The Greek says "a licentious man meeting a licentious, a handsome a handsome" (ἀκόλαστος ἀκολάστῳ, καλὸς καλῷ), and then "the self-controlled handsome man" (ὁ δὲ σώφρων καλός) with one like him: the virtue named is self-control. |
| III.3.6.p1 | Clearly the reason is that all contraries coalesce | The Greek says all contraries are "woven together" (συμπεπλέχθαι), the weaving of III.3.2 and III.3.4; they do not coalesce, but stay contraries in one web. |
| III.3.6.p1 | Take, for example, Shape and (its contrary) Matter | The Greek has "form and matter" (τὴν μορφὴν καὶ τὴν ὕλην) as an example of things woven together; "its contrary" is MacKenna's. |
| III.3.6.p3 | the scriptures of Nature | The Greek has "a reading of natural letters" (ἀνάγνωσις φυσικῶν γραμμάτων): reading, not scripture. |
| III.3.6.p3 | Here conspires with There and There with Here | The Greek says "these things move together with those, and those with these" (συμφέρεται γὰρ καὶ ταῦτα ἐκείνοις κἀκεῖνα τούτοις): the signs and the things signified, here below and in the heaven; the intelligible "There" is not meant. |
| III.3.6.p4 | Universal interdependence, there could not be, but universal resemblance there must | The Greek says the opposite of the first half: "it was not right that all things should be cut off from one another" (οὐ γὰρ ἔδει ἀπηρτῆσθαι ἀλλήλων τὰ πάντα), but they must be made like one another somehow. |
| III.3.6.p4 | Correspondences maintain the Universe | The Greek says "proportion holds all things together" (συνέχει τὰ πάντα ἀναλογία), the proportion of the Timaeus. |
| III.3.6.p4 | virtue with right action and vice with unrighteousness | The Greek has "as virtue to justice, so vice to injustice" (ὡς ἀρετὴ πρὸς δικαιοσύνην καὶ κακία πρὸς ἀδικίαν): justice, the virtue, not "right action". |
| III.3.7.p1 | We cannot complain about the lower in the higher | The Greek says "we must not blame the worse in the better" (οὐκ αἰτιατέον τὸ χεῖρον ἐν τῷ βελτίονι), the worse that is in the better whole; MacKenna's "complain" softens a charge of blame. |
| III.3.7.p2 | each single outgrowth bearing its trace of the common source | The Greek says each bears "an image" (εἴδωλον) of it, the word for the image of a pattern, not a trace. |
| III.3.7.p3 | operation is to be traced only from point to next point | The Greek says that one "would think" (ὡς ... οἴεσθαι) the affection came only from the neighbour: an appearance, which the next sentence corrects. |
| III.4.1.p1 | in our view, the Soul generates by its motion | The Greek says soul "was said" (ἐλέγετο) to make while moving: a reference to what has been shown before, not a view put forward here. |
| III.4.1.p1 | but is not the dominant since it is not the whole man | The Greek says nature "does not rule, being a part" (οὐ κρατεῖ δὲ μέρος οὖσα); "the whole man", and the Intellectual Principle in MacKenna's brackets, are his. |
| III.4.1.p2 | Let it be raised to its highest degree and it becomes body | The Greek says "being completed (τελειούμενον) it becomes body": brought to its end, given a shape. Nothing is "raised"; the indefinite is made definite. |
| III.4.2.p1 | forms varying with the variety of place | The Greek, quoting the Phaedrus, says "at different times" (ἄλλοτε) in different forms: the soul changes over time, from life to life, not from place to place. |
| III.4.2.p2 | the lower element also has a footing | The Greek says the worse parts "have a place of their own" (χώραν τινὰ ἔχοντα); MacKenna's sentence makes them a footing for the better, where the Greek says only that neither rules always. |
| III.4.2.p3 | futile and flighty visionaries ever soaring skyward | The Greek says "students of the things on high without wisdom" (μετεωρολόγους δὲ ἄνευ φρονήσεως), those who study the heavens, as in the Timaeus; "futile" and "visionaries" are MacKenna's. |
| III.4.2.p3 | observance of civic and secular virtue | The Greek has only "civic virtue" (τὴν πολιτικὴν ἀρετήν); "secular" is MacKenna's. |
| III.4.3.p1 | What, then, is the spirit | The Greek has δαίμων, "daemon", Plato's word in the passages Plotinus is reading (Phaedo 107d, Republic X 617e, 620d-e, Timaeus 90a); MacKenna's "spirit" is not the word, and it hides that the daemon here is a power of our own soul. |
| III.4.3.p1 | the dominant is the spirit which takes possession of the human being at birth | The Greek says the daemon "that was allotted to him while he lived" (ὅσπερ ζῶντα εἰλήχει), the Phaedo's words; nothing is said of taking possession at birth. |
| III.4.3.p2 | the spirit of the previous life pays the penalty | The Greek says only "he has his punishment" (ἔχει ἐκεῖνος τὴν δίκην): the man whose worse character weighs him down. It is not the daemon that is punished. |
| III.4.3.p3 | by what is the highest, to the Divine Intellect | The Greek says we are joined "by what is above to the intelligible" (τοῖς δὲ ἄνω τῷ νοητῷ), the intelligible world, not "the Divine Intellect". |
| III.4.4.p1 | simply lying unmoved before it | The Greek says the world "lies in safety" (ἐν ἀσφαλεῖ τοῦ κόσμου κειμένου), which is why it gives its soul no cares. |
| III.4.4.p2 | Sensibility is present as the quality of growth is, unrecognised | The Greek says "the growing part is present not being present, and the perceiving part likewise" (πάρεστιν ... οὐ παρὸν): present without being there, not present unrecognised. |
| III.4.5.p1 | merely an allegorical statement of the Soul's tendency and temperament | The Greek says the choice "hints in riddles" (αἰνίττεται) at the soul's "deliberate choice" (προαίρεσις) and disposition: Plato's myth is read for a truth, and the truth is a choice, which "tendency and temperament" lose. |
| III.4.5.p2 | the sample lives are exhibited with the casual circumstances attending them and that the choice is made upon vision | The Greek has "the patterns of lives" (τὰ τῶν βίων παραδείγματα), the Republic's words, and says they choose "from what is there" (ἐκ τῶν παρόντων) according to their characters; "sample" and "made upon vision" are MacKenna's. |
| III.4.5.p3 | Man cannot cease to be characteristically Man | The Greek says "it cannot become other than what it is" (οὐ γὰρ δύναται ἄλλο γενέσθαι ἢ ᾗ ἔστιν): the part that acts cannot be above itself; man is not named. |
| III.4.6.p1 | It does not suffice to perfect virtue to have only this Spirit | The Greek asks whether he would not be good "when he has his daemon working with him" (συνεργοῦντα ἑαυτῷ τὸν δαίμονα ἔχων), and answers that intellect is active in him; "perfect virtue", "only", and "equivalent in all men" are MacKenna's. |
| III.4.6.p1 | An instinct which the Sage finally rectifies in every respect | The Greek asks "does he then succeed in everything?" (πάντως οὖν κατορθοῖ): of the good man's acts, not of an instinct rectified. |
| III.4.6.p3 | this star or the next highest power will stand to them as God or more exactly as. tutelary spirit | The Greek says they will have "such a god, and daemon" (τοιούτῳ θεῷ καὶ δαίμονί γε), "either this very one or the one above this power": the god or daemon is a power of the soul, not the star itself. |
| III.4.6.p4 | its unity can always be reconstructed | The Greek says "the same is whole in all, and again one" (τὸ γὰρ αὐτὸ ἐν πᾶσιν ὅλον καὶ πάλιν ἕν): it is never broken, so nothing has to be reconstructed. |
| III.4.6.p5 | this (personal answer to incident) it is that constitutes destiny | The Greek says "for such is destiny" (τοιοῦτον γὰρ ἡ εἱμαρμένη): destiny is the whole, ship, wind and passenger together, not the personal answer alone. |
| III.5.1.p1 | a Celestial Spirit, a state of mind | The Greek has δαίμων, "daemon", Plato's word for Eros in the Symposium (202d-e), and πάθος, a "passion" of the soul; MacKenna's "Celestial Spirit" is not the word, and he keeps changing it ("Spirit-being", "Celestial", "Supernal") through the treatise. |
| III.5.1.p1 | from a point of view entirely his own | The Greek says Plato "wrote much about love in many places in his works" (πολλὰ πολλαχῇ τῶν ἑαυτοῦ): τῶν ἑαυτοῦ is "of his own writings", not a point of view of his own. |
| III.5.1.p2 | that of the good whose devotion is for beauty itself | The Greek says the one longing is "of the self-controlled" (παρὰ σωφρόνων), made at home with beauty itself; MacKenna's "the good" loses the virtue, to which the chapter comes back: for the self-controlled, the attachment to beauty here is without fault. |
| III.5.1.p3 | a tendency of the Soul towards pure beauty | The Greek speaks of the longing for beauty itself that is in souls "beforehand" (πρότερον), before any beautiful body is seen, together with a "recognition" (ἐπίγνωσις) of it: love begins from what the soul already knows. MacKenna's "tendency" loses the "beforehand". |
| III.5.1.p3 | the consistent total of the good | The Greek συστοιχία is a "column" of the Pythagorean table of opposites, in which the limited stands with the good and the unlimited with the bad; "the consistent total" and "the system of evil" hide the reference. |
| III.5.1.p6 | a last outgrowth, an attenuation of the higher | The Greek calls the beauty here "a product" (ἀποτέλεσμα) and "a plaything" (παίγνιον) of the beauty there; "attenuation" is MacKenna's, and the plaything is lost. |
| III.5.2.p1 | Theologians (Orphic teachers) | The Greek has only "theologians" (θεολόγοι), those who tell of the gods, Hesiod as well as the Orphic poets; MacKenna's bracket narrows them. |
| III.5.2.p1 | minister of beautiful children | The Greek ἔφορον καλῶν παίδων is "overseer of beautiful boys", Plato's words for Eros in the Phaedrus (265c): the boys are the young who are loved, not children who are born. |
| III.5.2.p1 | Poros, Possession, the father | Πόρος is a "way through", a "means", "resource": the son of Metis, Cunning (Symposium 203b), the opposite of the helplessness (ἀπορία) of his partner Poverty; "Possession" makes him a having rather than a finding of ways. |
| III.5.2.p2 | justly called not Celestial Spirit but God | The Greek has δαίμονα, "a daemon": the heavenly Aphrodite is a god and not a daemon, because she is unmixed. MacKenna's "Celestial Spirit" hides that the distinction is Plato's, between gods and daemons (Symposium 202d-203a). |
| III.5.2.p3 | not even in the same order | The Greek says love sees first, "though not in the same way" (οὐ μὴν ὁμοίως ὁρῶν): it fixes the sight in the lover and itself reaps the vision. Nothing is said of an order, nor that desire sees "only through the efficacy of Love". |
| III.5.2.p3 | beauty playing immediately above it | The Greek says the beauty "runs past" it (παραθέουσαν), not that it plays above it. |
| III.5.3.p1 | That Love is a Hypostasis (a " Person ") | The Greek says love is a "reality" (ὑπόστασις) and a "being" (οὐσία); "Person" is MacKenna's. |
| III.5.3.p1 | towards the very Highest | The Greek says the soul looks "towards what was the first being" (πρὸς ἐκεῖνο ... ὃ πρώτη ἦν οὐσία): intellect, the first being, not the One, which is beyond being. |
| III.5.3.p2 | though not, perhaps, so loftily celestial a being as the Soul | The Greek says "even though we called the soul heavenly as much as could be" (εἰ καὶ ὅτι μάλιστα οὐρανίαν τὴν ψυχὴν εἴπομεν): her name does not tie her to the heaven, and so her love is separate too. No comparison is made between the love and the soul. |
| III.5.3.p3 | giving birth, therefore, to the Love concerned with the universal life | The Greek says that this Aphrodite, "not only soul nor simply soul", begot the love in this world, who has to do with marriages; MacKenna's "the Love concerned with the universal life", followed by "no", turns the Greek's statement into a denial. |
| III.5.4.p1 | the Spirit which, as we are told, walks with every being | The Greek has ὁ δαίμων, "the daemon" that goes with each, Plato's daemon allotted to each of us (Phaedo 107d, Republic X 617e; III.4); MacKenna's "Spirit" hides the reference. |
| III.5.4.p1 | the guiding spirit realising its worth and the quality of its Being | The Greek says the soul begets the love "answering to its worth and its being" (εἴς τε ἀξίαν καὶ πρὸς οὐσίαν): the love is as worthy as the soul that begets it. It is the soul that begets, not the guiding spirit that realises. |
| III.5.4.p3 | the Love in the mingling Soul will be a celestial spirit | The Greek says the love of the mixed soul is "a daemon" (δαίμων), as the love of the soul above is "a god": Plato's distinction between gods and daemons, which "celestial spirit" hides. |
| III.5.5.p1 | Poros — Possession — who is son of Metis — Resource | Μῆτις is "cunning", practical wisdom, the mother of Πόρος (Symposium 203b), and it is Poros himself who is "resource"; MacKenna gives the son's name to the mother. |
| III.5.5.p1 | the passage has been misunderstood | The Greek says only that to suppose Plato meant the world "has much against it"; MacKenna's bracket says who misunderstood. |
| III.5.5.p2 | Our only escape would be to make the Kosmos a complex of Supernals | The Greek says "and the world will be a compound made of just this, of daemons" (ἔσται σύστασις αὐτὸ τοῦτο ἐκ δαιμόνων): one more absurdity, not an escape; "Supernals" is MacKenna's word for daemons. |
| III.5.5.p2 | Love, again, is called the Dispenser of beautiful children | The Greek has ἔφορος καλῶν παίδων, "overseer of beautiful boys" (Phaedrus 265c): not a giver of children. |
| III.5.6.p1 | distinguish the Gods from the Celestials | The Greek has δαίμονες, "daemons", throughout; MacKenna's "Celestials", "Spirits" and "Supernals" change from sentence to sentence for one Greek word. |
| III.5.6.p3 | A Celestial is the representative generated by each Soul when it enters the Kosmos | The Greek asks (ἆρά γε ...;) whether daemons are "the trace" (ἴχνος) of a soul that has come into the world: a trace left behind, not a representative sent out, and a question, not an answer. |
| III.5.7.p1 | Penia (Poverty) had participation in the Intellectual before the lower image of that divine Realm had appeared | The Greek says Poverty shared in the intelligible nature, "not of an image of the intelligible" pictured from there: the contrast is between the intelligible and its image, not one of time; "before" is MacKenna's. |
| III.5.7.p2 | Love, therefore, is like a goad | The Greek οἶστρος is a "gadfly", the insect whose sting drives cattle wild, and so a frenzy: Plato's word for the lover driven by desire (Phaedrus 240d); a goad is the herdsman's, used from outside. |
| III.5.7.p4 | but this is mere blank possession when they ignore their own | The Greek says they "have left idle" (ἀργὸν ἀφέντας) the daemon they had simply, and act by another they chose: as in III.4.3, where the daemon above stands over us idle while the one below acts. |
| III.5.7.p4 | merged all the Love-Principles within them in the evil desires | The Greek says they "fettered" (ἐπέδησαν) all the loves in them with evil desires, as they fettered the right reason, "which is inborn" (σύμφυτος), with evil opinions; "merged", "which belongs to our kind" and "from another source" are MacKenna's. |
| III.5.7.p5 | All the natural Loves, all that serve the ends of Nature, are good | The Greek calls the natural loves "beautiful" (καλοί), the word of the whole treatise, not "good"; "that serve the ends of Nature" is MacKenna's for "according to nature". |
| III.5.7.p6 | a given triangular figure is made up of two right angles | The Greek has the old theorem: this triangle has its angles equal to "two right angles" (δύο ὀρθάς), and one sees it in this triangle in so far as it is simply a triangle. A triangle is not "made up of" right angles. |
| III.5.8.p1 | though elsewhere he seems to rank him as one of three | The Greek says that elsewhere Plato calls Zeus "the third, I think" (τρίτον, οἶμαι): a rank, not one of three. |
| III.5.8.p2 | he must be the highest for several reasons | The Greek says Zeus must be ranked "by the better" (κατὰ τὸ κρεῖττον) of what is in him, intellect rather than soul, so "he will be on the side of intellect"; not that he is the highest. |
| III.5.9.p1 | being more diffused, kneaded out as it were | The Greek has ἁπλωθείς, "unfolded", "spread out", as against what is "coiled together" (συνεσπειραμένον) in intellect; "kneaded" is MacKenna's. |
| III.5.9.p1 | breaking into the garden of Zeus | The Greek says the reason-principle "flowed into" (εἰσρυείς) his garden: nothing breaks in. |
| III.5.9.p2 | what the god-nature essentially demands | The Greek asks "what is nectar to the gods but what the divine receives (κομίζεται)?", and answers that what is below intellect receives a reason-principle: what is received, not demanded. |
| III.5.9.p3 | breaking away from it | The Greek says soul "has its reality from intellect" (παρὰ νοῦ ὑποστᾶσα): it comes from intellect, and stays "with intellect"; it does not break away from it. |
| III.5.9.p5 | will leave it still unchanged in Kind | The Greek says that what is turned to itself is "form alone, staying in itself", and that what longs to receive makes the receiver matter for what comes: MacKenna's striving "(with attainment)" that leaves a thing "unchanged in Kind" is not in it. |
| III.5.9.p5 | and at the same time a Celestial, sprung of the Soul | The Greek says love is "this daemon" (δαίμων οὗτος), born from soul: the daemon of the Symposium, whom the treatise set out to explain. |
| III.8.1.p1 | a light handling of our own ideas | The Greek says there is no danger "in playing with what is our own" (ἐν τῷ παίζειν τὰ αὐτῶν): the treatise's own word "play" (παίζειν), which comes back in the next sentences, where Plotinus asks whether we are contemplating "as we play"; "a light handling" loses the link. |
| III.8.1.p2 | The case of Man will be treated later on | The Greek says only "but this later" (ἀλλὰ ταῦτα μὲν ὕστερον): what is put off is the question of action, voluntary and necessary, which the treatise takes up in chapters 4 and 6; man is not named. |
| III.8.1.p2 | held to be devoid of reason and even of conscious representation | The Greek ἀφάνταστον is "without imagination" (φαντασία), the power of forming images, which the Stoics and Aristotle denied to nature; "conscious representation" is MacKenna's. |
| III.8.2.p1 | Nature need possess no outgoing force as against that remaining within | The Greek says nature "does not need some parts that stay and others that are moved" (οὐ γὰρ δὴ δεῖται τῶν μὲν ὡς μενόντων, τῶν δὲ ὡς κινουμένων): it is not split into a resting and a moving part. There is no "outgoing force" in the Greek. |
| III.8.2.p2 | brother, no doubt, to that which gives mere shape, but having life-giving power | The Greek says the living principle is the brother "of the one that made the shape" (ὁ τοῦ ποιήσαντος τὴν μορφὴν ἀδελφός), and "has the same power" (τὴν αὐτὴν δύναμιν ἔχων): it is the brother of nature, the maker, not of the dead principle in the shape, and its power is the same, not a different, "life-giving" one. |
| III.8.3.p1 | above it stands the total Logos with its two distinguishable phases | The Greek says only that every forming principle before the last is contemplation "in one way or another" (ὁ μὲν ἄλλος ἄλλως), one as soul, one in nature and as nature; "the total Logos" is MacKenna's. |
| III.8.3.p1 | Wholly and solely ? | The Greek πάντως μὴν ἐκ θεωρίας is an answer, "wholly from contemplation", not a question; and the next question asks whether it "has contemplated itself" (τεθεωρηκὼς αὑτόν). |
| III.8.3.p2 | The Contemplation springing from the reasoning faculty — that, I mean, of planning its own content, it does not possess | The Greek says nature lacks contemplation "from reasoning" (ἐκ λόγου), "I mean looking into what is in it" (τὸ σκοπεῖσθαι περὶ τῶν ἐν αὑτῇ): examining, not "planning". The point is that to look into a thing is not yet to have it. |
| III.8.4.p1 | The mathematicians from their vision draw their figures | The Greek has "geometers" (γεωμέτραι), who "draw as they contemplate" (θεωροῦντες γράφουσιν): the drawing goes with the contemplating, it is not drawn "from" it, and nature's figures are "the lines of bodies" (αἱ τῶν σωμάτων γραμμαί). |
| III.8.4.p1 | As with my Mother (the All-Soul) and the Beings that begot me | The Greek says nature "has the experience" (πάθος) of its mother and parents: they too came from contemplation. "The All-Soul" is MacKenna's gloss; the Greek names only the mother and parents. |
| III.8.4.p2 | we are applying to sleep a word borrowed from the wake | The Greek says nature's awareness is to ours "as if one were to compare that of sleep with that of one awake" (οἷον εἴ τις τὴν τοῦ ὕπνου τῇ τοῦ ἐγρηγορότος προσεικάσειε): a likeness between two states, sleep and waking, not a word borrowed. |
| III.8.4.p2 | It is a Vision silent but somewhat blurred | The Greek says nature's contemplation is "noiseless" (ἄψοφος) "but dimmer" (ἀμυδροτέρα δέ); "blurred" is MacKenna's. |
| III.8.4.p3 | find in action their trace of vision and of reason | The Greek says such men "make action a shadow of contemplation and of reason" (σκιὰν θεωρίας καὶ λόγου τὴν πρᾶξιν ποιοῦνται); MacKenna's "trace" softens the shadow. |
| III.8.4.p3 | when the result shall prove fairly well equal to the plan | The Greek says they want others to see "when their purpose is, as far as possible, action" (ὅταν ἡ πρόθεσις αὐτοῖς ὡς οἷόν τε πρᾶξις ᾖ): there is no matching of result to plan. |
| III.8.4.p3 | an attenuation or a complement of vision | The Greek's second word is παρακολούθημα, "a consequence" of contemplation, something that follows on it, not "a complement". |
| III.8.5.p1 | a given science, complete in itself, becomes the source and cause of what might be called a minor science in the student | The Greek speaks of "craft" (τέχνη), not science, which makes a small craft "in a child" (ἐν παιδίῳ) who has "an image of it all" (ἴνδαλμα ἁπάντων); MacKenna turns the child into a student who has "some partial knowledge of all its divisions". |
| III.8.5.p1 | dim and helpless by the side of the content of the Soul | The Greek says the sights of the second are "unable to help themselves" (οὐ δυνάμενα βοηθεῖν ἑαυτοῖς), Plato's phrase for the written word, which cannot defend itself (Phaedrus 275e); "by the side of the content of the Soul" is MacKenna's. |
| III.8.5.p2 | for energy runs through the Universe and there is no extremity at which it dwindles out | The Greek says "the activity of life" (ζωῆς ἐνέργεια) "reaches everywhere" (πανταχοῦ φθάνει): it is life's activity, not "energy" in general. |
| III.8.5.p2 | act, however, is not at this stage existent since it depends upon contemplation | The Greek says "action did not yet exist, for it cannot be before contemplation" (οὐ γὰρ οἷόν τε πρὸ θεωρίας): action cannot come before contemplation; MacKenna's "depends upon" turns an order into a dependence. |
| III.8.5.p3 | Vision is not cabined within the bournes of magnitude | The Greek says "it is not bounded by magnitude" (οὐ γὰρ περιγέγραπται μεγέθει) of what is the same "in every soul"; MacKenna's archaism keeps the sense but the subject is contemplation in soul. |
| III.8.5.p3 | they, taking that gift, showed that they were hungry for what made that vision | The Greek says the horses "would plainly long for what he saw" (δῆλον ὅτι ὀρέγοιντο ἂν ὧν εἶδεν), because "they did not receive all of it" (ἔλαβον γὰρ οὐ πᾶν): they long for the seen, not for "what made that vision", and the reason is that the gift was partial. |
| III.8.6.p1 | the more certainly such a principle as the impression made is the deeper | The Greek says only "and the more so, the more it is" (καὶ μᾶλλον, ὅσῳ μᾶλλον): the more fully one receives it, the more silent it is. "The impression made is the deeper" is MacKenna's. |
| III.8.6.p1 | This vision achieved, the acting instinct pauses | The Greek says the soul, having been filled, "is at rest and seeks nothing" (ἡσυχίαν ἄγει καὶ οὐδὲν ζητεῖ); there is no "acting instinct". |
| III.8.6.p2 | as when Ideal-Principles laid up in the mind or Soul remain idle | The Greek says the doubleness "has not yet been made one's own" (οὔπω ᾠκείωται), as when forming principles in the soul "do nothing" (μηδὲν ποιῶσι); and the soul is the soul, not "the mind or Soul". |
| III.8.6.p3 | informed as it now is by the purely intellectual, it sees (in its outgoing act) as a stranger looking upon a strange world | The Greek says the soul, putting its contents before itself, "thinks them through and looks at them as one thing looks at another" (διανοουμένη βλέπει ὡς ἄλλο ὂν ἄλλο): it is the soul's discursive thinking, not anything "purely intellectual", and there is no "strange world". |
| III.8.6.p3 | but its function is to see a (lower) realm which these do not see | The Greek says the soul is a forming principle and a sort of intellect, "but one seeing another" (ἀλλ’ ὁρῶν ἄλλο): it sees something other than itself, not a "lower realm" that intellect does not see. |
| III.8.6.p3 | The Sage, then, is the man made over into a Reason-Principle | The Greek says the good man (σπουδαῖος) "has done his reasoning already" (λελόγισται ἤδη): he has finished reasoning, not been "made over into a Reason-Principle". |
| III.8.7.p1 | manifest to sensation or to true knowledge or to surface-awareness | The Greek has "perception" (αἴσθησις), "knowledge" (γνῶσις) and "belief" (δόξα), opinion; "surface-awareness" is MacKenna's. |
| III.8.7.p1 | brings into being the vision and Idea that we know in it | The Greek says nature "makes the object of contemplation in it and the forming principle, completing another forming principle" (τὸν λόγον ποιεῖ ἄλλον λόγον ἀποτελοῦσα): nature makes a forming principle that completes another, as in chapter 2; there is no "Idea that we know". |
| III.8.7.p2 | in the end the sorriest craftsman is still a maker of forms, ungracefully | The Greek says "the bad craftsman is like one who makes ugly forms" (ἔοικεν αἰσχρὰ εἴδη ποιοῦντι): the stress is on the ugliness of his forms, a swerving from what he contemplates, not that he is "still a maker of forms". |
| III.8.7.p2 | So Love, too, is vision with the pursuit of Ideal-Form | The Greek says "loves" (οἱ ἔρωτες), in the plural, belong "to those who have seen and hasten to a form" (ἰδόντων καὶ πρὸς εἶδος σπευδόντων); "Love" is not personified. |
| III.8.8.p1 | we cannot stop at a principle containing separate parts ; there must always be a yet higher | The Greek says that if seer and seen were two, "there will again be another, which is no longer one and another" (πάλιν γὰρ αὖ ἄλλο ἔσται, ὃ οὐκέτι ἄλλο καὶ ἄλλο): a regress that intellect itself must stop by being one; MacKenna's "a yet higher" points beyond intellect, which is not the argument here. |
| III.8.8.p1 | it will, therefore, be a Seeing that lives | The Greek says "a living contemplation" (θεωρία ζῶσα), "not an object of contemplation like what is in something else"; MacKenna's "The Supreme" for its subject is his: the subject is intellect. |
| III.8.8.p2 | Thus every Life, of the order strictly so called, is an Intellection | The Greek says "every life of this kind" (ἡ τοῦ γένους τούτου), of the kind just described, every life in the scale from growth up, is thought; "strictly so called" is MacKenna's. |
| III.8.8.p3 | The explanation is that in a unity there can be no seeing (a pure unity has no room for vision and an object) ; and in its Contemplation the One is not acting as a Unity | The Greek says intellect is many "because it does not contemplate as one" (ὅτι οὐχ ἓν θεωρεῖ), and that "even when it contemplates the One" (ὅταν τὸ ἓν θεωρῇ), it does not do so as one. The subject is intellect looking at the One, not the One contemplating. |
| III.8.8.p3 | The Highest began as a unity but did not remain as it began | The subject of "began as one" is intellect, as the rest of the sentence shows ("otherwise intellect does not come to be"; "it became second"); MacKenna makes it "the Highest", which would make the One fall into multiplicity. |
| III.8.8.p3 | it grew, as it were, pregnant | The Greek says intellect became many "as though weighed down" (οἷον βεβαρημένος), heavy, not "pregnant". |
| III.8.8.p3 | The Intellectual-Principle on the other hand was never merely the Principle of an inviolable unity | The Greek says "intellect is not the intellect of some one thing" (οὐχ ἑνός τινος νοῦς), but of all; there is no "inviolable unity". |
| III.8.9.p1 | the source of a number such as this must be the authentically One | The Greek says "the beginning of number, and of such a number, is what is truly one" (ἀριθμοῦ δὲ ἀρχὴ καὶ τοῦ τοιούτου τὸ ὄντως ἕν): the One is the beginning of number in general and of this one; MacKenna gives only the second. |
| III.8.9.p1 | the Prior demanded by that duality must be something on the further side of the Intellectual-Principle | The Greek says that what is first "will escape the two" (ἐκφεύξεται τὰ δύο) and so "must be beyond intellect" (ἐπέκεινα δεῖ νοῦ εἶναι): ἐπέκεινα, "beyond", Plato's word for the Good "beyond being" (Republic VI 509b). |
| III.8.9.p2 | nay, nothing, ripe for that participation, can be void of it | The Greek says "there is nowhere that it is not" (οὐκ ἔστιν, ὅπου μὴ ἔστιν) "for those who can share in it" (οἷς ἔστι μετέχειν αὐτοῦ): the point is about place, it is everywhere, as the next sentence says, not about things "void of it". |
| III.8.9.p2 | imagine a voice sounding over a vast waste of land, and not only over the emptiness alone but over human beings | The Greek says a voice "filled a desert" (φωνῆς κατεχούσης ἐρημίαν), the desert and the men in it, and that an ear set anywhere in the desert would take "the whole voice and again not the whole"; "a vast waste of land" is MacKenna's. |
| III.8.9.p3 | essentially a thing facing two ways, it must deliver itself over to those powers within it which tend upward | The Greek says intellect must "give itself up to what is behind it" (ἑαυτὸν ἀφέντα τοῖς εἰς ὄπισθεν αὐτοῦ), "since it faces both ways" (ἀμφίστομον): what is behind it is the One, its source, not "powers within it". |
| III.8.9.p3 | the outflow, that is, of the first moment, not that of the continuous process | The Greek says intellect's unrolling (διέξοδος) is not one "that is still unrolling, but one that has unrolled" (οὐ τῇ διεξιούσῃ, ἀλλὰ τῇ διεξελθούσῃ): its going-through of all things is complete, not "the first moment". |
| III.8.10.p1 | the tides that proceed from it are at one within it before they run their several ways | The Greek says the rivers, "having gone out of it, before each flows its own way, still go together" (τοὺς δὲ ἐξ αὐτῆς προεληλυθότας πρὶν ἄλλον ἄλλῃ ῥεῖν ὁμοῦ συνιόντας ἔτι): they have already come out of the spring; they are together outside it, not "within it". |
| III.8.10.p1 | And this surprises no one : though it is in fact astonishing | The Greek says "and no wonder. Or rather a wonder too" (καὶ θαῦμα οὐδέν. ἢ καὶ θαῦμα): Plotinus corrects himself; it is not that "no one" is surprised. |
| III.8.10.p1 | nothing would come into being if its cause, thus broken up, changed character | The Greek says nothing would come to be "if the beginning did not stay by itself, being different" (μὴ μενούσης τῆς ἀρχῆς ἐφ’ ἑαυτῆς ἑτέρας οὔσης): the beginning must stay apart and other, not "change character". |
| III.8.10.p2 | the All has its One, its Prior but not yet the Absolute One | The Greek says each whole is led back "to the one before it, not the simply one" (εἰς ἓν τὸ πρὸ αὐτοῦ, οὐχ ἁπλῶς ἕν), "until one comes to the simply One": "the All" (τὸ πᾶν) here is "the whole" of each thing, and the One at the end is "simply one", not "Absolute". |
| III.8.10.p2 | seek to grasp it more and more — understanding it by that intuitive thrust alone | The Greek says "understand it more, knowing it by touching it" (συννόει μᾶλλον τῇ προσβολῇ συνείς): the grasp is by direct contact (προσβολή), a touching, as in VI.9. |
| III.8.11.p1 | The Intellectual-Principle is a Seeing, and a Seeing which itself sees | The Greek says intellect is "a kind of sight" (ὄψις τις), "and a sight that sees" (ὄψις ὁρῶσα): MacKenna's "itself sees" reads in self-seeing, which the Greek does not say here. |
| III.8.11.p1 | for The Good is the centre of all else | The Greek says other things "have their activity about the Good and because of the Good" (περὶ τὸ ἀγαθὸν καὶ διὰ τὸ ἀγαθόν); "the centre" is MacKenna's image. |
| III.8.11.p1 | Do not even say that it has Intellection | The Greek says do not add "thinking" (τὸ νοεῖν) either, "so that you may not add something other" (ἵνα καὶ μὴ ἄλλο); MacKenna drops the reason, that thinking would be one thing more added to the Good. |
| III.8.11.p2 | the concentrated strain towards its Form | The Greek says in intellect there is "a converging" (σύννευσις), an inclining together, towards its form; "concentrated strain" is MacKenna's, and it hides that the longing is intellect's leaning towards the form it receives. |
| III.8.11.p3 | this lovely abundance so abundantly endowed | The Greek plays on κόρος, which means both "boy" and "fullness": intellect is "a beautiful boy" (κόρον καλόν) and "a fullness that came from him" (παῤ αὐτοῦ γενόμενον κόρον). The pun alludes to Kronos, child of Ouranos and father of Zeus, whom Plotinus makes intellect; "abundance" keeps one sense and loses the boy. |
| III.8.11.p3 | must overwhelm with awe any that has seen it, and penetrated it, to become a unit of its Being | The Greek says wonder would seize whoever saw it and, "as one should, went into it and became one with it" (αὐτοῦ γενόμενον ἕνα); "a unit of its Being" is MacKenna's. |
| III.9.1.p1 | though, in a unity, admitting that distinction, of the intellectual act (as against passivity) | The Greek says only that the two may be one but "divided by thinking" (διαιρούμενα δὲ τῇ νοήσει); there is nothing of act "as against passivity". |
| III.9.1.p2 | by a common accident, a particular trend of thought has occasioned the discrimination | The Greek says that, "as in many places, he puts it forward one way, and another understands it another way, as three" (ὥσπερ ἐν πολλοῖς προτείνων ἄλλως ὁ δὲ ἄλλως νοεῖ τρία εἶναι): readers take differently what Plato proposes. There is no "common accident". |
| III.9.1.p3 | and thus a Soul — with its particular Souls — may be the separative principle | The Greek says it is "soul that divides into many souls" (ψυχὴν εἶναι τὴν μερίσασαν εἰς πολλὰς ψυχάς): the one soul divides itself into the many; "with its particular Souls" makes the many souls dividers too. |
| III.9.2.p1 | an integral thing in its highest Principle and its last detail | The Greek says each theorem "has the whole potentially" (ἔχει δὲ ἕκαστον δυνάμει τὸ ὅλον), and that "the same is beginning and end" (οὗ τὸ αὐτὸ ἀρχὴ καὶ τέλος); "potentially" is lost. |
| III.9.2.p2 | followed, as next, by the Universe or, at least, by all beneath the sun | The Greek says what comes next is the All, "as what is near, or what is under the sun" (οἷον τὸ πλησίον ἢ τὸ ὑφ̓ ἡλίῳ): an image of nearness, as the world lies under the sun's light; "or, at least" is MacKenna's. |
| III.9.2.p3 | by a sort of inferior orientation, it looks downward | The Greek says the soul, "looking again, as with a second glance" (πάλιν δὲ ἰδοῦσα οἷον δευτέρᾳ προσβολῇ), shaped the image: a second look, not a looking "downward"; and it "gave shape" (ἐμόρφωσε) to the image before going into it, which MacKenna loses. |
| III.9.2.p3 | stripping itself of its own determined form | The Greek says the soul becomes "more indefinite" (ἀοριστοτέρα γινομένη); it is not said to strip itself of a form. |
| III.9.3.p1 | Because, universality demands a previous unity | The Greek says "because there must be one before all things" (ὅτι δεῖ πρὸ πάντων ἓν εἶναι): one before all, not "universality" demanding unity. |
| III.9.3.p3 | In a word, intellection is vested in the activities themselves | The Greek says "it saw these, then, all that are activities" (ταῦτα ἄρα εἶδεν, ὅσα ἐνέργειαι): the thinking saw the activities, of substance and of life; "vested in the activities" is MacKenna's. |
| III.9.3.p3 | and self-intellection conveys the Image of the Intellectual Sphere | The Greek says "the thinking of them carries their image" (ἡ δὲ νόησις ἡ αὐτῶν τὴν εἰκόνα φέρει), the image of what we truly are; "the Intellectual Sphere" is MacKenna's. |
| III.9.3.p4 | it knows itself as deficient since its virtue consists in this knowing | The Greek says what thinks "is lacking" (ἐλλιπές), "because it has its well-being in thinking, not in its existence" (ὅτι ἐν τῷ νοεῖν ἔχει τὸ εὖ, οὐκ ἐν τῇ ὑποστάσει): it is lacking, not that it knows itself to be, and its "well-being" (τὸ εὖ) is not "virtue". |
| III.9.3.p6 | neither Intellection in general nor even the Intellection of The Good | The Greek says "nor, then, every thinking, but the thinking of the Good" (οὔκουν οὐδὲ πᾶσα, ἀλλ’ ἡ τοῦ ἀγαθοῦ): not all thinking is majestic, only the thinking of the Good is; MacKenna reverses it, and denies majesty to the thinking of the Good too. |
| III.9.3.p6 | But intellection does not mean self-ownership ; it means turning the gaze towards the Primal | The Greek says what thinks "is said to think not by having itself, but by looking to the first" (τῷ πρὸς τὸ πρῶτον βλέπειν): the subject is now what thinks, intellect, which thinks by looking to the first. |
| III.9.3.p7 | it observes itself in order, by that activity, to become master of its Being | The Greek says what is aware of itself is so "that by this activity it may be with itself" (ἵνα τῇ ἐνεργείᾳ ταύτῃ συνῇ αὑτῷ): to be together with itself, not to "become master of its Being". |
| III.9.3.p7 | this can mean only that ignorance inheres in it | The Greek says it "must have happened to be unlearned by itself" (ἀκαταμάθητον τετυχηκέναι αὑτῷ): not yet learned through, before it learns itself; "ignorance inheres" is stronger than the Greek. |
