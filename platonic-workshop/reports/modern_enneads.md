## Flags

| Paragraph | MacKenna's words | Why |
|---|---|---|
| I.1.2.p1 | which Reason manifests | The Greek ἥντινα ἂν φήνῃ ὁ λόγος means "whatever the argument shows it to be": the "reason" (λόγος) here is the discussion now under way, not the soul's faculty of Reason. |
| I.1.4.p2 | Compare the passage (in Plato) where we read that " it is absurd to suppose that the Soul weaves " | MacKenna puts the saying in Plato; it is Aristotle's, in On the Soul I.4 (408b11-15), where he says that to call the soul angry is like saying that it weaves or builds. |
| I.1.7.p2 | Discursive-Reasoning, Sense-Knowledge and Intellection | The Greek has διάνοιαι καὶ δόξαι καὶ νοήσεις, "reasonings, opinions and acts of thought": δόξαι are opinions, not "sense-knowledge". |
| I.1.8.p1 | This also we possess as the summit of our being. | The Greek says we have intellect "above us" (ὑπεράνω ἡμῶν); MacKenna makes it the summit of our own being. |
| I.1.8.p1 | one of the emanations from the Intellectual-Principle | The Greek calls it a state (ἕξις) that the soul has, one of the things that come from intellect; the word "emanation" is MacKenna's. |
| I.1.8.p2 | the Divinity is contained in the Intellectual-Principle and Authentic-Existence | The Greek says God "rides upon" (ἐποχούμενον) the intelligible nature and real being: he is above it, not contained in it. |
| I.1.10.p1 | they are its repugnances, desires, sympathies | The Greek names "envies, jealousies and pities" (φθόνοι καὶ ζῆλοι καὶ ἔλεοι), as the feelings that show the vices belong to the compound. |
| I.1.12.p1 | We may take either view at will : they are easily reconciled. | The Greek is more careful: one may side with whichever account one wishes, "but perhaps one could find" how they do not conflict. There is no "easily". |
| I.1.12.p2 | Thus the Life is one thing, the Act is another and the Expiator yet another. | The Greek says the soul has "another life and other activities", and "what is punished is something different": not three things, but the soul's own life against the compound that is punished. τὸ κολαζόμενον is what is punished, not an "expiator". |
| I.1.13.p1 | Does this mean that the Soul reasons by possession (by contact with the matters of enquiry) ? | The Greek asks whether we inquired "by having" the soul; the words in brackets are MacKenna's guess, and not in the Greek. |
| I.1.13.p1 | Its Act subsists without movement | The Greek asks a question, "will it not, then, be moved?", and answers that we must grant the soul a movement that is not a body's: the soul is not said to be without movement. |
| I.4.1.p2 | it is impossible to deny the good of life to any order of living things | The Greek says that whoever takes living well away from "the other animals" is absurd; the plants have just been set aside, so it does not speak of every order of living things. |
| I.4.2.p1 | are really denying it to all living things | The Greek says they "run the risk" (κινδυνεύουσιν) of not granting it even to all the animals: the point is about the animals, and is put as a likelihood. |
| I.4.2.p3 | Let us try basing it upon Life. | These words are not in the Greek of this chapter, which ends with the people who are at a loss. The next chapter begins "let us say from the beginning what we take being happy to be", and only then puts it in life. |
| I.4.3.p2 | If mere Being is insufficient | There is no such clause in the Greek, which says: "if being happy belongs to what has life in the full (ἄγαν), that is, to what lacks nothing of life". |
| I.4.3.p3 | The Good, as a Divine Hypostasis | The words in brackets are MacKenna's explanation; the Greek says only "the nature of the Good". |
| I.4.4.p2 | for nothing is good that lies outside him | The Greek says "there is no good that he does not have" (οὐδὲν ... ἀγαθὸν ὃ μὴ ἔχει): it is about what he has, not about where goods lie. |
| I.4.4.p3 | to that in him which stands apart from the Supreme | The Greek says "what in him does not have intellect" (τὸ ἐν αὐτῷ νοῦν οὐκ ἔχον): the part without intellect, not a part that stands apart from the Supreme. |
| I.4.5.p1 | misery and disgrace | The Greek names poverty (πενία) and obscurity, or lack of good name (ἀδοξία): not misery and disgrace. |
| I.4.7.p1 | but they do serve towards the integrity of his being | The Greek says only that they contribute "to being" (πρὸς τὸ εἶναι), to his existing, as their opposites tend "towards not being". |
| I.4.7.p2 | or . . . Zeus ... by mortality among mortals | νὴ Δία is an oath, "by Zeus!": the Greek reads "who thinks wood and stones, and, by Zeus, the deaths of mortals, great things". The wood and stones are the razed city. |
| I.4.7.p3 | into his enemies' hands, into prison | The Greek says only "if he is led away captive" (αἰχμάλωτος), as a prisoner of war; and "the way out" is out of life, not an escape from prison. |
| I.4.8.p1 | when he is put to torture he will plan what is to be done | The Greek says "if it is drawn out" (εἰ παρατείνοι), of the pain; no torture is named. He "will take counsel", keeping his power over himself (τὸ αὐτεξούσιον). |
| I.4.10.p1 | When the Intellect is in upward orientation | The Greek says that awareness comes about "when the thought bends back" (ἀνακάμπτοντος τοῦ νοήματος); nothing is said of intellect turned upwards, and the words in brackets that follow are MacKenna's. |
| I.4.12.p1 | nor in any violent emotions | The Greek names περιχαρείας, transports of joy or wild delight: not violent emotions in general. |
| I.4.13.p1 | like a part of his being | The Greek says the greatest study is "always at hand and with him" (πρόχειρον ἀεὶ καὶ μετ’ αὐτοῦ); it does not call it a part of his being. |
| I.4.14.p1 | even a happy balance of temperament | The Greek has "nor, again, in perceiving well" (οὐδ’ αὖ ἐν τῷ αἰσθάνεσθαι εὖ): good senses, not good temperament. |
| I.4.15.p3 | it is to himself and in his own great concern that he is the Sage | The Greek says "he is such towards himself too, and in his own affairs" (τοιοῦτος γὰρ καὶ περὶ αὑτὸν καὶ ἐν τοῖς ἑαυτοῦ): the sternness is not kept for others; he is the same with himself. |
| I.5.2.p1 | at once time supplants moral excellence as the measure of felicity | The Greek says only that happiness "will no longer be measured by virtue" (τῇ ἀρετῇ); that time takes its place is MacKenna's gloss. |
| I.5.6.p2 | true happiness is not vague and fluid | The Greek says positively that happiness "has a limit and a bound" (ὅρον τε καὶ πέρας) and is always the same. |
| I.5.7.p1 | a mimic of eternity | The Greek says εἰκὼν αἰῶνος, "an image of eternity", Plato's words in the Timaeus (37d): an image, not a mimic. |
| I.5.8.p1 | last year's morality | The Greek is τὸ δὲ τῆς φρονήσεως, ὅτι πέρυσιν ἐφρόνουν, "and of wisdom, that last year I was wise": wisdom, not morality. |
| I.5.10.p2 | the good of the act is for all alike, no matter whose was the saving hand | The Greek says that "the pleasure at the saving of one's country could come to him even if another did it": it is about the good man's own pleasure, not a good shared by all. |
| I.6.1.p1 | another for the bodiless | The Greek asks whether beauty is "one thing in a body, another in something else" (ἐν ἄλλῳ); it does not yet speak of the bodiless. |
| I.6.1.p2 | so that there is a good deal between being body and being beautiful | The Greek says "as if being bodies were one thing and being beautiful another" (ἄλλου ... ἄλλου): the two are different, not far apart. |
| I.6.1.p4 | morality means weakness of will | The Greek says that self-control (σωφροσύνη) is foolishness (ἠλιθιότης), and justice a "noble simplicity" (γενναία εὐήθεια): these are the bad views of Thrasymachus in the Republic, not "weakness of will" and "honesty". |
| I.6.2.p1 | something which the soul names as from an ancient knowledge | The Greek says the soul speaks of it "as if it understood it" (ὥσπερ συνεῖσα): no ancient knowledge is named, though the soul is "reminded" of itself later in the chapter. |
| I.6.2.p3 | by communicating in the thought that flows from the Divine | The Greek says "by sharing in a reason (λόγου) that has come from gods" (ἀπὸ θεῶν), in the plural: a forming principle, not a thought, and from gods rather than from "the Divine". |
| I.6.3.p1 | never in doubt whenever any lovely thing presents itself for judgement | The Greek says that nothing has more authority to judge its own objects "when the rest of the soul judges along with it" (ὅταν καὶ ἡ ἄλλη συνεπικρίνῃ ψυχή): a judgement shared with the rest of the soul, not a power that is never in doubt. |
| I.6.3.p3 | The beauty of colour is also the outcome of a unification | The Greek says the beauty of colour is "simple" (ἁπλοῦν), by shape and mastery of the dark: Plotinus has just said that colours are beautiful without being composite, so they are not a unification of parts. |
| I.6.5.p2 | courage of the majestic face | The Greek says courage "with a grim face" (βλοσυρὸν ἔχουσαν πρόσωπον), fierce to look at, as Homer's Ajax is "grim of face" (Iliad 7.212); not majestic. |
| I.6.5.p3 | thinking, in the little thought it has, only of the perishable and the base | The Greek says "thinking all that it thinks as mortal and lowly things" (πάντα φρονοῦσα ἃ δὴ καὶ φρονεῖ θνητὰ καὶ ταπεινά): "the little thought it has" is MacKenna's. |
| I.6.5.p4 | by a fall, a descent into body, into Matter | The Greek speaks of "mixing and blending and inclining" (νεύσει) towards body and matter: a leaning, not a fall or a descent. |
| I.6.6.p1 | And Wisdom is but the Act of the Intellectual-Principle withdrawn | The Greek says wisdom is "thinking" (νόησις), "in a turning away from the things below": the soul's own thinking, not an act of intellect. |
| I.6.6.p2 | Hence the Soul heightened to the Intellectual-Principle is beautiful to all its power | The Greek says the soul led up to intellect "is beautiful the more" (ἐπὶ τὸ μᾶλλον): more beautiful, not beautiful to all its power. |
| I.6.6.p2 | for from the Divine comes all the Beauty and all the Good in beings | The Greek says "from there comes the beautiful and the other portion of beings" (ἡ μοῖρα ἡ ἑτέρα τῶν ὄντων); the Good is not named. The phrase is hard, and translators read it differently. |
| I.6.7.p1 | Even the desire of it is to be desired as a Good | The Greek says "it is desired as good, and the desire is towards it" (ἐφετὸν μὲν γὰρ ὡς ἀγαθὸν καὶ ἡ ἔφεσις πρὸς τοῦτο): the Good is what is desired; the desire itself is not called desirable. |
| I.6.7.p1 | who will set all their forces towards it | The Greek says those who "have turned round" (ἐπιστραφεῖσι): the soul's turning back to what is above, not an effort of all its forces. |
| I.6.7.p2 | Gods or Supernals | The Greek has "of gods or daemons" (θεῶν ... ἢ δαιμόνων), the spirits between gods and men; "Supernals" is MacKenna's word. |
| I.6.7.p3 | spurning the world of sense from beneath his feet | The Greek says only "leaving these behind and looking down on them" (καταλιπών τις ταῦτα καὶ ὑπεριδών), the kingdoms and rule just named; the world of sense under his feet is MacKenna's picture. |
| I.6.9.p1 | the perfect goodness surely established in the stainless shrine | The Greek says "self-control standing on a holy pedestal" (σωφροσύνην ἐν ἁγνῷ βεβῶσαν βάθρῳ), the Phaedrus' words (254b): self-control, not "perfect goodness". |
| I.6.9.p3 | who cares to see God and Beauty | The Greek says "the Good and the beautiful" (τἀγαθόν τε καὶ καλόν): the Good, not God. |
| I.6.9.p4 | the Primal Good and the Primal Beauty have the one dwelling-place | The Greek puts this as another way of speaking: "or he will place the Good and the first beautiful in the same" (ἢ ἐν τῷ αὐτῷ ... θήσεται), "but in any case the beautiful is there": a choice left open, not a conclusion. |
| I.7.1.p2 | it can but remain unmoved | The Greek says the Good is "at rest" (ἐν ἡσύχῳ), "in quiet": not "unmoved" in Aristotle's sense, though the meaning is close. |
| I.7.1.p3 | try all you will to separate the light from the sun, or the sun from its light, for ever the light is in the sun | The Greek says "even if you wish to cut it off on one side, the light is towards the sun" (πρὸς τὸν ἥλιον): the light still stretches back to its source. "Or the sun from its light" is not in the Greek, and the light is "towards" the sun, not "in" it. |
| I.7.2.p1 | But the Universe outside ; how is it aligned towards the Good ? | The Greek asks "and all the other things, how are they towards it?" (τὰ δ’ ἄλλα πάντα): it does not speak of a universe outside. |
| I.7.2.p1 | are no more than images of the Ideal-Form | The Greek says that what each thing shares in are "images of being and of the one, and the form likewise" (εἴδωλα ὄντος καὶ ἑνός, καὶ τὸ εἶδος ὡσαύτως): images of being and the one, not of the Ideal-Form. |
| I.7.3.p1 | the misfortune is still life, a life of a definite character | The Greek says only that life is evil there too, "since it is not life alone" (ὅτι μὴ ζωὴ μόνον): it is life with something added, the punishment; "a life of a definite character" is MacKenna's. |
| I.7.3.p2 | feel itself at home | The Greek says the soul "will be able to receive both" (ἀμφοτέρων δεκτική), life and death: it can be in either state, not that it feels at home in both. |
| I.8.1.p2 | are nearer to Non-Being | The Greek says the worse are "rather a privation" (στέρησις μᾶλλον), the lack of a form, Aristotle's word; MacKenna's "nearer to Non-Being" says something else. Where the question comes back, he has "utter Lack" for the same word. |
| I.8.2.p2 | And the First Act is the Act of The Good stationary within Itself, and the First Existence is the self-contained Existence of The Good | In the Greek it is intellect that "is the first activity of that one and its first being" (πρώτη ἐνέργεια ἐκείνου καὶ πρώτη οὐσία), while the Good "stays in itself": the Good does not itself act or exist as intellect does. MacKenna gives the first activity and the first existence to the Good. |
| I.8.2.p3 | circles around the Intellectual-Principle | The Greek says the soul "dances" round it (χορεύουσα), as a chorus round an altar: Plotinus' image for the soul's ordered life about what it loves. |
| I.8.3.p1 | of an utterly different order from Authentic-Being | The Greek says non-being here is "only other than being" (ἕτερον μόνον τοῦ ὄντος), Plato's sense of non-being in the Sophist; MacKenna makes it "utterly different". |
| I.8.3.p1 | there is no question here of movement or position with regard to Being | The Greek says this non-being is not like "motion and rest, which are about being" (ὡς κίνησις καὶ στάσις ἡ περὶ τὸ ὄν): two of the Sophist's greatest kinds, which are "not being" only because they are other than it. MacKenna's "position" is rest (στάσις). |
| I.8.3.p2 | that part is all lawless void | The Greek says only "that too is all of these" (καὶ αὐτὸ πάντα ταῦτα): the whole list of what evil is. "Lawless void" is MacKenna's. |
| I.8.3.p3 | become a cause of evil by consecration to this Absolute | The Greek says such a thing is evil "by looking towards it" (βλέπον πρὸς αὐτό), or "makes things such" (ποιητικόν): there is no "consecration". |
| I.8.4.p1 | Soul, on the contrary, since not every Soul is evil, is not an evil Kind | The Greek has two claims: the soul "in itself is not evil" (καθ’ ἑαυτὴν μὲν οὐ κακή), and "nor is every soul evil"; MacKenna leaves out "in itself" and makes the second the reason for a conclusion of his own. |
| I.8.4.p1 | all the states, foreign to the true nature | The Greek calls them "involuntary affections" (ἀκούσια παθήματα): the soul does not choose them. |
| I.8.4.p1 | not by their true value but by the mere test of like and dislike | The Greek says the false opinions make the soul "think evil and good the things it flees and pursues": it mistakes what it avoids and seeks for evil and good. There is no "true value" or "like and dislike". |
| I.8.5.p3 | the Evil which holds men down binds them against their will | The Greek speaks of the evils (ἃ δ’ ἂν ἀνθρώπους κατάσχῃ) "that take hold of men", in the plural; it means the evils, not "the Evil". |
| I.8.6.p1 | with justice and piety in the light of philosophy | The Greek, quoting Plato, says "just and holy with wisdom" (μετὰ φρονήσεως): practical wisdom, not philosophy. |
| I.8.6.p2 | The Good in the Supernal | The Greek says only "that Good" (ἐκείνῳ τῷ ἀγαθῷ). |
| I.8.6.p2 | the good thing by which Matter is brought to order | The Greek says virtue is "a good, which makes us master matter" (ὃ κρατεῖν τῆς ὕλης ποιεῖ): mastering, not ordering. |
| I.8.6.p2 | established by practical proof | The Greek says "shown by induction" (τῇ ἐπαγωγῇ δεδειγμένον), by going through the cases one by one: Aristotle's argument in the Categories. |
| I.8.7.p2 | Since you possess only a derivative being | The Greek of the Timaeus says "since you have come into being" (ἐπείπερ ἐγένεσθε); MacKenna's words of introduction, "in which the Father of Gods addresses the Divinities of the lower sphere", are his own explanation. |
| I.8.8.p1 | not in the concrete signification but in the presence or absence of just such forms | The Greek says "fillings, emptyings, and not fillings simply, but fillings with such and such things" (πληρώσεις τοιῶνδε): being full of this food or that. There is nothing about concrete significations or the absence of forms. |
| I.8.8.p3 | the incoming Idea should have been able to conquer the Matter | The Greek says only "but one should master it, someone might say" (ἀλλὰ κρατεῖν ἔδει): it is we, or the reason in us, who should master the body's desires; "the incoming Idea" is MacKenna's. |
| I.8.8.p3 | in times of stress, we are not the same | The Greek speaks of being "full" and "empty" (πλήρεις, κενοί), filled with this or with that: our desires and thoughts change with what we have eaten and drunk, not with stress and peace. |
| I.8.9.p1 | by means of the philosophic habit | The Greek says "by intellect itself and by wisdom" (νῷ αὐτῷ καὶ φρονήσει), and gives the reason MacKenna leaves out: "for it knows itself". |
| I.8.9.p2 | an ugly appearance in Matter | The Greek has "an ugly face" (αἰσχρόν τι πρόσωπον), an example taken from life. |
| I.8.10.p1 | give it Quality and in its very Evil it would almost be a Form | The Greek says matter is evil not by having a quality, "so that perhaps it is not evil as being a form" (ἵνα μὴ ᾖ ἴσως κακὴ εἶδος οὖσα), but as a nature contrary to form; MacKenna's "give it Quality" and "almost" are his reading. |
| I.8.11.p1 | This objection may be answered by applying the principle to the case of Evil in the Soul | In the Greek the objection goes on: if evil is privation, it is in the soul, as its vice, and "nothing outside"; there is no answer until the next paragraph ("But if privation..."). MacKenna makes the objector's conclusion into an answer. |
| I.8.11.p2 | it has much good in it | The Greek says the soul "has something good" (ἔχουσά τι ἀγαθόν), "a trace of intellect": the argument needs only some good, not much. |
| I.8.13.p2 | where, fallen from all our resemblance to the Divine, we lie in gloom and mud | The Greek says "sinking into it, one will have fallen into dark mud" (εἰς βόρβορον σκοτεινόν): "fallen from all our resemblance to the Divine" is MacKenna's gloss on "the place of unlikeness". |
| I.8.13.p2 | And the death of Soul is twofold | The Greek says only "its death": "while still in the body" to be sunk in matter, "and when it has gone out" to lie there. The two times are there, but "twofold" is MacKenna's. |
| I.8.14.p1 | as hasty to compromises | The Greek says the evil soul is "rash in its assents" (προπετῆ εἰς συγκαταθέσεις): quick to agree to impressions, the Stoics' word for judgement, not "compromises". |
| I.8.14.p1 | burned away by every heat | The Greek says such weak things are "easily carried about" (ῥᾳδίαν τὴν φοράν) by winds and the heat of the sun: carried about, not burned away. |
| I.8.14.p3 | for the Soul cannot lift up this foreign thing close by, since the evil of it makes it invisible | The Greek says the source of light "does not bear with" matter (οὐ γὰρ ἀνέχεται αὐτήν), though matter is present, "because matter does not see" it, by its badness: it is matter that cannot see, not matter that is invisible, and nothing is "lifted up". |
| I.8.15.p2 | within that place of its choice | The Greek says the soul must be "with intellect alone, settled in it" (ἐν αὐτῷ ἱδρυμένην): "that place of its choice" is MacKenna's. |
| I.8.15.p3 | not destitute of Images of the Good and Beautiful | The Greek has "images of the beautiful" (εἰδώλοις τοῦ καλοῦ): "the Good" is added. |
| I.9.1.p1 | For wheresoever it go, it will be in some definite condition | The Greek says "it will go out having something" (ἔχουσά τι), something of the body, "if it goes out at all": the reason for the opening command, not a condition it will be in. |
| I.9.1.p2 | movements which it is unlawful to indulge | The Greek says only "but one must do nothing" (δεῖ δὲ μηδὲν πράττειν): nothing is done that would come from these feelings. There is no law in the Greek. |
| I.9.1.p2 | That is not likely in the Sage | The Greek's σπουδαῖος is "the good man", serious about virtue, as elsewhere in the Workshop (see I.4); MacKenna's "Sage" makes him a Stoic figure. |
