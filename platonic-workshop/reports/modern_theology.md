## Flags

| Paragraph | Taylor's words | Why |
|---|---|---|
| I.1.p1 | capable of enjoying it for a time | Taylor's "for a time" is unclear: it can be read as "for a while", or as "in time", that is, to souls who live in time. The modern version takes the second reading, which fits the contrast with the gods; the Greek is not yet on the site to settle it. |
| I.1.p3 | lead it about the Vesta of Plato | Taylor gives Hestia her Latin name, Vesta. She is the goddess of the hearth, who in the Phaedrus (247a) stays at home while the other gods go round; Proclus means the fixed centre of Plato's teaching, round which the soul is led. |
| I.2.p4 | intelligence in conjunction with reason [true] beings | Taylor adds "true" in brackets, and his footnote corrects the Greek he translated (from "intelligence with reason" as a subject to "by intelligence with reason"). The phrase is the Timaeus' (28a): what always is, is grasped "by intellectual insight together with a rational account". |
| I.3.p3 | one multitude indeed being uniform | Taylor's "uniform" (Greek henoeides, "one-like") does not mean "all the same": it means having the form of the One, made of unities. The first multitude is that of the henads, the gods. |
| I.3.p5 | the multiform, and many-shaped genera, which are hurled forth before the Gods | Taylor's footnote says these are "evil daemons". They are the frightening apparitions that, in the rites, appear before the god appears; the modern version keeps Proclus' image. |
| I.4.p1 | the liberated rulers of the universe | "Liberated" (Greek apolytoi, "unbound") is Proclus' name for an order of gods between the gods above the world and those in it: they rule the world but are not bound to it. The Workshop keeps Taylor's word. |
| I.4.p2 | that it is a passive one | Taylor's "passive one" renders the Sophist's phrase (245a): being "has unity as an affection", has the one as something that happens to it. It does not mean that being is inactive; the modern version says what is meant. |
| I.4.p5 | when we frame precedaneous demonstrations | Taylor's "precedaneous" (an old word for "going first, principal") means the demonstrations that belong first and properly to the subject: the account of the Parmenides, which is the core of the work. |
| I.5.p4 | the two first principles of things, together with the triad | Taylor's footnote: the Greek he translated lacks the word "triad". The two principles are limit and the unlimited (Philebus 16c, 23c), and the triad is the mixture and its cause; Proclus treats them in Book III. |
| I.5.p5 | Pythagoras first of all learning from Aglaophemus the orgies of the Gods | Taylor's "orgies" means the secret rites of the gods (Greek orgia), not revelry. |
| I.6.p2 | certain persons, junior to Plato, have delivered in their writings, and left to their disciples, one perfect form of theology | The objector's point is that later writers (it is not said who; Proclus may mean the authors of the Chaldean Oracles, or his own school) have left complete theologies, while Plato himself has not, in any one work. |
| I.6.p3 | For I think the latter are species of the former | Pleasure and the life of intellect (the latter) are kinds of the unlimited and of limit (the former): in the Philebus (27e-28a, 31a) pleasure belongs to the unlimited, and intellect is kin to the cause of the mixture. |
| I.7.p2 | the celebrated generation of the Gods | Proclus means a theogony, an account of the gods' births such as Hesiod's and the Orphics'; the Parmenides, read by the school, gives the same in the form of a deduction. |
| I.9.p1 | assimilates himself to the Ibycean horse | In the Parmenides (137a) the old Parmenides compares himself to the ageing racehorse in a poem of Ibycus, who trembles before the race because he knows what is coming; so Parmenides fears to swim "so great a sea of words". |
| I.9.p2 | the dæmoniacal Aristotle | Taylor's "dæmoniacal" means "more than human, inspired": a title of honour, not of reproach. The power Aristotle speaks of is dialectic, the art of arguing from accepted opinions (Topics I, 100a-101b). |
| I.9.p4 | accuses Plato of dissimilitude of imitation | That is, of making Parmenides speak out of character, an imitation unlike its original: the fault Plato finds in the poets (Republic III 390e-391e, Achilles and the gifts). |
| I.9.p7 | surrounds all disciplines like a defensive enclosure | The Republic's word (534e) is a coping stone, set on top of the studies: dialectic crowns the mathematical studies as the top course of a wall. |
| I.9.p8 | presides over the demonstrative phantasy | Taylor's "phantasy" is imagination: the merely logical method rules the imagination that follows the steps of a proof, not intellect. |
| I.10.p3 | confabulator of Plato (that I may use the language of Homer) | Homer's word (oaristes, "familiar companion in talk") is used of Minos, who "conversed with great Zeus" (Odyssey 19.179); Proclus gives it to Syrianus, as Plato's familiar. |
| I.11.p4 | has a redundant hyparxis with respect to the imparticipable unity | Taylor's "redundant" means "having something over": first being has, beyond the unshared One, being added to its unity; it is one-and-being, not the One alone. |
| I.12.p1 | subsist in unproceeding union with this first cause | Taylor's "unproceeding" means "without going forth": though true beings proceed from the One, through the one in them they also remain in it. |
| I.12.p3 | the highest negations are only enunciative, but some in a supereminent manner, and others according to deficiency | The first hypothesis denies everything of the One because the One is above it all; the fifth denies everything of matter because matter falls short of it all. Taylor's "enunciative" means simply denying, without affirming anything else. Taylor's footnotes correct the Greek he translated in two places of this paragraph ("likeness" for "unlikeness"). |
| I.13.p6 | rightly following Socrates, and considering what the end of things is | Proclus seems to mean Socrates in the Phaedrus (245c-e), who proves the soul immortal as the self-moving source of motion; Taylor's "the end of things" is unclear, and may mean "the result", what follows. |
| I.13.p7 | possess this intellectual, perfect, and beneficent power, according to participation, or according to essence | That is, whether the world-soul is intelligent by its very being (so that every soul would be), or by sharing in an intellect above it. Proclus takes the second: intellect is above soul. |
| I.13.p10 | but soul, by looking to intellect, and living from itself, is primarily divine | This seems to contradict what was just said, that divinity is first in intellect. Proclus means that soul is the first of the things that are divine by sharing, before body, while intellect is divine by its very being; Taylor's footnote supplies the word "divine" earlier in the paragraph. |
| I.14.p4 | with the punishment of what Ixion | Ixion was bound for ever to a turning wheel; Proclus means a providence that would be endless forced labour, like his. |
| I.15.p2 | paternal, guardian, ruling and pæonian powers | "Paeonian" means healing, from Paeon, the healer of the gods, a name of Apollo. Those "wise in divine things" are the theologians, the Orphics and the Chaldean Oracles, which name such orders of gods. |
| I.15.p2 | consequently be alone the causes of adumbrant good | Taylor's "adumbrant" means shadowy: a good in appearance only. |
| I.17.p1 | for that which is primarily good will not be the second after the Gods | The point is that nothing can be good first and yet below the gods: whatever is good first is itself divine, so the gods are the causes of all good. |
| I.17.p3 | For evil itself is even beyond that which in no respect whatever has an existence | Proclus means that evil "itself", evil without any good, would be even less than nothing; so it cannot exist, as the Good is above what wholly is. Taylor's footnotes supply "cause" and correct "darken" in the Greek he translated. |
| I.17.p4 | there are precedaneous productive principles of evil in nature | Taylor's "precedaneous" (going before, original) and "productive principles" (logoi): Proclus denies that evil has forming principles in nature or patterns in intellect, against those, like the Gnostics and the Manichaeans, or a reading of the Laws' "evil soul" (896e), who posit a cause of evil. |
| I.18.p1 | the first demiurgus the best of causes | Timaeus 29a: the world is the most beautiful of things that have come to be, and its maker the best of causes. Proclus' point is that each god is "best" in his own order, though the intelligible pattern is above the Craftsman. |
| I.18.p3 | precedaneous causes | Taylor's "precedaneous" means going before: the causes prior to bodies, from which bodies get their being. |
| I.18.p4 | when speaking of the intelligible subjections | Taylor's "subjections" seems to mean the levels set below the intelligible (soul below intellect, intellect below the intelligible). The place in Plotinus is not certain; compare III.8.8 and V.3. |
| I.19.p2 | various phantasms are hurled forth before the presence of them | In the rites, apparitions were believed to appear before a god shows himself; Proclus explains them as lights of the gods mixed with what receives them, not as the gods' own shapes. |
| I.20.p1 | For the first parts do not admit of a truth of this kind | The "first parts" of speech are names and words taken alone, which are neither true nor false; in the Cratylus (385c) Socrates says that if a statement is true, its parts, down to names, are true too. |
| I.22.p1 | is educed by us according to a projecting energy, and an adhesion of the dianoetic power | Taylor's "projecting energy" (epibolē) is the mind's reaching towards an object; "dianoetic power" is discursive reasoning (dianoia). The desirable, Proclus says, cannot be grasped by either. |
| I.22.p3 | the primary and most principal fountain and vestal seat of things | Taylor's "vestal seat" is the hearth (hestia), the fixed centre of a household or city; Proclus uses it for the firm seat from which all things proceed. |
| I.24.p1 | the intelligible place of survey | That is, the "place above the heavens" of the Phaedrus (247c), where the souls see true being; Proclus places the first beauty there, in the intelligible order. |
| I.24.p2 | denominating it the delicate | In the Symposium (204c) Phaedrus' word is "delicate" (habron): "what is lovable is truly beautiful, delicate, perfect and most blessed". Proclus gives the delicate, the bright and the loved as the three signs of beauty. |
| I.24.p3 | walking on the extremities of its feet | From the Symposium (195d), where Agathon says Love is delicate and walks not on the earth but "on the softest things"; Proclus' image is of beauty treading lightly, scarcely touching what it moves. |
| I.25.p1 | neither gnostically | Taylor's "gnostically" means by way of knowledge (gnōsis); Proclus means that the good is reached by a faith beyond knowing, not by an act of knowledge. |
| I.26.p2 | but at the same time is that which participates and that which is participated; of which the latter is a God, but the former is divine | Taylor's "latter" and "former" are the wrong way round for his own order of words: what is shared (the henad) is a god, and what shares in it (being) is divine. The modern English follows the sense. |
| I.26.p2 | Whether however, prior to the participated unities, there is something which is separate and participated | Taylor's "and participated" should be "and not participated", as the sense requires: the question is whether there is an unshared henad (the One) before the shared ones. |
| I.26.p3 | the series of beings ends in the one itself | "Ends" in the sense of having its summit there: going upward, the series of beings ends in the One. |
| I.26.p4 | a renovated immortality | The Statesman 270a: the universe receives from its maker "a renewed (or acquired) immortality"; its body does not hold itself together, but is renewed by its cause. |
| I.27.p3 | in the Orphic writings, indeed, the first cause is on this account denominated Time | In the Orphic theogony the first principle is Chronos, Time; Proclus takes the name as a symbol of order by cause, not of a beginning in time. |
| I.27.p3 | some posterior to Plato perceiving, have indefinitely shown that the whole heaven is unbegotten | Proclus seems to mean Aristotle (On the Heavens I.10-12), who holds that the heaven is unbegotten and imperishable. "Indefinitely" means without qualifying in what sense. |
| II.1.p3 | will be multitude in energy | That is, many in act and not only in potential: each supposed part would actually be many, without end. |
| II.1.p5 | privations being changed, entirely lead into habits the things that are changed | Taylor's "privation" and "habit" are Aristotle's pair, deprivation (sterēsis) and possession (hexis): a thing that changes out of lacking something comes to have it. |
| II.1.p13 | since now also they subsist together causally. It is however impossible for the many to subsist separate from the one. The mixture therefore is not causal | Taylor's "causally" here must mean "by chance" (the Greek is probably kata symbebēkos or apo tychēs): the argument is that the mixture is not a matter of chance, since the many cannot ever have existed apart from the One. |
| II.1.p13 | The one therefore, and that which is not one, are the cause of nothing else to beings than of their being one | That is, even a supposed not-one above the One would give beings nothing but union, which is what the One gives; so it would be no higher than the One. Taylor's footnotes correct and supply words of the Greek here. |
| II.2.p1 | they must be divulsed from each other | Taylor's "divulsed" means torn apart: principles with nothing in common. |
| II.2.p6 | the one will be passive to essence | Taylor's "passive to" means "an attribute of", something that being "undergoes" or has: if being were before the One, oneness would be only a property of being. Taylor's footnote: if being and the One ranked together, they would be two, and so share in the first many. |
| II.3.p1 | Is, therefore, permanency every where | Taylor's "permanency" is rest, stillness (stasis), one of the Sophist's five great kinds. |
| II.3.p2 | that which is in generation, or passing into existence, is said to be, and is destitute of essence | Proclus means that what is coming to be (genesis) is spoken of as being, yet is not yet being in the full sense; Timaeus 27d-28a: what always comes to be "never really is". |
| II.3.p4 | the summits of inferiors are conjoined with the boundaries of superiors | A rule of Proclus' system (Elements of Theology 147): the highest member of each lower order is joined to the lowest member of the order above. |
| II.3.p6 | as some say, who generate things more excellent from things subordinate | Proclus seems to mean the old physicists, and the Pythagoreans and Speusippus as Aristotle reports them (Metaphysics XII.7, 1072b; XIV.4-5), who held that the good and the beautiful come at the end, not the beginning. |
| II.3.p7 | that which is best if it receives another addition through that which is inferior will be more perfect than that which does not abide in the best | Taylor's English says the opposite of what the argument needs: what is best, if mixed with something lower, will be less perfect than what remains purely in the best. The modern English follows the argument. |
| II.4.p1 | Origen, who was a partaker of the same erudition with Plotinus | This is Origen the Platonist, a fellow pupil of Plotinus under Ammonius Saccas, not the Christian Origen. He wrote a treatise "That the King is the only Maker", which Proclus takes as making intellect the first principle. |
| II.4.p1 | because the one is perfectly unhyparctic | Taylor's "unhyparctic" means without hyparxis, without any existence of its own. |
| II.4.p3 | permanency or motion | Taylor's "permanency" is rest (stasis): the Sophist's kinds, rest and motion, both are, but neither is being itself (250a-c). "Rest" here means remaining still. |
| II.5.p1 | nor defining the sameness in words analogously, and words in habitudes, endeavour to calumniate this anagogic progression | The sense seems to be: let no one object that analogy only finds a sameness of words or of relations (habitudes), and so slander this way that leads up (anagogic) to the first. |
| II.6.p2 | neither by intellectual injection, [or projection] | Taylor's "injection" and "projection" render epibolē, the mind's reaching out to its object. Things are united to the One not by any act of knowing. |
| II.7.p2 | imitates the primogenial cause of itself | Taylor's "primogenial" means first-born, original; Proclus means that the sun imitates the Good, its own cause. |
| II.7.p4 | to the superessential unities of intelligibles and the first essences | Proclus seems to mean the henads at the summit of the intelligible order, beyond being, and the first beings that hang from them. |
| II.7.p5 | from the name of idea | That is, from the name of a Form (idea): the Good is named after the Form of the good, though it is beyond the Forms. |
| II.7.p6 | as the most splendid of being | Republic VI 518c: the Good is "the brightest of being" (to phanotaton tou ontos), which the soul must learn to bear to look at. |
| II.8.p5 | but you will not speak it | Proclus' distinction: we can speak about the first, from what comes after it, but never say what it is. |
| II.8.p5 | superessential torches | Taylor's "torches" are the lights or flames of the gods (a Chaldean image): their summits, beyond being, by which they touch the first. |
| II.9.p4 | the fountain of the beautiful | Plotinus (VI.7.32-33, I.6.9) calls the Good "the source and principle of beauty", beyond beauty itself. |
| II.10.p2 | does not begin his negations from the Gods that are united to the first | That is, the first denial ("the One is not many", 137c) answers not to the highest gods (the intelligible gods, united to the One) but to the order where multitude first appears; the denials of being at the end (141e) return to the highest. |
| II.11.p1 | beyond the first adyta | The adyta are the innermost shrines of a temple, which only priests may enter; Proclus sets the One beyond even the first, the intelligible gods. |
| II.11.p1 | Titannically divide the intellect that is in us | In the Orphic myth the Titans tore the child Dionysus to pieces; the Neoplatonists read this as the dividing of the one intellect into many, and of the soul into the body. |
| II.12.p1 | in the summit of the first intellectual Gods, and in the intelligible place of survey | Proclus' "intellectual gods" here are the intelligible and at the same time intellectual gods, the middle rank between the intelligible and the intellectual (Book IV); "the place of vision" is the Phaedrus' plain above the heavens, which he puts at their summit. |
| II.12.p5 | For if the one and the same differ only in name, the many will not be in consequence of participating of sameness with each other | The argument (Parmenides 139d-e): if being the same were being one, then whatever is the same as something would be one with it, and the many, being the same as each other, would be one. The modern English follows the sense. |
| II.12.p8 | younger and at the same time older both than themselves and other things | The Parmenides 140e-141d: the One is neither older nor younger, nor the same age, nor in time at all. Proclus reads these as the marks of divine souls, which live in time but in circles, always returning to their beginning. |
| II.12.p9 | asserting that the one is above essence, and being characterized by the one | The Parmenides 141e-142a: the One does not share in being, and so "is not even one"; Proclus reads this as setting the One above the one being, the summit of the intelligible gods. Taylor's footnote corrects the Greek he translated ("to the gods"). |
| III.1.p1 | the inartificial theory of Plato | Taylor's "inartificial" seems to mean unlaboured, natural (atechnos): Plato's account of the gods comes not from art or contrivance, but from the things themselves. |
| III.2.p2 | it contains in one the reasons, [i.e. productive principles] of all things | Taylor's "reasons" and "productive principles" are logoi, the forming principles that nature holds and spreads into bodies. |
| III.2.p3 | possess a less extended power of signification | Taylor's "signification" seems to mean reach or scope: the further causes are from the first, the fewer things they extend to. Compare Elements of Theology 56-62. |
| III.3.p2 | connects the one with being, surveys all things about the one | Proclus means the one being of the Parmenides' second hypothesis (142b), "if one is", from which all the orders of gods are drawn. Taylor's two footnotes correct "one" to "being" and "present" to "leading" in the Greek he translated. |
| III.3.p3 | But neither shall we be persuaded by Parmenides who produces the one together with being | The sense required is the opposite: "Nor shall we be persuaded otherwise by Parmenides", or "We shall not fail to be persuaded": Proclus is calling on the second hypothesis, which joins one and being, as evidence that the henads are shared by beings. Taylor's footnote corrects "unlike" to "like" in the Greek he translated. |
| III.5.p1 | their luciform vehicles | The soul's "vehicle of light" (augoeides ochēma), the subtle body of light that, for the later Platonists, the soul has always, before it takes on the material body. Taylor's footnote corrects the Greek he translated here ("light-formed" for "self-formed"). |
| III.5.p2 | in the souls of the more excellent genera | The "more excellent kinds" are the angels, daemons and heroes, set between the gods and human souls. |
| III.6.p3 | the orectic powers | Taylor's "orectic" means of appetite or desire (orexis). |
| III.6.p6 | the former as being moved and standing still at one and the same time, and the latter as being motion and permanency | That is, intellect is moved and at rest, while life is motion and rest themselves. Taylor's "permanency" here is rest (stasis). |
| III.8.p1 | nor in reality is that which is the first the one; for, as has been frequently said, it is better than the one | Proclus means that the first principle is beyond even the name "one"; the one that is the principle of being is limit, the first after the first. |
| III.8.p2 | by restitutions and periods | Taylor's "restitutions" are returns (apokatastaseis): the soul's, and the heaven's, coming back round to the same state at the end of each period. |
| III.9.p2 | deriving its subsistence from the Vesta of beings | Taylor's "Vesta" is Hestia, the hearth: being is the fixed centre from which all beings have their existence. |
| III.9.p2 | luminous patefactions | Taylor's "patefaction" means a laying open, a manifestation: limit and the unlimited are "shown" (Philebus 23c) by the first God, not made. |
| III.9.p4 | calls being the first power | The Sophist 247d-e: the Stranger proposes "that being is nothing else than power" (dynamis), to act or be acted on. Proclus reads it as the power, born of the unlimited, that being holds. |
| III.10.p1 | concauses are subject to the things which are produced from them | Taylor's "concauses" are joint or contributing causes (synaitia), such as matter: in the lower world they come before, and are set below, what is made from them. |
| III.12.p1 | deriving its completion indeed through the intelligible connective triad | That is, the mixed is completed through the triad of limit, unlimited and mixed itself, the intelligible triad that "holds together" (synektikē). |
| III.13.p1 | some one calls even the motion of material bodies life | Aristotle, Physics VIII.1 (250b): motion is "a kind of life for all things that exist by nature". Taylor's footnote corrects "irrational" to "analogous" in the Greek he translated. |
| III.13.p2 | as in the progression according to breadth | Proclus' two dimensions: "breadth" is the spread of the three terms within one triad (limit, unlimited, mixed); "depth" is the descent of the three triads one below another. |
| III.14.p2 | the first triad is an intelligible God primarily. But the triad posterior to it is an intelligible and intellectual God. And the third triad is an intellectual God | Not the later orders of the intelligible-intellectual and intellectual gods (Books IV and V), but the three intelligible triads, each named by its character: the third, intelligible intellect, is "intellectual" within the intelligible. Taylor's footnotes correct the Greek he translated in three places. |
| III.15.p1 | animal itself | Taylor's "animal itself" is the Timaeus' "living being itself" (autozōon), the pattern of the world. |
| III.16.p1 | The dæmoniacal Aristotle | "Daemonic" as high praise: Aristotle, On the Heavens I.9 (279a), calls eternity (aiōn) "immortal and divine", from which "being and life" depend for all things. |
| III.18.p1 | eternity is the cause of never-failing life, of unwearied power, and unsluggish energy | From the Chaldean Oracles (frag. 49 and 59 are related), where Aion is a god who gives unfailing life and power. Taylor's footnote supplies "time" in the Greek he translated. |
| III.18.p3 | If, however, truth is indeed the first, beauty the second, and symmetry the third | In the Philebus (65a-66b) the order of goods is measure, proportion and beauty, then truth (intellect); Proclus means here the order in the third mixture's list, where truth comes before beauty and proportion. |
| III.19.p2 | the cause of air-wandering and volant animals | Timaeus 40a: the four kinds are the heavenly race of gods, the winged kind "that travels in the air", the kind that lives in water, and the kind "that walks on land". Proclus takes them as kinds of gods first, then of daemons and mortals. |
| III.20.p3 | the proteleia of the mysteries of the Parmenides | The proteleia were the preliminary rites before an initiation; the Sophist prepares for the Parmenides as these did for the mysteries. |
| III.21.p4 | being inspired by the Nymphs | In the Phaedrus (238c-d, 241e) Socrates says he is possessed by the Nymphs of the place where he speaks. |
| III.22.p1 | that when it appeared astonished the intellectual Gods, and made them admire their father, as Orpheus says | From the Orphic theogony: when Phanes, the first-born, appeared, the gods marvelled at the light; Proclus sets Phanes at the end of the intelligible order. |
| III.23.p1 | some say it is, in the first hypothesis, concerning God and the Gods | Proclus' Commentary on the Parmenides (VI-VII) reviews earlier readings: some (perhaps Origen, or Amelius) took the first hypothesis to be about the first God and the gods, others about the intelligible gods. Taylor's footnote corrects a word of the Greek he translated. |
| III.23.p2 | with a divine head pursued these sacred paths | A Homeric phrase ("with the head inspired"); Proclus means that he followed Syrianus as an initiate follows the guide, with the highest part of the soul. |
| III.24.p1 | the habitude which is the middle of both | Taylor's "habitude" is relation (schesis): the link between the one and being, which Proclus identifies with power. |
| III.26.p2 | flowing according to the Oracle | A Chaldean phrase for the procession of the intelligible into multitude. |
| III.26.p2 | this triad, according to Parmenides, is primogenial | Taylor's "primogenial" is first-born (prōtogonos): Proclus links the third intelligible triad with Phanes, called Protogonos in the Orphic poems. |

Taylor's footnote supplies "cause" in the Greek he translated. |
| IV.1.p1 | according to the Oracle perceiving intellectually are at the same time intellectually perceived | The Chaldean Oracles speak of gods who "think and are thought"; the phrase "intelligible and intellectual" (noētoi kai noeroi) names this whole order. |
| IV.1.p1 | generating all the intellectual hebdomads | The intellectual gods form a hebdomad (seven): Kronos, Rhea, Zeus, the three Kouretes (the "unpolluted" guardians), and the god who separates (Book V). |
| IV.3.p3 | the connectedly containing Gods | Taylor's "connectedly containing" (synochic) is Proclus' name for the second of these triads: the gods who "hold together" all things. The third are the "perfecting" (teletarchic) gods. |
| IV.4.p1 | blessed spectacles, and discursive energies of the intelligibles | The Phaedrus 247a: "many and blessed are the sights and paths (diexodoi) within the heaven". Taylor renders diexodoi "discursive energies", as if it meant the passage of thought; the Greek means ways through, or courses. |
| IV.4.p2 | according to habitude towards the heaven | Taylor's "habitude" is relation (schesis). Taylor's footnote supplies "and the place above the heaven" in the Greek he translated. |
| IV.5.p1 | exchange intelligible for doxastic nutriment | The Phaedrus 248b: souls that fail to see true being "use the food of opinion" (trophē doxastē). Taylor's footnote says the sentence after this one is too corrupt in the Greek he translated to render, and he leaves it out. |
| IV.5.p3 | sight, says he, looking to the things above, is Heaven | The Cratylus 396b-c: ouranos (heaven) is explained as horōsa ta anō, "looking at the things above". Zeus' name (Zēna, Dia) is "through whom (di' hon) life (zēn)" comes; Kronos' is koros nou, "pure intellect". |
| IV.6.p1 | a partial intellect contemplates the supercelestial place | The Phaedrus 247c: being in the place above the heaven "is seen only by intellect, the soul's pilot". A "partial" intellect is the individual intellect of a soul, as against the one making intellect of the whole. |
| IV.7.p1 | the arch, the intelligible extremity | Taylor prints "intelligible" here; the sense (the place above the heaven is the intelligible summit, the arch the extremity where intellect turns back) seems to want "intellectual", and the Workshop renders it so. This is a reading from the sense, not from the Greek. |
| IV.9.p1 | through the liberated leaders | The "liberated" (apolytoi, "unbound") leaders are the twelve gods who lead in the Phaedrus, ranked by Proclus among the gods above the world; the "source" (fontal) gods are the intellectual gods, sources of the orders after them. The order of the ascent runs from the lowest of these orders upward. |
| IV.9.p2 | muesis and epopteia | The two grades of the Eleusinian mysteries: muēsis, initiation (from muein, to close the eyes), and epopteia, the final "watching". Taylor's footnote quotes Hermeias' commentary on the Phaedrus: in muesis the soul no longer takes in the divine mysteries by sense but with the pure soul itself, and in epopteia it is set in them and becomes their watcher. |
| IV.9.p2 | theurgists order the whole body to be buried, except the head | A Chaldean rite of "immortalisation", known only from this passage and a few others: the initiate was buried to the neck, as if the body were already dead. |
| IV.9.p2 | elevating the head of the charioteer to the place beyond the heaven | The Phaedrus 248a: the best souls "raise the head of the charioteer into the place outside". |
| IV.11.p2 | the one and united triad | Proclus' name for the first of the intelligible and intellectual triads, the place above the heaven: one and "united" (hēnōmenē) as the image of the hidden union of the intelligibles. |
| IV.12.p2 | But the one,” says he, “does not touch itself | The Parmenides 138a: the One is "neither in itself nor in another", so it neither touches itself nor anything else; Proclus takes the denial of contact as one of those that come from a definite order of gods. Here "the one" is Parmenides' one. |
| IV.12.p3 | known by physiologists | Taylor's "physiologists" are the natural philosophers (physiologoi), the students of nature, as against the theologians. |
| IV.14.p1 | elevating them to their paternal port | "The fatherly harbour" (patrikos hormos) is a Chaldean phrase for the soul's home in the intelligible, to which it returns. |
| IV.14.p1 | in a silent path, as they say | Euripides, Trojan Women 887-888: justice "leads all mortal things aright, going on a noiseless path". |
| IV.15.p2 | the one is as it were humid, and not bounded from itself; but the other is as it were solid | Here "the one" is nectar and "the other" ambrosia, though ambrosia was named first in the sentence before; the next sentences show it. The modern version names them. |
| IV.16.p2 | In the first monads however [i.e. in the plain of truth and the meadow,] this intelligible light subsists contractedly | Taylor's brackets take "the first monads" as the plain of truth and the meadow, and "the third" as the cause that nourishes the gods. The sentence before says the opposite (the plain of truth is the third monad); Proclus means the first two monads, food and meadow, with the light shining out in the third, the plain of truth. |
| IV.17.p1 | the sacred law of Adrastia | Taylor's "sacred law" is thesmos, "ordinance", as against nomos, "law". Adrastia ("she whom none escapes") was in the Orphic poems the nurse and guardian of the infant Zeus. |
| IV.17.p2 | receiving brazen drumsticks, and a drum made from the skin of a goat | Orphic fragment (Kern 105, 152): Adrastia stood before the cave of Night and beat a bronze drum, so that the gods heard her laws. Taylor's footnote corrects a word of the Greek he translated ("a kind of proclamation"). |
| IV.18.p1 | producing its own meadow from the fontal summit which is there | Proclus means the source of life in the second intelligible triad (eternity), from which the meadow, the life-giving power of this place, is drawn. |
| IV.19.p1 | the nature of spirit and local motion | The Stoics held that pneuma ("breath", Taylor's "spirit"), a fiery air pervading bodies, holds them together by its "tensile motion". Taylor's footnote corrects "intelligibles" to "intellectuals" in the Greek he translated. |
| IV.19.p2 | fabulous sections | The mythical "cuttings", the castration of Ouranos by Kronos and of Kronos by Zeus in Hesiod and the Orphic poems, which Proclus reads as the separation of the intellectual orders. |
| IV.19.p2 | discussive energies | The Phaedrus' diexodoi again (247a), the "paths" of the gods (see IV.4). |
| IV.21.p2 | the proximate sunocheus of the subcelestial arch | Sunocheus is "holder-together", one of the Synocheis, the Chaldean name for the gods of this order. Taylor's footnotes explain the word, correct a gender to agree with "arch", and gloss "interval" as the heavenly depth. |
| IV.21.p2 | the back (το νωτον) according to the intelligible (κατα το νοητον) | A play on words in the Greek: nōton (back) and noēton (intelligible) differ by one letter. |
| IV.22.p2 | Heaven the father of Saturn is the third | In the Orphic theogony there are three kings before Kronos (Phanes, Night, Ouranos); Ouranos is the third. Taylor's footnote corrects "first" to "third" in the Greek he translated. |
| IV.22.p2 | the Gods who are the sources of purity | The "undefiled" (amiantoi) gods, the Kouretes who guard the intellectual fathers, which Proclus discusses in Book V. |
| IV.23.p1 | who was truly a Bacchus | A Bacchant (bakchos), one possessed by Dionysus: Plato's Phaedo 69c, "many carry the wand, but few are Bacchants", meaning true philosophers. |
| IV.23.p2 | our fathers and grandfathers | Proclus' teachers (Syrianus) and their teachers (Iamblichus, Theodorus), in the succession of the Platonic school. Taylor's brackets supply the end of the first sentence, which is broken off in the Greek. |
| IV.24.p2 | betake themselves to entelechias | Aristotle's entelecheia, "being-at-the-end", the actual completeness of a thing (his definition of soul in On the Soul II.1); Proclus means the Peripatetics, who find perfection only in forms in their subjects. |
| IV.24.p4 | Teletarchs | "Rulers of initiation" (teletarchai), the Chaldean name for the gods of this third triad. |
| IV.27.p2 | The negation therefore says that the one is not a whole | The Parmenides 137c-d: in the first hypothesis the One "is not a whole" and "is not many"; in the second (142d, 143a) the one being is a whole and is many. Proclus' rule is that each denial in the first denies of the One what the second affirms of an order of gods. Taylor's footnote corrects a word of the Greek he translated. |
| IV.28.p1 | the third God was father and mother | In the Orphic theogony Phanes, whom Proclus sets at the third intelligible triad (the living being itself), is male and female at once. |
| IV.29.p1 | the two coordinations | The two "columns" (systoichiai) of opposites in the Pythagorean table (Aristotle, Metaphysics I.5, 986a): limit and unlimited, odd and even, one and many, and so on, the better column and the worse. Taylor's footnote supplies "brings out" in the Greek he translated. |
| IV.29.p3 | the Muses in Plato | The Republic VIII 546a-d: the Muses' riddle of the "perfect number" that holds the period of a divine generated thing, and the "geometrical number" that rules better and worse births. Taylor's footnote corrects "intellectual" to "intelligible" in the Greek he translated. |
| IV.29.p4 | there are indeed superessential numbers, but there are not superessential forms | The henads, the gods, are numbers beyond being (Proclus, Elements of Theology, props. 113-118); the Forms all belong to being. Taylor's footnote corrects "beneath the heaven" to "heavenly" in the Greek he translated. |
| IV.31.p2 | the oddly-odd, the evenly-even and the evenly-odd | The Parmenides 143e-144a: "even times even, odd times odd, even times odd, odd times even", products of two and three. Proclus maps them on limit, power and being. Taylor's footnote supplies "the evenly odd" in the Greek he translated. |
| IV.32.p1 | Plotinus admits that number is prior to animal itself | Plotinus VI.6.8-9 (On Numbers): number, "the base and place of beings", comes after being but before the living being itself, which is "a numbered living being". |
| IV.34.p1 | unknown according to a discursive energy | Taylor's "discursive energy" here is step-by-step reasoning (dianoia), unlike the "discursive energies" of the Phaedrus in IV.4. |
| IV.34.p2 | the most venerable of divine operations | Theurgy: the Chaldean rites, which used numbers (and stones, plants and names) for their hidden sympathy with the gods. Taylor's footnote corrects "each other" to "other things" in the Greek he translated. |
| IV.34.p3 | a divine generated nature | The Republic VIII 546b: "for a divine generated thing there is a period which a perfect number holds". Taylor's footnote says Plato means every body that moves in an everlasting circle, as the heavens do. |
| IV.35.p3 | those who think that all the conclusions of the second hypothesis are concerning intellect | Plotinus and Porphyry, among others, took the second hypothesis' "one that is" as Intellect, the second hypostasis, rather than the whole series of the gods. Taylor's footnote corrects "everlastingness" to "character" in the Greek he translated. |
| IV.37.p1 | But the third, as they say, to the saviour | A proverb from the Greek drinking custom: the third libation was poured to Zeus the Saviour, so "the third, to the saviour" meant "third time lucky", the last and best. Plato uses it (the Philebus 66d, the Republic IX 583b). |
| IV.39.p1 | one of the deities of the unknown triad carried in the first of the worlds | The Chaldean Oracles' worlds: the empyrean (of fire), the aethereal and the material, divided again into seven (one empyrean, three aethereal, three material). The "gods who hold together" (Synocheis) and the "rulers of initiation" (teletarchs) are Chaldean names, set by Proclus in these orders. |
| IV.39.p1 | the wing of fire | A Chaldean image: the soul's "wing of fire" that carries it up. Taylor's footnote supplies "the mixed" in the Greek he translated. |
| V.1.p3 | the marriages which are celebrated by fables | The "sacred marriages" of the myths (Kronos and Rhea, Zeus and Hera), which Proclus reads as the joining of a fatherly god with a life-giving goddess. Taylor's footnote corrects "intellectual" to "intelligible" in the Greek he translated. |
| V.2.p2 | the undefiled deities | The "undefiled" (amiantoi) or "unbending" gods, whom Proclus identifies with the Kouretes (V.3). |
| V.2.p3 | The sphere also which is there, is the intellectual order | Parmenides' being "like a well-rounded sphere" (frag. 8.43), which Proclus takes as the image of intellect; see IV.38. |
| V.2.p5 | when they denominate the heptad light according to intellect | Pythagorean names of numbers: the seven was called "motherless" and "virgin" (Athena), and was linked with light (phōs) and intellect. |
| V.3.p2 | the Saturnian bonds which Socrates mentions in the Cratylus | The Cratylus 403e-404a: Pluto binds souls with "the strongest bond", desire, and does not let them go; Socrates jokes that even Kronos is bound there. In Hesiod Zeus binds Kronos after his victory. Taylor's footnote supplies, from Portus' Latin, the missing words "one by the intelligible power of the intellectuals". |
| V.3.p3 | celebrating the armed sports of the Curetes, and their rhythmical dance | The Laws VII 796b: "the armed games of the Kouretes" in Crete. In the myth the Kouretes danced in arms round the infant Zeus, clashing their shields so that Kronos would not hear him cry; Orpheus makes them his guards. |
| V.3.p4 | the fabulous exections, both the Saturnian and the Celestial | Hesiod, Theogony 154-182: Kronos castrates Ouranos; in the Orphic poems Zeus does the same to Kronos. The Republic II 377e-378a: such tales should be told, if at all, only to a few, after a great sacrifice; the Euthyphro 5e-6a. |
| V.4.p2 | symbols of the assimilative | The "assimilative" (aphomoiōtikoi) gods, who make things like, are Proclus' name for the ruling gods above the world (Book VI); the "liberated" gods come after them. |
| V.5.p4 | the illustrious Amelius | Amelius, Plotinus' pupil, read Timaeus 39e ("intellect sees the Forms in the living being that is") as naming three intellects or three Craftsmen: one that is, one that has, and one that sees. Proclus discusses this at length in his Commentary on the Timaeus. |
| V.7.p3 | nor as the first man with us is alone earth-begotten | The Statesman 271a-b: in the age of Kronos men were born from the earth, old, and grew younger; Proclus reads the earth-born as souls raised from earthly bodies. Taylor's footnote corrects "visible" to "unseen" in the Greek he translated. |
| V.8.p1 | he is said to feed them distributedly | The Statesman 271e, 272b: the god "pastured" (enemen) men; Proclus hears in nemein both "to pasture" and "to distribute". |
| V.8.p2 | having made many and blessed discursive energies in the kingdom of Saturn | The Phaedrus' "many blessed sights and paths (diexodoi)" again (247a); see IV.4. Taylor's footnote corrects "above the heaven" to "beneath the heaven" in the Greek he translated. |
| V.9.p1 | the three kings mentioned in the Gorgias | The Gorgias 523a: "Zeus, Poseidon and Pluto divided the rule when they took it over from their father". Proclus reads the three sons of Kronos as one undivided rule shared out. |
| V.9.p2 | law is the distribution of intellect | Laws IV 714a: Plato derives nomos (law) from nous (intellect), "the distribution (dianomē) of intellect". |
| V.10.p1 | as the Barbarians say | "The barbarians" are the Chaldeans: Proclus' usual name for the Chaldean Oracles' theology. |
| V.10.p3 | the source of the unallured Gods | Taylor's "unallured" renders a Chaldean word for the undefiled gods (ameiliktoi, "unsoftened", that nothing can win over). Taylor's footnote corrects a word of the Greek he translated. |
| V.11.p1 | the immutable guard of the Gods | The Kouretes, whom Rhea produces to guard the infant Zeus (V.3). |
| V.11.p2 | he himself denominates the causes of the subsistence of all the Gods, fontal fluxions | The Cratylus 402a-d: Rhea and Kronos are "names of streams"; Homer's "Ocean, origin of the gods, and mother Tethys"; Tethys from "what leaps and filters", "the name of a spring". |
| V.11.p4 | the gift of nature | Proclus may mean grain, Demeter's gift (see the next paragraph): the Cratylus 404b derives Demeter's name from "giving as a mother" the gift of food. |
| V.11.p5 | he refers the whole of a legitimate life to the union of Ceres and Proserpine | Proclus' reading of the Laws (perhaps VI 782b, on Demeter's gifts); Plato does not name the "law-giving" goddesses there. Thesmophoros, "law-bringer", was a title of Demeter and Persephone. Taylor's footnote supplies "from" in the Greek he translated. |
