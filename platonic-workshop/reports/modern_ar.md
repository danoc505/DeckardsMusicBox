### Flags

| Paragraph | In | Older words | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cat.1.p1 | Taylor (1812) | the reason of essence according to the name is different | Taylor renders λόγος τῆς οὐσίας as "the reason of essence". The Greek means the account (or definition) of what each thing is; the modern version says "the account of their being". In the rest of the work οὐσία is the category, "substance". |
| Cat.2.p2 | Taylor (1812) | Thus science is in the soul as in a subject | Taylor's "science" is ἐπιστήμη, knowledge of any kind; here the example is grammar, which is a kind of knowledge, not a science in today's sense. |
| Cat.3.p2 | Taylor (1812) | differences arc also different in species | Taylor's "in species" renders τῷ εἴδει, which here means only "in kind": Aristotle is not yet using εἶδος in its technical sense of a species under a genus. |
| Cat.5.p1 | Taylor (1812) | nor is any subject | The Greek says "nor is in any subject" (μήτ᾿ ἐν ὑποκειμένῳ τινί ἐστιν); Taylor's English, as the scan reads it, has lost "in", and so says the opposite of what Aristotle means. |
| Cat.6.p1 | Owen (1853) | Discrete quantity is, as number and sentence | Owen's "sentence" renders λόγος, which here means spoken speech measured in long and short syllables, as Aristotle says a few lines on; not a sentence, nor "reason" or "account", as λόγος is elsewhere in the work. |
| Cat.6.p6 | Edghill, Oxford (1928) | nor is one set of three more truly three than another set | The Greek shown, Bekker's, says "nor five more five than three is three" (οὐδὲ τὰ πέντε τῶν τριῶν); Edghill's clause renders another text of the Greek, not this one. |
| Cat.7.p1 | Edghill, Oxford (1928) | being either said to be of something else or related to something else | Edghill's "of something else" is literal. The Greek genitive can be "of", "than" or "relative to", as in "larger than something" and "double of something"; the modern version uses whichever English the example needs. |
| Cat.8.p1 | Taylor (1812) | But quality is among the number of things which are multifariously multifiiri | The scan of Taylor's page doubles a word ("multifariously multifiiri"); the Greek says only that quality is "spoken of in several ways". |
| Cat.8.p3 | Taylor (1812) | Similarly to these also passive qualities and passions are denominated in the soul | Taylor's "passive qualities" and "passions" are literal; Aristotle explains that sweetness is called παθητική because it produces an affection in the senses, not because it is passive, and the modern version says "affective". |
| Cat.9.p2 | Taylor (1812) | Since there are ten genera, four of which have been considered | These words are not Aristotle's: they begin Taylor's own note on the chapter, which the scan of his page reads as part of the text. |
| Cat.10.p4 | Taylor (1812) | For the sight is habit, but blindness privation | Taylor's "habit" (as Edghill's "positive") renders ἕξις, which here is the having of a natural capacity, such as sight; the modern version says "possession". In chapter 8 the same word is a lasting "state", such as a virtue. |
| Cat.14.p1 | Taylor (1812) | Of motion there are six species | Taylor's "motion" for κίνησις; Aristotle means change of every kind, of which motion from place to place is only one. |

### Flags

| Paragraph | In | Older words | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Int.1.p1 | Taylor (1812) | symbols of the passions of the soul | Taylor's "passions" renders παθήματα, what the soul undergoes or experiences: the impressions and thoughts that things produce in it. Today "passions" suggests strong emotions, which is not what Aristotle means. |
| Int.2.p1 | Taylor (1812) | a sound significant from compact | Taylor's "from compact" renders κατὰ συνθήκην, "by agreement" or "by convention": a word means what it does because people have settled on it, not by nature. |
| Int.2.p1 | Taylor (1812) | For illiterate sounds also signify something | Taylor's "illiterate" renders ἀγράμματοι, sounds that cannot be written in letters: inarticulate noises like the cries of animals. It has nothing to do with being unable to read. |
| Int.3.p2 | Edghill, Oxford (1928) | Verbs in and by themselves are substantival and have significance | Edghill's "substantival" renders ὀνόματά ἐστι, "are nouns" (or "names"). Aristotle's point is simpler than a grammar term: a verb said alone, like "walks", names something and fixes the mind on it, as a noun does. |
| Int.4.p2 | Edghill, Oxford (1928) | not as being the natural means by which a physical faculty is realized | Edghill expands three Greek words, οὐχ ὡς ὄργανον, "not as a tool (or instrument)": speech does not signify by nature, as the eye sees, but by convention. Taylor has "not as an instrument". |
| Int.6.p1 | Taylor (1812) | to enunciate that which is inherent as if it were not inherent | Taylor's "inherent" renders ὑπάρχον, what "belongs" to or holds of a thing. Aristotle means only that a predicate holds or does not hold; "inherent" suggests something lodged inside the thing. |
| Int.9.p2 | Edghill, Oxford (1928) | there are no real alternatives | Edghill's phrase renders ὁπότερ᾿ ἔτυχεν, "whichever way it happens", what can turn out either way. "Real alternatives" is Edghill's gloss; the modern version keeps closer to the Greek with "as it happens". |
| Int.9.p6 | Edghill, Oxford (1928) | but it cannot be either actually true or actually false | Edghill's "actually" brings in the language of actuality, which the Greek does not use here. The Greek says οὐ μέντοι ἤδη ἀληθῆ ἢ ψευδῆ, "but not yet (or not already) true or false", as Owen has it: "yet not already true nor false". |
| Int.10.p1 | Edghill, Oxford (1928) | ‘every man is’, ‘every man is not’ | Edghill's "every man is not" renders οὐκ ἔστι πᾶς ἄνθρωπος. In today's English "every man is not" sounds like "no man is", the contrary; the Greek denial is the contradictory, "not every man is", as Aristotle explains in chapter 7. |
| Int.13.p6 | Edghill, Oxford (1928) | Some things are actualities without potentiality, namely, the primary substances | "Primary substances" are, in the Categories (chapter 5), individual things like this man or this horse. Here αἱ πρῶται οὐσίαι are the first substances, the eternal and unchanging movers of Metaphysics XII, which are always actual. Taylor's "the first essences" keeps them apart. |
| Int.13.p6 | Taylor (1812) | are energies without capacity | Taylor's "energies" and "capacity" render ἐνέργεια and δύναμις, actuality and potentiality. "Energy" today means a physical quantity, which is not Aristotle's sense; the modern version says "actuality" and "potentiality". |

### Flags

| Paragraph | In | Older words | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| APr.I.1.p1 | Jenkinson, Oxford (1928) | the faculty that carries it out demonstrative science | Jenkinson's "faculty" is his own word: the Greek says only that the inquiry is "of" (τίνος) demonstrative knowledge, that is, it belongs to that science. The modern version keeps the plainer sense. |
| APr.I.1.p2 | Jenkinson, Oxford (1928) | I call that a perfect syllogism which needs nothing | Jenkinson's (and Owen's) "perfect" renders τέλειος, "complete": a syllogism whose necessity is plain from the premises alone. An "imperfect" syllogism is not faulty; it is valid, but needs a further step (a conversion, or a reduction to the impossible) to show it. |
| APr.I.3.p2 | Jenkinson, Oxford (1928) | and it is in this way we define the possible | Jenkinson's sentence is right, but it needs the definition it points to: in chapter 13 Aristotle defines the possible (τὸ ἐνδεχόμενον) as what is not necessary but, if supposed to be, involves nothing impossible. So "possible" in these chapters is two-sided: what may be and may also not be. |
| APr.I.5.p1 | Taylor (1812) | of not being present with, essence, animal, stone | Taylor's "essence" renders οὐσία, which in Aristotle's logic is "substance", the first of the categories; and his "being present with" renders ὑπάρχειν, "belonging to". Neither is wrong in Taylor's Platonist English, but today both mislead: "essence" now means a thing's nature, and "present with" sounds like being nearby. |
| APr.I.6.p1 | Jenkinson, Oxford (1928) | It is possible to demonstrate this also per tmpossibele and by exposition | Jenkinson's "exposition" renders ἔκθεσις, "setting out": picking out one particular instance of the middle term (here, one of the Ss) and seeing that both extremes belong to it. It is a proof by example, not an explanation, which is what "exposition" now suggests. |
| APr.I.13.p1 | Jenkinson, Oxford (1928) | that which is not necessary but, being assumed, results in nothing impossible | Jenkinson's sentence is close to the Greek, but "being assumed" leaves out what is assumed: that the thing holds (τεθέντος δʼ ὑπάρχειν). The test is to suppose that the possible is actually so, and see whether anything impossible follows. |
| APr.I.13.p2 | Jenkinson, Oxford (1928) | In another sense the expression means the indefinite | Jenkinson's "the indefinite" renders τὸ ἀόριστον, "the undetermined": what is not fixed either way, like chance events. In these chapters "indefinite" also renders a different word, ἀδιόριστος, a premise that does not say "all" or "some"; the modern version keeps them apart. |
| APr.I.14.p3 | Jenkinson, Oxford (1928) | so that as predicates they cover unequal areas | Jenkinson's "areas" is a picture of his own; the Greek says only that B need not be "predicated on equal terms" (ἐπ᾿ ἴσων), that is, of the same range of things as A. |
| APr.I.31.p1 | Taylor (1812) | For division is as it were an imbecile syllogism | Taylor's "imbecile" renders ἀσθενής, "weak" or "feeble". In Taylor's day "imbecile" could mean simply "weak"; today it is an insult, and Aristotle is making a logical point: division assumes the very step it should prove. |
| APr.I.31.p1 | Taylor (1812) | either a pedestrious or biped animal | Taylor's "pedestrious" renders ὑπόπουν, "having feet", "footed"; and in this sentence his "biped" stands where the Greek has ἄπουν, "footless", the other side of the division. The modern version says "footed or footless". |
| APr.I.31.p2 | Jenkinson, Oxford (1928) | division into classes | Jenkinson's "classes" renders γένη, "genera" (kinds), the word for the higher kinds of the Categories and of Porphyry's tree. The division meant is the cutting of a genus into its species. |
| APr.I.46.p3 | Jenkinson, Oxford (1928) | the proper negation is ‘every animal is not white’ | Jenkinson's "every animal is not white" renders οὐκ ἔστι πᾶν ζῷον λευκόν. In today's English "every animal is not white" sounds like "no animal is white", which is false; the Greek denial is the contradictory, "not every animal is white", as the modern version has it. The same holds of his "all are not white". |
| APr.II.14.p1 | Taylor (1812) | a demonstration leading to the impossible differs from an ostensive demonstration | Taylor and Jenkinson both say "ostensive" for δεικτική, a word no modern reader uses; this version says "direct proof", the term used in logic textbooks today. |
| APr.II.16.p1 | Taylor (1812) | begging the question from the beginning | "Begging the question" is the old Latin-based name (petitio principii) and is still Jenkinson's; today it is often heard as "raising the question". The Greek says "asking for what was at the start", so this version says "assuming the point at issue". |
| APr.II.17.p1 | Jenkinson, Oxford (1928) | he will not say, ‘False cause’ | Jenkinson renders the objection as "False cause", the name it later had in logic manuals; the Greek is the plain phrase "not because of this", which this version keeps. |
| APr.II.20.p1 | Taylor (1812) | when there will not be an elenchus | Taylor keeps the Greek word ἔλεγχος as "elenchus"; in this chapter it means simply a refutation of what the answerer has granted, so this version says "refutation". |
| APr.II.25.p1 | Taylor (1812) | Abduction, however, is, when it is evident that the first | Taylor's "abduction" renders ἀπαγωγή, "leading away"; since Peirce, "abduction" names inference to the best explanation, which is something else. This version says "reduction", as Jenkinson does. |
| APr.II.27.p1 | Taylor (1812) | The consentaneous, however, and a sign, are not the same | Taylor's "consentaneous" for εἰκός, "what is likely", is a word now unknown; Jenkinson says "probability", and this version says "likelihood", which keeps it apart from mathematical probability. |
