### Flags

| Paragraph | In | Older words | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cat.1.p1 | Taylor (1812) | the reason of essence according to the name is different | Taylor renders λόγος τῆς οὐσίας as "the reason of essence". The Greek means the account (or definition) of what each thing is; the modern version says "the account of their being". In the rest of the work οὐσία is the category, "substance". |
| Cat.2.p2 | Taylor (1812) | Thus science is in the soul as in a subject | Taylor's "science" is ἐπιστήμη, knowledge of any kind; here the example is grammar, which is a kind of knowledge, not a science in today's sense. |
| Cat.3.p2 | Taylor (1812) | differences arc also different in species | Taylor's "in species" renders τῷ εἴδει, which here means only "in kind": Aristotle is not yet using εἶδος in its technical sense of a species under a genus. |
| Cat.5.p1 | Taylor (1812) | nor is any subject | The Greek says "nor is in any subject" (μήτ᾿ ἐν ὑποκειμένῳ τινί ἐστιν); Taylor's English, as the scan reads it, has lost "in", and so says the opposite of what Aristotle means. |
| Cat.6.p1 | Owen (1853) | Discrete quantity is, as number and sentence | Owen's "sentence" renders λόγος, which here means spoken speech measured in long and short syllables, as Aristotle says a few lines on; not a sentence, nor "reason" or "account", as λόγος is elsewhere in the work. |
| Cat.6.p6 | Edghill, Oxford (1928) | nor is one set of three more truly three than another set | The Greek shown, Bekker's, says "nor five more five than three is three" (οὐδὲ τὰ πέντε τῶν τριῶν); Edghill's clause renders another text of the Greek, not this one. |
| Cat.7.p1 | Edghill, Oxford (1928) | being either said to be of something else or related to something else | Edghill's "of something else" is literal. The Greek genitive can be "of", "than" or "relative to", as in "larger than something" and "double of something"; the modern version uses whichever English the example needs. |
| Cat.8.p1 | Taylor (1812) | But quality is among the number of things which are multifariously multifiiri | The scan of Taylor's page doubles a word ("multifariously multifiiri"); the Greek says only that quality is "spoken of in several ways". |
| Cat.8.p3 | Taylor (1812) | Similarly to these also passive qualities and passions are denominated in the soul | Taylor's "passive qualities" and "passions" are literal; Aristotle explains that sweetness is called παθητική because it produces an affection in the senses, not because it is passive, and the modern version says "affective". |
| Cat.9.p2 | Taylor (1812) | Since there are ten genera, four of which have been considered | These words are not Aristotle's: they begin Taylor's own note on the chapter, which the scan of his page reads as part of the text. |
| Cat.10.p4 | Taylor (1812) | For the sight is habit, but blindness privation | Taylor's "habit" (as Edghill's "positive") renders ἕξις, which here is the having of a natural capacity, such as sight; the modern version says "possession". In chapter 8 the same word is a lasting "state", such as a virtue. |
| Cat.14.p1 | Taylor (1812) | Of motion there are six species | Taylor's "motion" for κίνησις; Aristotle means change of every kind, of which motion from place to place is only one. |
