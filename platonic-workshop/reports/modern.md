### Flags

| Paragraph | Taylor's words | Why |
|---|---|---|
| p0021 | Prior to the one, therefore, is that which is simply and perfectly ineffable | Damascius' view that there is something "beyond" even the One is his own, and the later Platonists did not all accept it; Taylor follows him here. |
| p0023 | the immediate offspring of the one cannot be any thing else than unities | That the "unities" (henads) are the gods is the teaching of Iamblichus and Proclus, read back into Plato; Plato does not say so in these words. |
| p0027 | as cited by Kercher (in Oedip. Egyptiac.) | Taylor takes these lines from Athanasius Kircher, a seventeenth-century source; their claim to be by Pherecydes is very doubtful. |
| p0032 | and links together all intelligent nature | Taylor's quotation adds to Plato. Symposium 202e says only that the daemonic fills the space between gods and humans, "so that the whole is bound together with itself." |
| p0036 | a deity of a fabricative characteristic, whom Plato calls Jupiter | Taylor uses the Latin names of the gods (Jupiter for Zeus) throughout. The modern version uses the Greek names. |
| p0045 | restrain your intellect (i.e. even cease to energize intellectually) | Homer's words (kata son noon ischane) mean "keep it to yourself" or "hold it in your mind"; Taylor reads noos as intellect in the Platonists' sense. |
| p0047 | which doubtless gave birth to the Christian | This is Taylor's own opinion, stated without evidence; historians do not accept that the Christian Trinity came from a Platonic trinity in this way. |
| p0048 | the three fabricators, and their demiurgic allotment | This is Proclus' reading of the myth at Gorgias 523a, where Zeus, Poseidon and Pluto divide the rule of the world; Plato does not call them "makers." |
| p0054 | and hence Plato says | Taylor is following Olympiodorus' commentary on the Gorgias here; Plato says no such thing in these words, though "bound with chains of iron and adamant" is from Gorgias 509a. |
| p0058 | For all the Grecian theology is the progeny of the mystic doctrine of Orpheus | This is Proclus' view of the history of Greek religion, which modern historians do not share; the story of Pythagoras and Aglaophamus comes from Iamblichus. |
| p0058 | the Parmenides, and the mystic conceptions of this dialogue, will accomplish all you desire | That the Parmenides is a theology of the gods is the reading of Iamblichus, Syrianus and Proclus. Many readers, ancient and modern, take it as a logical exercise. |
| p0060 | But dianoëtic forms or ideas imitate the intellectual | Taylor's text runs together intelligible, intellectual and "dianoetic" Forms, following Syrianus; the three ranks are the later Platonists' and are not set out in Plato. |
| p0063 | Plutarch, Atticus, and Democritus | The "Democritus" here is a Platonist of the third century AD, not the atomist; Taylor does not say so. |
| p0066 | παιτ?? | The Greek of this line is damaged in the copy the Workshop has; the word should be πάντων ("of all"). |
| p0068 | must be consubsistent with its cause, or, in other words, must be a perpetual emanation from it | Whether Plato's Timaeus means that the world had no beginning in time was disputed in antiquity. Proclus and the later Platonists said it had none; Aristotle, Plutarch and Atticus read Plato as saying it had one. |
| p0073 | he who rushes from the forge, or the mines, with a soul distorted, crushed and bruised by base mechanical arts | This is Taylor's attack on the working-class lay preachers of his own day; it is his opinion, not Plato's, and it is kept here only because the modern version leaves nothing out. |
| p0074 | he is endued with an intellect subsisting in energy | Taylor here speaks of an intellect in us that is "always active"; in the next paragraph he follows the later Platonists, who said that our intellect is not always active and needs a higher intellect to perfect it. |
| p0075 | which in his Sophista he defines to be that power which knows the conclusion of dianoia | Sophist 264a defines opinion as the completion of thinking (dianoia), "when the soul silently affirms or denies"; "knows the conclusion" is the later Platonists' reading. |
| p0078 | two vehicles, according to Plato, are necessary as media | The teaching of the soul's "vehicles" belongs to the later Platonists (Proclus, Hermias); Plato only hints at it, for example in the soul's "vehicle" at Timaeus 41e. |
| p0081 | hence it possesses a power of descending infinitely into generation | That every human soul comes down and goes up again for ever is the teaching of Proclus; Iamblichus and Plotinus held different views, and Plato's own words (Phaedrus 248e-249a) speak of cycles of ten thousand years. |
| p0082 | but the shadows formed by the fire falling on the opposite part of the cave | Taylor's summary moves the entrance: in Plato (Republic 514a-515b) the prisoners face the back wall, and the entrance and the fire are behind them. The modern version follows Plato. |
| p0093 | in conjunction with intellectual prudence | Theaetetus 176b has "with wisdom" (meta phronēseōs); Taylor's "intellectual" is his own addition. |
| p0096 | we know all things as in a dream, and are again ignorant of them, according to vigilant perception | Taylor credits this to the Sophist, but the words are from the Statesman (277d). |
| p0099 | the same with what Aristotle calls the first philosophy and wisdom | That Plato's dialectic is Aristotle's "first philosophy" is Taylor's view, following the later Platonists; Aristotle himself sets dialectic below first philosophy. |
| p0101 | to the beauty in laws; from the beauty in laws to the ample sea of beauty | Taylor follows Olympiodorus' list; in the Symposium (210a-d) the order is bodies, souls, practices and laws, kinds of knowledge, then the great sea of beauty. |
| p0106 | Hence Fate is nature | Taylor is following Proclus' treatise On Providence and Fate here; Plato himself does not say that fate is nature. |
| p0117 | a rational elective power, desiderative of true and apparent good | This account of free choice (proairesis) comes from Proclus, On Providence and Fate; Plato has no single word for it. |
| p0118 | they have been unfrequented from the reign of the emperor Justinian to the present time | Justinian is said to have closed the Platonic school at Athens in 529; Taylor passes over the Platonists of Byzantium and Islam (Psellus, al-Farabi), and below he dismisses those of the Renaissance; all of them did walk these paths. |
| p0119 | those who consider experiment as the only solid criterion of truth | Taylor's attack on experimental science (here to p0125) is his own, written against the science of his day; the ancient Platonists had no such opponents in view. |
| p0125 | Eudoxus, Archimedes and Euclid | Some of those Taylor lists (Archimedes, Demosthenes, Virgil) cannot be shown to have been followers of Plato; the list is his own claim. |
| p0128 | The depth of this philosophy then does not appear to have been perfectly penetrated | That Plotinus and his successors, and not the earlier Platonists, understood Plato truly is the heart of Taylor's position, and the Workshop's own preference for the ancient Platonists; modern scholars mostly hold the opposite view. |
| p0132 | Ficinus, Picus, Dr. Henry Moore, and other pseudo Platonists | This is Taylor's own harsh judgment; Ficino in particular translated and spread Plato and Plotinus across Europe, and Taylor himself relied on his Latin. |
| p0133 | Was it when the fierce champions for the trinity fled from Galilee to the groves of Academus | Taylor's hostility to Christianity is his own opinion, not Plato's or the Workshop's. |
| p0135 | they had books to consult, written by the immediate disciples of Plato | It is true that the later Platonists had books now lost; but they quote the earliest philosophers mostly at second hand, and some of the "Pythagorean writings" they trusted were forgeries of later centuries. |
| p0161 | no accurate and scientific arrangement then of these dialogues has been transmitted to us from the ancients | Taylor overlooks the order of Iamblichus' curriculum, which the Anonymous Prolegomena (26) hands down; the Workshop lists the dialogues in that order. |
| p0162 | which are in every respect worthy that prince of all true philosophers | Most modern scholars think that most or all of the Letters are not by Plato; the ancient Platonists accepted them. |
| p0163 | the number of the legitimate dialogues of Plato is fifty-five | Taylor's count follows the ancient Thrasyllan list; many of the works he counts (the Second Alcibiades, the Lovers, the Theages, the Minos) are now thought not to be by Plato. |
| p0166 | have divided long periods into a number of short ones | The Workshop's modern version does just what Taylor refused to do here, breaking his long sentences into short ones; his own words are always one tap away. |
| p0170 | polymathy does not teach intellect | Heraclitus' word noos here means "understanding" or "sense"; "intellect" is Taylor's Platonist rendering, kept for the sake of the key term. |
| p0172 | It is well said indeed by Lysis, the Pythagorean | The "Letter of Lysis to Hipparchus" that Taylor quotes is a later Pythagorean writing, not by the early Pythagorean Lysis. |

### Flags

| Paragraph | Taylor's words | Why |
|---|---|---|
| p0001 | Hence with primitive zeal he wrote twelve books of Laws, and eleven books on a Republic | Taylor has "eleven books"; the Greek, as the scan shows it, has a number that is hard to read, and the Republic has ten books. |
| p0001 | since he was by no means an obscure man | The Greek plays on Homer: Plato was "no Nobody" (Outis, the name Odysseus gave the Cyclops); Taylor's "obscure man" loses the joke. |
| p0005 | But Plato calls himself a fellow-servant with swans | The phrase is from Phaedo 85b, where Socrates says it of himself; Olympiodorus applies it to Plato. |
| p0006 | as his instructor in gymnastic | The Greek says a trainer in wrestling (gymnastēs); Taylor's "gymnastic" is close enough. |
| p0007 | Draco, the son of Damon | The Greek says the pupil (mathētēs) of Damon, not his son. Plato mentions Damon in the Republic (400b), not Draco. |
| p0009 | With their late offspring parents seem to mix | The line is hard: the scan reads it with a word that is probably corrupt. The Workshop takes it to mean that parents are called by the names of their children, which fits the point being made; Taylor's line is a guess too. |
| p0010 | is said to have been the first dialogue which Plato composed | That the Phaedrus was Plato's first dialogue is an ancient story (also in Diogenes Laertius 3.38); modern scholars place it among his later middle works. |
| p0013 | in the midst of his oration seized with a hiccup | In the Symposium (185c) Aristophanes has the hiccups before his speech, and so speaks out of turn; he is not stopped halfway. |
| p0015 | Anatolius the grammarian, once reciting this verse, very much pleased Vulcan | Hermann prints this story in square brackets as a later addition to the text. "Hephaestus" here is the name of a governor of Alexandria, and "Pharos" is Alexandria's island; Taylor's "Vulcan" hides the pun. |
| p0017 | sitting on his bosom | The Greek has "on his knees" (en tois gonasin). |
| p0018 | he again went into Italy | The Greek also says "again"; but Olympiodorus has not told of a first visit, and the order of Plato's journeys is not certain. |
| p0019 | he ordered him to depart at sunrise | The Greek says "while the sun is above the earth," that is, before sunset that day. Taylor also leaves out the last sentence, that Plato was driven out in disgrace. |
| p0020 | Pollidis of Ægina, a Sicilian merchant | The Greek names him Pollis (Pollidi is the Greek dative), a man of Aegina trading with Sicily. Other ancient writers tell the slave story of Plato's first journey, under the elder Dionysius. |
| p0021 | was at length cast into prison | In Plato's Seventh Letter Dion is in exile, not in prison, when Plato makes his third journey; Olympiodorus' story differs. |
| p0022 | he was instructed by them in magic | The Greek word (magikē) means the lore of the Magi, the Persian priests; the Workshop says "lore" because "magic" in modern English suggests tricks or sorcery. |
| p0023 | preparing men and also women in a virile habit to be his auditors | The Greek can mean that he had the women come dressed as men. Diogenes Laertius (3.46) names two such women, Axiothea and Lastheneia. |
| p0024 | According to the Socratic Simmias | The same dream, read by Simmias, is told in the Anonymous Prolegomena (1); there is no early evidence for it, and nothing is known of Simmias so late in life. |
| p0024 | When he was near his death | Taylor leaves out the first sentence, that Plato won many devoted followers and did good to very many. |
| p0025 | From great Apollo Pæon sprung | The Greek names Asclepius, the god of healing, where Taylor has Paeon. Read strictly, "the one ... the other" follows the order of the names and would make Asclepius the healer of souls; the sense intended is plainly the reverse, as Taylor gives it. |
