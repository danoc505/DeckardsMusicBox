## Plotinus in modern English

- Made from: It is translated from the Greek of Volkmann's edition (Leipzig, 1883-1884), as First1KGreek gives it, chapter by chapter, and checked against Thomas Taylor's translation of 1817.
- Treatises: I.2 On the virtues, I.3 On dialectic, II.4 On matter, II.9 Against the Gnostics.
- Chapters: 47, every one of the treatises', each with a short title and a summary.
- Paragraphs: 108, each with its Greek.
- Words: 19,461 modern, 14,678 Greek, 28,650 in Taylor's English of the same chapters.
- Sentences (each can be marked in the reader): 878.
- Flags (where Taylor and the Greek differ, or the Greek is hard): 82.
- Problems found: 1.

### Problems

- plotinus II.9.16.p3: the Greek has 'ομοιωσ', the modern version none of ['like']

### Flags

| Paragraph | Taylor's words | Why |
|---|---|---|
| I.2.1.p1 | and to the intellect which is the leader in it | The Greek says only "the ruling power in it"; Taylor names it intellect, as the later Platonists did. |
| I.2.1.p2 | For rumor also says, that these are divine | The Greek says that tradition calls these men divine, that is, the people who have the civic virtues; Taylor makes it the virtues. |
| I.2.1.p3 | but that virtue in him is in reality greater than virtue [because it subsists causally.] | Taylor's words in square brackets are his own explanation, from the later Platonists; they are not in the Greek. |
| I.2.2.p2 | and taking away false opinions from a more excellent nature | The Greek says the virtues remove false opinions "by what is altogether better", the means by which they work; it does not say "from a more excellent nature". |
| I.2.2.p2 | Perhaps, too, these measures are in soul as in matter | There is no "perhaps" in the Greek here: the virtues are measures in the soul as in matter. |
| I.2.3.p3 | is divided when compared with it | Taylor has "divided" twice here; Volkmann's Greek has μεμιμημένος, "imitated", and the modern version follows the Greek shown. |
| I.2.4.p1 | if the soul was good prior to her impurity | The Greek says "before the purification", not "before her impurity". |
| I.2.4.p2 | She did not, therefore, possess these, nor recollect them | The Greek is a question ("Did it not have them, then, and does it not recollect?"), which the next sentence answers; Taylor makes it a statement. |
| I.2.4.p2 | when it looks to the illuminating cause | The Greek says "when it looks towards it", that is, when the soul looks to intellect; "the illuminating cause" is Taylor's. |
| I.2.5.p2 | And as to the desire of meats and drinks for the sake of a remission of pain | The Greek has "for relaxation" (anesis); "of pain" is Taylor's addition. |
| I.2.6.p1 | but a correction of the man | The Greek word (katorthōsis) means right action, doing well; it is not "correction". |
| I.2.6.p2 | But coming hither, he will associate with intellect ; and will assimilate this to himself | A hard sentence. Volkmann's Greek has "with intellect" (νῷ), as Taylor does; read so, it is not clear what "this" is that the man makes like himself. From what follows ("not struck", "its master") it seems to be the lower part he lives with here. The modern version keeps the Greek shown and marks "this". |
| I.2.6.p3 | But he will possess intellect by contact | The Greek says that intellect has what it holds by direct contact; it does not say that the man possesses intellect. |
| I.2.6.p4 | Hence justice in the soul is to energize in a greater degree intellectually | The Greek says that the greater justice of the soul is its activity towards intellect; "greater" belongs to the justice, not to the activity. |
| I.2.7.p1 | and that which is as it were fortitude is immateriality | Volkmann's Greek has ταυτότης, "sameness"; Taylor has "immateriality", which is not what the Greek shown says. The modern version follows the Greek. |
| I.2.7.p2 | For if it uses the other virtues | The Greek says "if it is going to use other principles" (archai); Taylor has "the other virtues". |
| I.3.1.p3 | yet is not disposed to be moved from himself | The Greek says he is "less able to be moved from within himself", that is, he needs something outside to move him; Taylor's words come near this. |
| I.3.4.p2 | and again, by an analytic process it arrives at that to which it had proceeded from the first | The Greek says that it takes things apart again "until it comes to the principle" (archē), the first principle; Taylor's words do not say this. |
| I.3.6.p1 | The rational virtues also have habits, and what are now as peculiarities, which they derive from thence | A hard sentence in the Greek. The Workshop takes it to mean that the habits of reason hold what comes from dialectic as their own already, while most of the moral habits come with matter; Taylor's English is a guess too. |
| I.3.6.p2 | But whether is it possible to know | The Greek asks whether the lower virtues can exist without dialectic and wisdom; "to know" is not in it. |
| I.3.6.p2 | It is possible, however, for a man to be thus wise and skilled in dialectic without a knowledge of these | The Greek is a question ("Is it possible to be wise and a dialectician without these?"), and the answer is no; Taylor makes it a statement. |
| II.4.1.p1 | They, likewise, give a body to matter, calling it, body mid of quality | Taylor's "body mid of quality" is the scan's misreading of "body void of quality"; the Greek has a body without quality (apoion sōma). |
| II.4.2.p1 | and first natures will have a casual subsistence | "Casual" here means by chance (kata syntychian): if matter is eternal beside the first principles, the first things are together by accident. |
| II.4.3.p1 | through nature which has an energy directed to form | The Greek says the rational principles make composite "the nature that is active towards form"; Taylor's "through nature" makes nature the means. |
| II.4.4.p1 | Hence, if by intellect you take away its variety | The Greek "τῷ νῷ" can mean "in thought" (in your mind) or "from intellect"; Taylor takes it as "by intellect". The modern version reads it as "in your mind", the usual sense. |
| II.4.5.p1 | But so far as it is able, it proceeds into the profundity of body. say that matter is essence | Taylor's chapter, as the scan gives it, leaves out a passage here: the Greek's "the depth of each thing is matter", the dark beneath the colours, and the two kinds of matter, before "those who say that matter is substance". |
| II.4.5.p3 | does not always possess it | The Greek says that what receives the light "does not have true light" before it receives it; "always" is not there. |
| II.4.6.p1 | as if, for instance, a pot should be resolved into gold ; 1 but gold into water | The Greek has a bowl (phialē); the gold resolved into water is an old view of metals, which Plotinus uses only as an example. |
| II.4.7.p2 | the demiurgus | Taylor keeps the Greek word, the "demiurge" or craftsman of the world; the modern version calls him the Maker. |
| II.4.8.p1 | we must not attribute to the from this, and which is the nature of sensibles | Taylor's chapter, as the scan gives it, has one of his long footnotes printed in the middle of the text here ("matter is always truly the last sediment ... my translation of Aristotle's Physics"). It is Taylor's, from the later Platonists, not Plotinus. |
| II.4.8.p2 | And it would be absurd to suppose, that the introduction of quantity to the matter of a certain bird, is any thing else than adding to it its proper quality | The Greek says it is "no more remarkable" to bring quantity to matter than to add quality to it; Taylor makes it a different claim about one bird. |
| II.4.10.p1 | and wishing to become intelligence, is not intelligence, but, as it were, a privation of intellect | The Greek says that what "would like to be" thinking is not thinking, but rather a kind of thoughtlessness (anoia); Taylor's words are near this, but make reason the one that wishes. |
| II.4.10.p2 | Or does the indefinite consist in a certain negation 1 in conjunction with a certain affirmation | The Greek asks only whether the unbounded lies "in something positive" (en kataphasei tini); "a certain negation in conjunction with" is Taylor's addition. |
| II.4.10.p3 | and sees intellectually, not understanding | The Greek has "it thinks by not thinking" (noei ou nousa); Taylor turns the first verb into "sees". |
| II.4.11.p3 | because, as being the first matter, it is an aptitude to the reception of it | The Greek says matter has a phantom of bulk "as a first fitness for it"; it does not say "as being the first matter". |
| II.4.12.p1 | But forms are conversant with soul, and therefore are not bodies | The Greek says that rational principles alone are in soul, and so there would be no bodies; Taylor makes "forms" the subject. |
| II.4.12.p2 | for it has neither moisture, nor vapour | The Greek says only that matter has no flavours (chymoi), so that neither nose nor tongue grasps it; "vapour" is Taylor's. |
| II.4.13.p2 | For privation is a negation | The Greek word (arsis) means a taking away, a removal; Taylor's "negation" is near, but not the word. |
| II.4.15.p1 | For in the first place, that which happens to any thing ought to be formative ; but the infinite is not formative | The Greek says that what belongs to a thing must be a rational principle (logos), and the infinite is not one; "formative" is Taylor's gloss. |
| II.4.15.p2 | Hence the infinite which is in intelligible^, in consequence of having more of being, is but as an image [with respect to the infinity of matter.] | Taylor's words in square brackets are his own; and his chapter, as the scan gives it, has another of his footnotes (from Proclus on the Timaeus) printed into the text after this. |
| II.4.16.p1 | Perhaps it is not the same with difference simply considered,1 but with a part of difference | Taylor's "simply considered" is his own; the Greek says only "not the same, but a part of Otherness". |
| II.4.16.p2 | but it is the want of wisdom, and the want of virtue, of beauty, strength, morphe, form, and quality | Taylor adds "strength" a second time to the list; the Greek has wisdom, virtue, beauty, shape, form and quality. |
| II.9.1.p2 | For they [the Gnostics] will not say 1 that there is one principle in capacity, but another in energy | The Gnostics are not named in the Greek here; Taylor's square brackets say whom Plotinus means. "Capacity" and "energy" are Taylor's words for potency and act (dynamis, energeia). |
| II.9.1.p3 | if we are moderately worthy, it becomes the cause to us of folly | The Greek says that if this happened to us, who are at least moderately serious people, we would be charged with folly; Taylor's "it becomes the cause to us of folly" loses the charge. |
| II.9.2.p1 | For the more it looks to itself | The Greek says "the more it is towards that", what comes before it (pros autēi, read by the editors as towards the intelligible); Taylor's "looks to itself" says the opposite. |
| II.9.3.p1 | and should be generated by other things, because they depend on others for their subsistence | The Greek says only that the others are "made" (genēta) in that they come from others; Taylor's "generated" suggests a beginning in time, which Plotinus here denies. |
| II.9.4.p1 | For it does not possess nor see them obscurely, if it is without this tendency | The Greek says that even if the soul holds the things there dimly, it would not then sink further and see them more dimly still; Taylor's sentence says something else. |
| II.9.4.p2 | For this arises from forming too exalted an opinion of this sensible world | Taylor's words are close; the Greek says that those who find fault claim too high a worth for the world, asking it to be the intelligible world and not its image. |
| II.9.5.p2 | was it in order that souls might be saved | The Greek verb (phylaxōntai) means that souls might be on their guard, warned by the pattern; Taylor's "saved" gives it a Christian sense the Greek does not have. |
| II.9.6.p1 | such as transmigrations, repercussions, and repentances | The Greek words are Gnostic terms: paroikēseis "sojourns" (in the world as in a foreign land), antitypoi "antitypes" (copies), metanoiai "repentances". "Transmigrations" and "repercussions" are Taylor's guesses. |
| II.9.6.p2 | understood by the discursive energy of reason | The Greek verb (dienoēthē) here means simply that the maker "thought" or "intended"; the Gnostics made it a third, reasoning intellect, and Taylor's "discursive energy" leans their way. |
| II.9.6.p4 | hence in their oppositions to them, they introduce manifold generations and corruptions | The Greek says the Gnostics bring in "total" (panteleis) comings to be and perishings, of the whole world; not "manifold". |
| II.9.7.p1 | That this world, therefore, never began, nor will ever cease to be | This is the start of the Greek's chapter 7; Taylor runs chapters 6, 7 and 8 together as one. |
| II.9.7.p2 | but the latter being decayed, should permit the former to have its own life | The Greek says the graft, if it withers, leaves the stock its own life; Taylor reverses the two, making the stock decay. |
| II.9.7.p2 | And if in each of the remaining elements there should be a certain world | The Greek says "if there could be a world made of the remaining elements"; not a world in each element. |
| II.9.8.p1 | To enquire, however, why the world was made, is the same thing as to ask why soul is | This is the start of the Greek's chapter 8, in Taylor's long chapter 6. |
| II.9.8.p2 | so that this power perpetually 1 fabricates | Taylor adds "perpetually" from Ficino's Latin, as his footnote says; Volkmann's Greek says only "and so it worked". |
| II.9.8.p2 | If, however, there is another [sensible] world better than this, what is it | Volkmann's Greek has "not" here (οὐκ), which makes no sense in the question; Taylor, and the modern version, read it without the "not". |
| II.9.9.p1 | And if you kill another [instead of being killed yourself,] you have what you wish | The Greek says "if he kills you, you have what you want": death frees the soul from the world it blames. Taylor makes the man who is wronged the killer. |
| II.9.9.p1 | If, however, you still blame the administration of things, there is no necessity for you to continue any longer in life | The Greek says "if you find fault with being a citizen, you are not compelled to be one": not "to continue in life". |
| II.9.9.p3 | For there is much arrogance among men [of the present time.] | Taylor's words in square brackets are his own; the Greek speaks of men in general. |
| II.9.9.p4 | Divinity, however, knows the order of the wrorld | The Greek has an objection and an answer: "But they have no need of him." "Yet the world needs him, and knows its order." It is the world that knows its order, not God; Taylor makes it Divinity. |
| II.9.10.p1 | We, indeed, are ashamed of certain of our friends,1 who before they were intimate with us were conversant with these opinions | The Greek word (aidōs) is a respectful reticence: Plotinus holds back out of regard for friends; he is not "ashamed" of them. |
| II.9.10.p1 | and not to the many who are auditors of these men | The Greek says "speaking to our own pupils, not to them", the Gnostics; Taylor's "the many who are auditors of these men" is not there. |
| II.9.11.p2 | Again, whether is this illumination from a reasoning process, essence, or as they say, a conception | The Greek asks whether "this", the image, is a substance or, as they say, a "thought" (ennoēma); "this illumination from a reasoning process" is Taylor's. |
| II.9.12.p2 | and [the central position of] the earth | Taylor's words in square brackets are his own; the Greek says only "and the earth to be as it is". |
| II.9.12.p3 | But if matter also is the cause of evil, whence does it appear that it is so | The Greek says that the argument will carry matter too back to the first things, "from where it appeared"; it does not ask whether matter is the cause of evil. |
| II.9.13.p1 | by whom they imagine themselves to be considered as beings of a very honourable nature | The Greek says one should look at the souls of the stars, the souls by which the Gnostics themselves claim to be honourable; it is not that the stars think well of them. |
| II.9.13.p1 | For otherwise subordinate beings would not be generated, as long as the first of things subsist | The Greek says the natural events the stars help to bring about "could never fail to happen as long as the first things exist"; Taylor's sentence says something else. |
| II.9.14.p1 | and utter them as to the stars | The Greek says the incantations are addressed to "them", the things there, the higher beings, not to the stars. |
| II.9.14.p1 | but that sounds possess certain incorporeal powers | The Greek asks "how are incorporeal things moved by sounds?"; Taylor turns the question into a claim about sounds. |
| II.9.14.p3 | There are four lines more in this section in the original ; hut the meaning of them is so very obscure, that I have not attempted to translate them | Taylor's own footnote: he leaves the last sentences of the chapter untranslated. The modern version gives them: compare the rest with this philosophy; the Gnostics' is the opposite in every way. |
| II.9.15.p1 | But the doctrine of the Gnostics, as still more juvenile than this | The Greek word (neanikōteron) here means "more headstrong", more reckless, not "more juvenile". |
| II.9.15.p2 | though they ought to endeavour to correct those with whom they are well acquainted, applying themselves from a divine nature to human concerns | The Greek says that those "who have already come to know" should first set right their life here, as people who have come from a divine nature; Taylor's "those with whom they are well acquainted" and "to human concerns" are not there. |
| II.9.16.p1 | and no one is completely bad till he does despise them | The Greek says that one who was not bad before would become bad by this very act of despising the gods, even if he were not bad in anything else; Taylor's words say something different. |
| II.9.16.p1 | is utterly incongruous | The Greek word (asympathēs) means "without fellow-feeling": an honour to the intelligible gods that does not feel for what is akin to them. |
| II.9.16.p2 | For xvho among those that are stupidly proud , is so orderly and wise as the universe | The Greek plays on words: "which of those who are so foolishly over-wise (hyperphronountes aphronōs) is as ordered and wise (emphrōn) as the universe?" |
| II.9.16.p3 | But the geometrician and arithmetician, knowing in the sensible object the imitation of that w hich subsists in intellection | The Greek speaks here of those who look at paintings; recognising in the picture an imitation of what is in thought, they are stirred to recollection. Taylor gives this to the geometer and the arithmetician. |
| II.9.17.p1 | souls in an orderly series without bodies, imparting magnitude according to the intelligible | The Greek says the souls give size "without bodies", and lead the intelligible into extension; "according to the intelligible" is not there. |
| II.9.17.p3 | but if they despise what they before had acknowledged to be beautiful, and by which they were in a certain respect affected | The Greek says they are proud of despising what they first called beautiful, "being in a bad state" (kakōs diatethentes); Taylor's "by which they were in a certain respect affected" softens it. |
| II.9.18.p1 | The former of these, however, is thought to be the wiser of the two | The Greek says that the one who finds fault "would think himself" wiser and readier to leave; Taylor's "is thought to be" makes it others' opinion. |
| II.9.18.p2 | we shall hasten to be one and the same with them | The Greek says we would hasten "towards the same" goal as the world-soul and the stars, and the same things would be ours to see; not that we would become one with them. |
