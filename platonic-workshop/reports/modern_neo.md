## Plotinus in modern English

- Made from: It is translated from the Greek of Volkmann's edition (Leipzig, 1883-1884), as First1KGreek gives it, chapter by chapter, and checked against Thomas Taylor's translation of 1817.
- Treatises: I.2 On the virtues, I.3 On dialectic, II.4 On matter.

### Flags

| Paragraph | Taylor's words | Why |
|---|---|---|
| I.2.1.p1 | and to the intellect which is the leader in it | The Greek says only "the ruling power in it"; Taylor names it intellect, as the later Platonists did. |
| I.2.1.p2 | For rumor also says, that these are divine | The Greek says that tradition calls these men divine, that is, the people who have the civic virtues; Taylor makes it the virtues. |
| I.2.1.p3 | but that virtue in him is in reality greater than virtue [because it subsists causally.] | Taylor's words in square brackets are his own explanation, from the later Platonists; they are not in the Greek. |
| I.2.2.p2 | and taking away false opinions from a more excellent nature | The Greek says the virtues remove false opinions "by what is altogether better", the means by which they work; it does not say "from a more excellent nature". |
| I.2.2.p2 | Perhaps, too, these measures are in soul as in matter | There is no "perhaps" in the Greek here: the virtues are measures in the soul as in matter. |
| I.2.3.p3 | is divided when compared with it | Taylor has "divided" twice here; Volkmann's Greek has μεμιμημένος, "imitated", and the modern version follows the Greek shown. |
| I.2.4.p1 | if the soul was good prior to her impurity | The Greek says "before the purification", not "before her impurity". |
| I.2.4.p2 | She did not, therefore, possess these, nor recollect them | The Greek is a question ("Did it not have them, then, and does it not recollect?"), which the next sentence answers; Taylor makes it a statement. |
| I.2.4.p2 | when it looks to the illuminating cause | The Greek says "when it looks towards it", that is, when the soul looks to intellect; "the illuminating cause" is Taylor's. |
| I.2.5.p2 | And as to the desire of meats and drinks for the sake of a remission of pain | The Greek has "for relaxation" (anesis); "of pain" is Taylor's addition. |
| I.2.6.p1 | but a correction of the man | The Greek word (katorthōsis) means right action, doing well; it is not "correction". |
| I.2.6.p2 | But coming hither, he will associate with intellect ; and will assimilate this to himself | A hard sentence. Volkmann's Greek has "with intellect" (νῷ), as Taylor does; read so, it is not clear what "this" is that the man makes like himself. From what follows ("not struck", "its master") it seems to be the lower part he lives with here. The modern version keeps the Greek shown and marks "this". |
| I.2.6.p3 | But he will possess intellect by contact | The Greek says that intellect has what it holds by direct contact; it does not say that the man possesses intellect. |
| I.2.6.p4 | Hence justice in the soul is to energize in a greater degree intellectually | The Greek says that the greater justice of the soul is its activity towards intellect; "greater" belongs to the justice, not to the activity. |
| I.2.7.p1 | and that which is as it were fortitude is immateriality | Volkmann's Greek has ταυτότης, "sameness"; Taylor has "immateriality", which is not what the Greek shown says. The modern version follows the Greek. |
| I.2.7.p2 | For if it uses the other virtues | The Greek says "if it is going to use other principles" (archai); Taylor has "the other virtues". |
| I.3.1.p3 | yet is not disposed to be moved from himself | The Greek says he is "less able to be moved from within himself", that is, he needs something outside to move him; Taylor's words come near this. |
| I.3.4.p2 | and again, by an analytic process it arrives at that to which it had proceeded from the first | The Greek says that it takes things apart again "until it comes to the principle" (archē), the first principle; Taylor's words do not say this. |
| I.3.6.p1 | The rational virtues also have habits, and what are now as peculiarities, which they derive from thence | A hard sentence in the Greek. The Workshop takes it to mean that the habits of reason hold what comes from dialectic as their own already, while most of the moral habits come with matter; Taylor's English is a guess too. |
| I.3.6.p2 | But whether is it possible to know | The Greek asks whether the lower virtues can exist without dialectic and wisdom; "to know" is not in it. |
| I.3.6.p2 | It is possible, however, for a man to be thus wise and skilled in dialectic without a knowledge of these | The Greek is a question ("Is it possible to be wise and a dialectician without these?"), and the answer is no; Taylor makes it a statement. |
| II.4.1.p1 | They, likewise, give a body to matter, calling it, body mid of quality | Taylor's "body mid of quality" is the scan's misreading of "body void of quality"; the Greek has a body without quality (apoion sōma). |
| II.4.2.p1 | and first natures will have a casual subsistence | "Casual" here means by chance (kata syntychian): if matter is eternal beside the first principles, the first things are together by accident. |
| II.4.3.p1 | through nature which has an energy directed to form | The Greek says the rational principles make composite "the nature that is active towards form"; Taylor's "through nature" makes nature the means. |
| II.4.4.p1 | Hence, if by intellect you take away its variety | The Greek "τῷ νῷ" can mean "in thought" (in your mind) or "from intellect"; Taylor takes it as "by intellect". The modern version reads it as "in your mind", the usual sense. |
| II.4.5.p1 | But so far as it is able, it proceeds into the profundity of body. say that matter is essence | Taylor's chapter, as the scan gives it, leaves out a passage here: the Greek's "the depth of each thing is matter", the dark beneath the colours, and the two kinds of matter, before "those who say that matter is substance". |
| II.4.5.p3 | does not always possess it | The Greek says that what receives the light "does not have true light" before it receives it; "always" is not there. |
| II.4.6.p1 | as if, for instance, a pot should be resolved into gold ; 1 but gold into water | The Greek has a bowl (phialē); the gold resolved into water is an old view of metals, which Plotinus uses only as an example. |
| II.4.7.p2 | the demiurgus | Taylor keeps the Greek word, the "demiurge" or craftsman of the world; the modern version calls him the Maker. |
| II.4.8.p1 | we must not attribute to the from this, and which is the nature of sensibles | Taylor's chapter, as the scan gives it, has one of his long footnotes printed in the middle of the text here ("matter is always truly the last sediment ... my translation of Aristotle's Physics"). It is Taylor's, from the later Platonists, not Plotinus. |
| II.4.8.p2 | And it would be absurd to suppose, that the introduction of quantity to the matter of a certain bird, is any thing else than adding to it its proper quality | The Greek says it is "no more remarkable" to bring quantity to matter than to add quality to it; Taylor makes it a different claim about one bird. |
| II.4.10.p1 | and wishing to become intelligence, is not intelligence, but, as it were, a privation of intellect | The Greek says that what "would like to be" thinking is not thinking, but rather a kind of thoughtlessness (anoia); Taylor's words are near this, but make reason the one that wishes. |
| II.4.10.p2 | Or does the indefinite consist in a certain negation 1 in conjunction with a certain affirmation | The Greek asks only whether the unbounded lies "in something positive" (en kataphasei tini); "a certain negation in conjunction with" is Taylor's addition. |
| II.4.10.p3 | and sees intellectually, not understanding | The Greek has "it thinks by not thinking" (noei ou nousa); Taylor turns the first verb into "sees". |
| II.4.11.p3 | because, as being the first matter, it is an aptitude to the reception of it | The Greek says matter has a phantom of bulk "as a first fitness for it"; it does not say "as being the first matter". |
| II.4.12.p1 | But forms are conversant with soul, and therefore are not bodies | The Greek says that rational principles alone are in soul, and so there would be no bodies; Taylor makes "forms" the subject. |
| II.4.12.p2 | for it has neither moisture, nor vapour | The Greek says only that matter has no flavours (chymoi), so that neither nose nor tongue grasps it; "vapour" is Taylor's. |
| II.4.13.p2 | For privation is a negation | The Greek word (arsis) means a taking away, a removal; Taylor's "negation" is near, but not the word. |
| II.4.15.p1 | For in the first place, that which happens to any thing ought to be formative ; but the infinite is not formative | The Greek says that what belongs to a thing must be a rational principle (logos), and the infinite is not one; "formative" is Taylor's gloss. |
| II.4.15.p2 | Hence the infinite which is in intelligible^, in consequence of having more of being, is but as an image [with respect to the infinity of matter.] | Taylor's words in square brackets are his own; and his chapter, as the scan gives it, has another of his footnotes (from Proclus on the Timaeus) printed into the text after this. |
| II.4.16.p1 | Perhaps it is not the same with difference simply considered,1 but with a part of difference | Taylor's "simply considered" is his own; the Greek says only "not the same, but a part of Otherness". |
| II.4.16.p2 | but it is the want of wisdom, and the want of virtue, of beauty, strength, morphe, form, and quality | Taylor adds "strength" a second time to the list; the Greek has wisdom, virtue, beauty, shape, form and quality. |
