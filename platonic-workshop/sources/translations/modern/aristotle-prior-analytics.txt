@treatise APr.I | Prior Analytics, Book I
The Prior Analytics is the third of the logical works the Platonists read, after the Categories (simple terms) and On Interpretation (statements). It is about the syllogism, the argument in which, when some things are laid down, something else follows from them of necessity. Book I first defines the premise, the term and the syllogism, and shows how premises convert (chapters 1 to 3). Then it sets out the three figures, the ways in which two premises can share a middle term, and finds which pairings of premises yield a conclusion and which do not: first for premises that simply state what belongs (chapters 4 to 7), then for premises about what is necessary or possible (chapters 8 to 22). It proves that every syllogism is made in one of these figures and can be brought back to the first (23 to 26). Then it teaches how to find premises for a given conclusion, by surveying what follows each term and what each term follows (27 to 31, with a criticism of Plato's method of division in 31), how to put arguments into the figures (32 to 46), and what goes wrong with words and their placing when one does.

@ch APr.I.1 | What the inquiry is about; premise, term, syllogism
The subject is demonstration. A premise affirms or denies one thing of another, universally, particularly or without saying which. A term is what a premise breaks into. A syllogism is an argument in which, when some things are laid down, something else follows of necessity because they are so. It is complete when nothing beyond the premises is needed to make the necessity plain.

@p
First we must say what our inquiry is about and what it belongs to: it is about demonstration, and it belongs to demonstrative knowledge. Next we must define a premise, a term and a syllogism, and which syllogism is complete and which incomplete; and after that, what it is for one thing to be or not to be in another as in a whole, and what we mean by being predicated of all or of none. A premise, then, is a sentence that affirms or denies something of something. It is either universal, or particular, or indefinite. By universal I mean belonging to all or to none; by particular, belonging to some, or not to some, or not to all; by indefinite, belonging or not belonging without saying whether universally or in part, as in "the knowledge of contraries is the same" or "pleasure is not good". A demonstrative premise differs from a dialectical one. The demonstrative premise takes one side of a contradiction (the one who demonstrates does not ask; he lays it down), while the dialectical premise asks which side of a contradiction the other person will grant. But this makes no difference to whether a syllogism comes about in either case. Both the demonstrator and the questioner reason by taking it that something belongs or does not belong to something. So a premise of a syllogism, without qualification, is the affirmation or denial of something of something, in the way described. It is demonstrative if it is true and obtained through the first assumptions of the science. It is dialectical when, for the one who inquires, it is a question offering both sides of a contradiction, and for the one who reasons, the taking of what seems so and is commonly accepted, as was said in the Topics. What a premise is, then, and how the premise of a syllogism, of a demonstration and of dialectic differ, will be said precisely in what follows; for our present need let this be defined well enough.
!flag the faculty that carries it out demonstrative science | Jenkinson's "faculty" is his own word: the Greek says only that the inquiry is "of" (τίνος) demonstrative knowledge, that is, it belongs to that science. The modern version keeps the plainer sense.
!note "Dialectic" here is the art of the Topics: reasoning by question and answer from what is commonly accepted, as in a debate, where the questioner offers both sides of a question and the answerer chooses one.

@p
@greek Ὅρον δὲ καλῶ εἰς ὃν διαλύεται ἡ πρότασις
I call a term that into which a premise breaks up, that is, the predicate and what it is predicated of, with "to be" or "not to be" added. A syllogism is an argument in which, when certain things have been laid down, something other than what was laid down follows of necessity because these things are so. By "because these things are so" I mean that it follows through them; and by "follows through them", that no term from outside is needed to make the conclusion necessary. I call a syllogism complete if it needs nothing besides what has been taken to make the necessity plain; incomplete, if it needs one or more further things which are necessary because of the terms laid down but have not been taken as premises. For one thing to be in another as in a whole is the same as for the one to be predicated of all of the other. We say that one thing is predicated of all of another when nothing can be found of which the other will not be said; and "predicated of none" likewise.
!flag I call that a perfect syllogism which needs nothing | Jenkinson's (and Owen's) "perfect" renders τέλειος, "complete": a syllogism whose necessity is plain from the premises alone. An "imperfect" syllogism is not faulty; it is valid, but needs a further step (a conversion, or a reduction to the impossible) to show it.
!note This is Aristotle's definition of the syllogism, wider than the three-term forms that follow: any argument in which the conclusion follows of necessity from the premises and is something other than they are.

@ch APr.I.2 | How premises convert
Every premise says that something belongs, belongs of necessity, or may belong. A universal negative converts into a universal negative; a universal affirmative into a particular affirmative; a particular affirmative into a particular affirmative; a particular negative does not convert.

@p
Every premise says either that something belongs, or that it belongs of necessity, or that it may belong. Of each of these, some are affirmative and some negative, in each way of speaking; and again, of the affirmative and the negative, some are universal, some particular and some indefinite. Now, a universal negative premise of belonging must convert in its terms: if no pleasure is good, nothing good will be pleasure. The affirmative must convert, but not universally, only in part: if every pleasure is good, some good is also pleasure. Of the particular premises, the affirmative must convert in part (if some pleasure is good, some good will be pleasure), but the negative need not: it does not follow that if man does not belong to some animal, animal does not belong to some man.

@p
@greek Πρῶτον μὲν οὖν ἔστω στερητικὴ καθόλου ἡ Α Β πρότασις
First, then, let the premise AB be a universal negative. If A belongs to no B, neither will B belong to any A. For if it belongs to some A, say C, it will not be true that A belongs to no B, since C is one of the Bs. If A belongs to every B, B will also belong to some A. For if to none, neither will A belong to any B; but it was laid down that it belongs to every B. It is likewise if the premise is particular. If A belongs to some B, B must also belong to some A; for if to none, neither would A belong to any B. But if A does not belong to some B, it is not necessary that B also does not belong to some A. Let B be animal and A man: man does not belong to every animal, but animal belongs to every man.
!note The proofs of conversion are the foundation of what follows: the second and third figures are shown valid by converting their premises into the first.

@ch APr.I.3 | Conversion of necessary and possible premises
Necessary premises convert as simple ones do. Affirmative premises of the possible convert alike in every sense of "possible". Negative ones convert alike where "possible" means necessary or not necessary; but where it means what holds for the most part, or by nature, the universal negative does not convert and the particular does. "May not belong" is affirmative in form.

@p
It will be the same with necessary premises. The universal negative converts universally, and each of the affirmatives converts in part. If it is necessary that A belongs to no B, it is necessary that B belongs to no A; for if it may belong to some, A too might belong to some B. If A belongs of necessity to every B or to some B, B must belong to some A; for if not of necessity, neither would A belong of necessity to some B. The particular negative does not convert, for the reason we gave before.

@p
@greek Ἐπὶ δὲ τῶν ἐνδεχομένων
With possible premises, since "possible" is said in several ways (we call possible what is necessary, what is not necessary, and what can be), the affirmatives will convert in like manner in every case. If A may belong to every B or to some B, B might belong to some A; for if to none, neither would A belong to any B, as was shown before. With the negatives it is not the same. Where something is called possible because it necessarily does not belong, or because it does not necessarily belong, the conversion is like the others: for instance, if one said that man may not be a horse, or that white may belong to no garment. In the first, the one necessarily does not belong to the other; in the second, it need not belong; and the premise converts likewise. If it is possible for horse to belong to no man, it is possible for man to belong to no horse; and if white may belong to no garment, garment may belong to nothing white; for if it necessarily belonged to some, white too would of necessity belong to some garment, as was shown before. The particular negative is likewise. But where something is called possible because it holds for the most part, or because it is natural, which is how we define the possible, it will not be the same with the negative conversions: the universal negative does not convert, and the particular does. This will be plain when we speak of the possible. For now let this much be clear, besides what has been said: that "may belong to none" or "may not belong to some" has the form of an affirmative. For "may" is placed like "is", and "is", whatever it is added to as a predicate, always makes an affirmation, as in "is not-good", "is not-white", or simply "is not-this". This too will be shown later. In conversion these will behave like the other affirmatives.
!flag and it is in this way we define the possible | Jenkinson's sentence is right, but it needs the definition it points to: in chapter 13 Aristotle defines the possible (τὸ ἐνδεχόμενον) as what is not necessary but, if supposed to be, involves nothing impossible. So "possible" in these chapters is two-sided: what may be and may also not be.

@ch APr.I.4 | The first figure
When the last term is in the middle as in a whole, and the middle in the first, there is a complete syllogism: if A of every B and B of every C, A of every C; if A of no B and B of every C, A of no C; and likewise with a particular minor premise. Other pairings give no syllogism, as counterexamples show. Every syllogism in this figure is complete, and it proves every kind of conclusion.

@p
Now that these things are settled, let us say through what, when and how every syllogism comes about. Afterwards we must speak of demonstration. We must speak of the syllogism before demonstration, because the syllogism is more universal: a demonstration is a kind of syllogism, but not every syllogism is a demonstration.

@p
@greek Ὅταν οὖν ὅροι τρεῖς
When three terms are so related to one another that the last is in the middle as in a whole, and the middle is or is not in the first as in a whole, there must be a complete syllogism of the extremes. I call the middle the term which is itself in another and has another in it, and which is also middle by its position. The extremes are the term which is itself in another and the term in which another is. If A is predicated of every B, and B of every C, A must be predicated of every C, for we have already said what we mean by "of every". Likewise, if A is predicated of no B, and B of every C, A will belong to no C. But if the first follows every middle, and the middle belongs to none of the last, there will be no syllogism of the extremes, for nothing necessary follows from these being so: the first may belong to all of the last or to none, so that neither a particular nor a universal conclusion becomes necessary; and where nothing is necessary through these premises, there will be no syllogism. Terms for belonging to all: animal, man, horse; for belonging to none: animal, man, stone. Nor is there a syllogism when the first belongs to none of the middle and the middle to none of the last. Terms for belonging: science, line, medicine; for not belonging: science, line, unit. With the terms universal, then, it is clear in this figure when there will be a syllogism and when not; and that if there is a syllogism the terms must be related as we said, and that if they are so related there will be a syllogism.
!note The "terms" Aristotle gives are counterexamples. With animal, man, horse the premises (animal belongs to every man; man to no horse) are true and the extremes are related as "every" (animal to every horse); with animal, man, stone the same premises hold but the extremes are related as "none". Since the same pair of premises allows both, nothing follows from it.

@p
@greek ὁ μὲν καθόλου τῶν ὅρων ὁ δʼ ἐν μέρει
If one of the terms is related universally and the other particularly, then when the universal premise is placed with the major term, affirmative or negative, and the particular premise with the minor, affirmative, there must be a complete syllogism; but when the universal is placed with the minor, or the terms are related in any other way, it is impossible. I call the major term that in which the middle is, and the minor that which is under the middle. Let A belong to every B, and B to some C. Then if "predicated of every" means what was said at the start, A must belong to some C. And if A belongs to no B and B to some C, A must not belong to some C, for we have also defined what we mean by "of none". So there will be a complete syllogism. It is likewise if BC is indefinite, provided it is affirmative: the syllogism will be the same whether it is taken as indefinite or as particular.
!note These are the four valid moods of the first figure, later named Barbara, Celarent, Darii and Ferio. The Latin names are medieval; Aristotle gives none.

@p
@greek Ἐὰν δὲ πρὸς τὸ ἔλαττον ἄκρον
If the universal is placed with the minor term, affirmative or negative, there will be no syllogism, whether the major premise is affirmative or negative, indefinite or particular: for instance if A belongs or does not belong to some B, and B belongs to every C. Terms for belonging: good, state, practical wisdom; for not belonging: good, state, ignorance. Again, if B belongs to no C, and A belongs or does not belong to some B, or not to every B, there will be no syllogism either. Terms: white, horse, swan; white, horse, raven. The same terms serve if AB is indefinite. Nor will there be a syllogism when the premise with the major term is universal, affirmative or negative, and the premise with the minor is particular and negative: for instance if A belongs to every B, and B does not belong to some C, or not to every C. For to whatever part of C the middle does not belong, the first may follow all of it or none of it. Suppose the terms animal, man, white; then from the white things of which man is not predicated, take swan and snow. Animal is predicated of all of the one and of none of the other, so there will be no syllogism. Again, let A belong to no B, and B not belong to some C, and let the terms be inanimate, man, white; then take, from the white things of which man is not predicated, swan and snow. Inanimate is predicated of all of the one and of none of the other. Further, "B does not belong to some C" is indefinite: it is true whether B belongs to no C or not to every C. And when terms are taken such that B belongs to no C, there is no syllogism, as was said before. So it is plain that with the terms so related there will be no syllogism, or there would be one in that case too. It will be shown likewise if the universal premise is negative.
!note "Good, state, practical wisdom": wisdom is a state (ἕξις) and is good; ignorance is a state and is not good. Both pairs of premises are true, but the conclusions run opposite ways, so nothing follows.

@p
@greek Οὐδὲ ἐὰν ἄμφω τὰ διαστήματα
Nor will there be a syllogism in any way if both relations are particular, affirmative or negative, or one affirmative and the other negative, or one indefinite and the other definite, or both indefinite. Terms common to all of these: animal, white, horse; animal, white, stone. It is plain, then, from what has been said, that if there is a particular syllogism in this figure, the terms must be related as we said; otherwise there is none at all. It is also clear that all the syllogisms in this figure are complete, since all are completed through what was first taken; and that every kind of conclusion is proved through this figure: belonging to all, to none, to some and not to some. I call this kind of figure the first.
!note "Relations" renders διαστήματα, literally "intervals": Aristotle pictures a premise as the distance between two terms, as on a line.

@ch APr.I.5 | The second figure
When the same term belongs to all of one and none of the other, or to all or none of both, the figure is the second: the middle is predicated of both extremes. No syllogism here is complete, and none is affirmative. There is a syllogism when the premises differ in quality and the major is universal; it is completed by converting a negative premise or by reduction to the impossible.

@p
When the same thing belongs to all of one term and to none of the other, or to all or none of each, I call this kind of figure the second. In it I call the middle the term predicated of both, and the extremes the terms it is said of. The major extreme is the one placed next to the middle, the minor the one farther from it. The middle is placed outside the extremes, and first in position. There will never be a complete syllogism in this figure, but there can be one both when the terms are universal and when they are not. When they are universal, there will be a syllogism if the middle belongs to all of one and to none of the other, whichever of them the negative is attached to; otherwise there is none at all. Let M be predicated of no N and of every X. Since the negative converts, N will belong to no M; but M was laid down to belong to every X; so N belongs to no X, for this was shown before. Again, if M belongs to every N and to no X, neither will X belong to any N. For if M belongs to no X, neither does X belong to any M; but M belonged to every N; so X will belong to no N, for the first figure has come about again. And since the negative converts, neither will N belong to any X; so it will be the same syllogism. These can also be shown by leading to the impossible. It is plain, then, that a syllogism comes about with the terms so related, but not a complete one, since the necessity is completed not only from what was first taken but from other things as well. But if M is predicated of every N and of every X, there will be no syllogism. Terms for belonging: substance, animal, man; for not belonging: substance, animal, number; the middle is substance. Nor when M is predicated of no N and of no X. Terms for belonging: line, animal, man; for not belonging: line, animal, stone. It is plain, then, that if there is a syllogism with the terms universal, they must be related as we said at the start; related otherwise, nothing necessary follows.
!flag of not being present with, essence, animal, stone | Taylor's "essence" renders οὐσία, which in Aristotle's logic is "substance", the first of the categories; and his "being present with" renders ὑπάρχειν, "belonging to". Neither is wrong in Taylor's Platonist English, but today both mislead: "essence" now means a thing's nature, and "present with" sounds like being nearby.
!note These are the moods later called Cesare and Camestres. Here the terms are M, N and X (in the Greek Μ, Ν, Ξ); in the third figure they will be P, R and S (Π, Ρ, Σ).

@p
@greek Ἐὰν δὲ πρὸς τὸν ἕτερον ᾖ καθόλου τὸ μέσον
If the middle is universal in relation to one of the extremes, then when it is universal in relation to the major, affirmative or negative, and particular in relation to the minor, and opposite in quality to the universal (I mean by "opposite" that if the universal is negative the particular is affirmative, and if the universal is affirmative the particular is negative), there must be a particular negative syllogism. If M belongs to no N and to some X, N must not belong to some X. For since the negative converts, N will belong to no M; but M was laid down to belong to some X; so N will not belong to some X, for a syllogism comes about through the first figure. Again, if M belongs to every N and does not belong to some X, N must not belong to some X. For if it belongs to every X, and M is predicated of every N, M must belong to every X; but it was laid down not to belong to some. And if M belongs to every N and not to every X, there will be a syllogism that N does not belong to every X; the proof is the same. But if M is predicated of every X and not of every N, there will be no syllogism. Terms: animal, substance, raven; animal, white, raven. Nor when it is predicated of no X and of some N. Terms for belonging: animal, substance, unit; for not belonging: animal, substance, science.
!note These are the moods later called Festino and Baroco. Baroco, with an affirmative universal and a negative particular, cannot be completed by conversion; it is shown by supposing the opposite of the conclusion and reaching an impossibility.

@p
@greek Ὅταν μὲν οὖν ἀντικείμενον ᾖ τὸ καθόλου
When the universal is opposite in quality to the particular, then, it has been said when there will be a syllogism and when not. When the premises are of the same form, both negative or both affirmative, there will be no syllogism in any way. First let them be negative, and let the universal be placed with the major term: let M belong to no N and not belong to some X. Then N may belong to every X or to none. Terms for not belonging: black, snow, animal. Terms for belonging to all cannot be found if M belongs to some X and not to some. For if N belongs to every X, and M to no N, M will belong to no X; but it was laid down to belong to some. So terms cannot be found in this way; it must be shown from the indefiniteness of the particular. Since "M does not belong to some X" is true even if M belongs to no X, and when it belonged to none there was no syllogism, it is plain that there will be none now either. Again, let them be affirmative, and the universal placed likewise: let M belong to every N and to some X. Then N may belong to every X or to none. Terms for belonging to none: white, swan, stone. Terms for belonging to all cannot be found, for the same reason as before; it must be shown from the indefinite. If the universal is with the minor, and M belongs to no X and does not belong to some N, N may belong to every X or to none. Terms for belonging: white, animal, raven; for not belonging: white, stone, raven. If the premises are affirmative, terms for not belonging: white, animal, snow; for belonging: white, animal, swan. It is plain, then, that when the premises are of the same form, one universal and the other particular, there is no syllogism at all. Nor is there if the middle belongs or does not belong to some of each, or belongs to some of one and not to some of the other, or to all of neither, or indefinitely. Terms common to all: white, animal, man; white, animal, inanimate. It is plain from what has been said, then, that if the terms are related to one another as was said, a syllogism comes about of necessity; and if there is a syllogism, the terms must be so related. It is clear too that all the syllogisms in this figure are incomplete, since all are completed by adding something which either holds in the terms of necessity or is laid down as a hypothesis, as when we prove through the impossible; and that no affirmative syllogism comes about through this figure, but all are negative, both universal and particular.

@ch APr.I.6 | The third figure
When two terms both belong, or both do not belong, to the same term, the figure is the third: the middle is the subject of both. No syllogism here is complete, and none is universal. There is a syllogism when both premises are affirmative, or when the major is negative and the minor affirmative; it is shown by conversion, by reduction to the impossible, or by setting out an instance.

@p
If one term belongs to all and the other to none of the same thing, or both to all or both to none, I call this kind of figure the third. In it I call the middle the term of which both are predicated, and the extremes the terms predicated. The major extreme is the one farther from the middle, the minor the nearer. The middle is placed outside the extremes, and last in position. No complete syllogism comes about in this figure either, but there can be one both when the terms are related to the middle universally and when they are not. When they are universal, if both P and R belong to every S, P will belong of necessity to some R. For since the affirmative converts, S will belong to some R; so since P belongs to every S and S to some R, P must belong to some R, for a syllogism comes about through the first figure. The proof can also be made through the impossible, and by setting out. For if both belong to every S, then if one of the Ss is taken, say N, both P and R will belong to it, so that P will belong to some R. And if R belongs to every S and P to none, there will be a syllogism that P will of necessity not belong to some R; the proof is made in the same way, by converting the premise RS. It could also be shown through the impossible, as before. But if R belongs to no S and P to every S, there will be no syllogism. Terms for belonging: animal, horse, man; for not belonging: animal, inanimate, man. Nor will there be a syllogism when both are said of no S. Terms for belonging: animal, horse, inanimate; for not belonging: man, horse, inanimate; the middle is inanimate. In this figure too, then, it is plain when there will be a syllogism and when not, with the terms universal. When both terms are affirmative, there will be a syllogism that one extreme belongs to some of the other; when both are negative, there will not. When one is negative and the other affirmative, if the major is negative and the other affirmative, there will be a syllogism that one extreme does not belong to some of the other; if the other way round, there will not.
!flag It is possible to demonstrate this also per tmpossibele and by exposition | Jenkinson's "exposition" renders ἔκθεσις, "setting out": picking out one particular instance of the middle term (here, one of the Ss) and seeing that both extremes belong to it. It is a proof by example, not an explanation, which is what "exposition" now suggests.
!note These are the moods later called Darapti and Felapton.

@p
@greek Ἐὰν δʼ ὁ μὲν ᾖ καθόλου πρὸς τὸ μέσον ὁ δʼ ἐν μέρει
If one is universal in relation to the middle and the other particular, then when both are affirmative there must be a syllogism, whichever of the terms is universal. If R belongs to every S and P to some S, P must belong to some R. For since the affirmative converts, S will belong to some P; so since R belongs to every S and S to some P, R will also belong to some P; and so P to some R. Again, if R belongs to some S and P to every S, P must belong to some R, for the proof is made in the same way. It can also be proved through the impossible and by setting out, as before.
!note These are the moods later called Disamis and Datisi.

@p
@greek Ἐὰν δʼ ὁ μὲν ἦ κατηγορικὸς ὁ δὲ στερητικός
If one is affirmative and the other negative, and the affirmative is universal, then when the minor is affirmative there will be a syllogism. If R belongs to every S and P does not belong to some S, P must not belong to some R. For if P belongs to every R, and R to every S, P will also belong to every S; but it did not. It is also shown without the reduction, if one of the Ss is taken to which P does not belong. When the major is affirmative, there will be no syllogism: for instance if P belongs to every S and R does not belong to some S. Terms for belonging to all: animate, man, animal. Terms for belonging to none cannot be found if R belongs to some S and not to some. For if P belongs to every S and R to some S, P will also belong to some R; but it was laid down to belong to none. It must be taken as before: since "does not belong to some" is indefinite, it is true to say of what belongs to none that it does not belong to some; and when it belonged to none, there was no syllogism. It is plain, then, that there will be no syllogism. If the negative term is universal, then when the major is negative and the minor affirmative there will be a syllogism. If P belongs to no S and R belongs to some S, P will not belong to some R; for there will again be the first figure when the premise RS is converted. When the minor is negative there will be no syllogism. Terms for belonging: animal, man, wild; for not belonging: animal, science, wild; in both the middle is wild. Nor when both are laid down as negative, and one is universal and the other particular. Terms, when the minor is universal in relation to the middle: animal, science, wild; animal, man, wild. When the major is universal, terms for not belonging: raven, snow, white. Terms for belonging cannot be found if R belongs to some S and not to some. For if P belongs to every R, and R to some S, P also belongs to some S; but it was laid down to belong to none. It must be shown from the indefinite.
!note These are the moods later called Bocardo and Ferison. Bocardo, like Baroco in the second figure, is proved by the impossible or by setting out an instance, not by conversion.

@p
@greek Οὐδ’ ἂν ἑκάτερος τινὶ τῷ μέσῳ
Nor will there be any syllogism if each belongs or does not belong to some of the middle, or one belongs and the other does not, or one belongs to some and the other not to all, or indefinitely. Terms common to all: animal, man, white; animal, inanimate, white. It is plain, then, in this figure too, when there will be a syllogism and when not; that if the terms are related as was said, a syllogism comes about of necessity; and that if there is a syllogism, the terms must be so related. It is plain too that all the syllogisms in this figure are incomplete, since all are completed by adding something; and that it will not be possible to reach a universal conclusion through this figure, either negative or affirmative.

@ch APr.I.7 | What the three figures share; all syllogisms brought back to the first
In every figure, two affirmative or two negative premises that give no syllogism give nothing at all, but an affirmative with a universal negative always gives a conclusion of the minor term about the major. An indefinite premise works like a particular. All incomplete syllogisms are completed through the first figure, and all syllogisms can be brought back to the universal syllogisms of the first figure.

@p
It is clear too that in all the figures, when no syllogism comes about, nothing necessary follows at all if both terms are affirmative or both negative. But if one is affirmative and the other negative, and the negative is taken universally, there is always a syllogism of the minor extreme in relation to the major. For instance, if A belongs to every B or to some, and B to no C: when the premises are converted, C must not belong to some A. It is likewise in the other figures, for a syllogism always comes about through conversion. It is clear too that an indefinite premise put in place of a particular affirmative will make the same syllogism in all the figures.
!note A conclusion "of the minor in relation to the major" is one in which the minor term is predicated of the major: the reverse of the usual order. Later logicians drew from this passage the "indirect" moods of the first figure, out of which the fourth figure was made.

@p
@greek Φανερὸν δὲ καὶ ὅτι πάντες οἱ ἀτελεῖς
It is plain too that all incomplete syllogisms are completed through the first figure. All of them are concluded either directly or through the impossible, and in both ways the first figure comes about. When they are completed directly, it comes about because all were concluded through conversion, and conversion made the first figure. When they are proved through the impossible, it comes about because when the false is laid down, the syllogism comes about through the first figure. For instance, in the last figure, if A and B belong to every C, A belongs to some B. For if to none, and B belongs to every C, A will belong to no C; but it belonged to every C. Likewise in the other cases.

@p
@greek Ἔστι δὲ καὶ ἀναγαγεῖν πάντας
It is also possible to bring all syllogisms back to the universal syllogisms of the first figure. Those of the second are plainly completed through them, though not all alike: the universal ones by converting the negative, and each of the particular ones by reduction to the impossible. The particular syllogisms of the first figure are completed through themselves, but they can also be proved through the second figure by leading to the impossible. If A belongs to every B and B to some C, A belongs to some C. For if to none, and to every B, B will belong to no C, as we know through the second figure. The proof will be likewise for the negative. If A belongs to no B, and B belongs to some C, A will not belong to some C. For if to every C, and to no B, B will belong to no C; and this was the middle figure. So since the syllogisms of the middle figure are all brought back to the universal syllogisms of the first, and the particular ones of the first to those of the middle, it is plain that the particular ones too will be brought back to the universal syllogisms of the first figure. Those of the third, when the terms are universal, are completed directly through those syllogisms; when they are taken particularly, through the particular syllogisms of the first figure. These were brought back to the universal ones, and so are the particular syllogisms of the third figure. It is plain, then, that all will be brought back to the universal syllogisms of the first figure. We have now said how the syllogisms that prove that something belongs or does not belong behave, both those of the same figure by themselves and those of different figures in relation to one another.

@ch APr.I.8 | Syllogisms from necessary premises
Belonging, belonging of necessity and possibly belonging are different, so their syllogisms differ. With two necessary premises the syllogisms are nearly the same as with plain ones, except that "of necessity" is added; but Baroco and Bocardo must be proved by setting out an instance.

@p
Since belonging, belonging of necessity, and possibly belonging are different (many things belong, but not of necessity; others neither belong of necessity nor belong at all, but may belong), it is clear that the syllogism of each will be different too, and with the terms not related alike: one is from necessary premises, one from premises of belonging, one from premises of the possible. With necessary premises things are almost the same as with premises of belonging. With the terms placed in the same way, whether something belongs or belongs or does not belong of necessity, there will be a syllogism or there will not; the only difference will be that "belongs of necessity" or "does not belong of necessity" is attached to the terms. The negative converts in the same way, and we shall explain "being in as in a whole" and "of all" likewise. In the other cases, then, the conclusion will be shown necessary in the same way, through conversion, as with belonging. But in the middle figure, when the universal is affirmative and the particular negative, and again in the third, when the universal is affirmative and the particular negative, the proof will not be alike. We must set out some part of the subject to which each predicate does not belong, and make the syllogism about it, for with these the syllogism will be necessary. And if it is necessary of what is set out, it is also necessary of some of the other term, since what is set out is just a part of it. Each of these syllogisms comes about in its own figure.
!note The reason for the special proof: in Baroco and Bocardo the reduction to the impossible would need the contradictory of a necessary conclusion, "possibly not", which does not combine with the premises as the plain case does. Setting out a part of the subject avoids this.

@ch APr.I.9 | One necessary premise in the first figure
In the first figure the conclusion is necessary if the major premise is necessary, but not if only the minor is. With a particular syllogism, the conclusion is necessary when the universal premise is necessary.

@p
It sometimes happens that the syllogism becomes necessary when only one of the premises is necessary; not whichever it may be, but the one with the major term. For instance, if A has been taken as belonging or not belonging of necessity to B, and B as simply belonging to C. With the premises taken in this way, A will of necessity belong or not belong to C. For since A belongs or does not belong of necessity to every B, and C is one of the Bs, it is plain that one or the other will hold of necessity of C too. But if AB is not necessary and BC is necessary, the conclusion will not be necessary. For if it is, it will follow, through the first figure and through the third, that A belongs of necessity to some B. But this is false, for B may be such that A may belong to none of it. Further, it is plain from terms that the conclusion will not be necessary. Let A be motion, B animal, and C man. Man is of necessity an animal, but an animal is not of necessity in motion, and neither is man. It is likewise if AB is negative, for the proof is the same.
!note This is the famous "two Barbaras" problem. Aristotle holds that "A belongs of necessity to every B; B belongs to every C" yields "A belongs of necessity to every C", but that "A belongs to every B; B belongs of necessity to every C" yields only "A belongs to every C". Theophrastus and Eudemus, his pupils, disagreed: the conclusion, they said, always follows the weaker premise. The commentators (Alexander, Philoponus) report the dispute.

@p
@greek ἐπὶ δὲ τῶν ἐν μέρει συλλογισμῶν
With particular syllogisms, if the universal premise is necessary, the conclusion too will be necessary; but if the particular is necessary, it will not, whether the universal premise is negative or affirmative. First let the universal be necessary: let A belong of necessity to every B, and let B simply belong to some C. Then A must belong of necessity to some C, for C is under B, and A belonged of necessity to every B. It is likewise if the syllogism is negative, for the proof will be the same. But if the particular premise is necessary, the conclusion will not be necessary, since nothing impossible results, just as in the universal syllogisms. It is likewise with negatives. Terms: motion, animal, white.

@ch APr.I.10 | One necessary premise in the second figure
In the second figure the conclusion is necessary if the negative premise is necessary, but not if the affirmative is: "no white thing is a man" follows of necessity only while no white thing is an animal.

@p
In the second figure, if the negative premise is necessary, the conclusion too will be necessary; if the affirmative is, it will not. First let the negative be necessary: let A be possible for no B, and let it simply belong to C. Since the negative converts, B is not possible for any A either; and A belongs to every C; so B is possible for no C, for C is under A. It is the same if the negative is placed with C. If A is possible for no C, neither is C possible for any A; and A belongs to every B; so C is possible for no B, for the first figure comes about again. So neither does B belong to C, since it converts likewise. If the affirmative premise is necessary, the conclusion will not be necessary. Let A belong of necessity to every B, and simply belong to no C. When the negative is converted, the first figure comes about, and it was shown in the first figure that when the negative premise with the major is not necessary, neither will the conclusion be necessary; so neither will it be of necessity here. Further, if the conclusion is necessary, it follows that C of necessity does not belong to some A. For if B of necessity belongs to no C, neither will C of necessity belong to any B. But B must belong to some A, if A belonged of necessity to every B. So C must not belong to some A. But nothing prevents A from being taken such that C may belong to all of it. Further, by setting out terms one could show that the conclusion is not necessary simply, but only necessary given these premises. Let A be animal, B man, C white, and let the premises be taken likewise. Animal may belong to nothing white. Then man too will belong to nothing white, but not of necessity; for a man may become white, though not while animal belongs to nothing white. So given these premises the conclusion will be necessary, but it is not necessary simply.
!note "Necessary given these premises" and "necessary simply" is the distinction later called necessity of the consequence (the conclusion must follow) and necessity of the consequent (the conclusion is itself a necessary truth).

@p
@greek Ὁμοίως δʼ ἕξει καὶ ἐπὶ τῶν ἐν μέρει συλλογισμῶν
It will be likewise with particular syllogisms. When the negative premise is universal and necessary, the conclusion too will be necessary; when the affirmative is universal and the negative particular, the conclusion will not be necessary. First let the negative be universal and necessary: let A be possible for no B, and let it belong to some C. Since the negative converts, neither could B belong to any A; and A belongs to some C; so B will of necessity not belong to some C. Again, let the affirmative be universal and necessary, and let it be placed with B. If A belongs of necessity to every B, and does not belong to some C, it is plain that B will not belong to some C, but not of necessity; for the same terms will serve for the proof as in the universal syllogisms. Nor will the conclusion be necessary if the negative is necessary and taken as particular, for the proof is through the same terms.

@ch APr.I.11 | One necessary premise in the third figure
In the third figure, with both premises affirmative, the conclusion is necessary if either universal premise is necessary; with one negative, it is necessary when the negative is universal and necessary, and not otherwise. Examples: waking, sleeping, two-footed, animal.

@p
In the last figure, when the terms are universal in relation to the middle and both premises are affirmative, the conclusion will be necessary if either one is necessary. If one is negative and the other affirmative, the conclusion will be necessary when the negative is necessary, but not when the affirmative is. First let both premises be affirmative: let A and B belong to every C, and let AC be necessary. Since B belongs to every C, C will also belong to some B, because the universal converts into the particular; so if A belongs of necessity to every C, and C to some B, A must also belong of necessity to some B, for B is under C. So the first figure comes about. It will be shown likewise if BC is necessary, for C converts with some A; so if B belongs of necessity to every C, it will also belong of necessity to some A.

@p
@greek Πάλιν ἔστω τὸ μὲν
Again, let AC be negative and BC affirmative, and let the negative be necessary. Since C converts with some B, and A of necessity belongs to no C, neither will A belong of necessity to some B, for B is under C. But if the affirmative is necessary, the conclusion will not be necessary. Let BC be affirmative and necessary, and AC negative and not necessary. Since the affirmative converts, C too will belong of necessity to some B; so if A belongs to no C, and C to some B, A will not belong to some B, but not of necessity; for it was shown in the first figure that when the negative premise is not necessary, neither will the conclusion be. It would also be plain through terms. Let A be good, B animal, C horse. Good may belong to no horse, and animal must belong to every horse; but it is not necessary that some animal is not good, since every animal may be good. Or, if that is not possible, waking or sleeping should be laid down as the term, since every animal can take these.
!note The doubt is whether every animal could be good, since "good" is said of so many things. So Aristotle offers a safer term: every animal is capable of waking and of sleeping, and none of them must be awake.

@p
@greek Εἰ μὲν οὖν οἱ ὅροι καθόλου πρὸς τὸ μέσον εἰσίν
When the terms are universal in relation to the middle, then, it has been said when the conclusion will be necessary. If one is universal and the other particular, and both are affirmative, the conclusion will be necessary when the universal becomes necessary. The proof is the same as before, for the particular affirmative also converts. If B must belong to every C, and A is under C, B must belong to some A. And if B belongs to some A, A must also belong to some B, for it converts. It is likewise if AC is necessary and universal, since B is under C. But if the particular is necessary, the conclusion will not be necessary. Let BC be particular and necessary, and let A belong to every C, but not of necessity. When BC is converted the first figure comes about, and the universal premise is not necessary while the particular is. When the premises were so, the conclusion was not necessary; so neither here. It is plain also from the terms. Let A be waking, B two-footed, and C animal. B must belong to some C, and A may belong to C, and A does not belong to B of necessity, since it is not necessary that something two-footed is asleep or awake. It will be shown likewise through the same terms if AC is particular and necessary.

@p
@greek Εἰ δʼ ὁ μὲν κατηγορικός ὁ δὲ στερητικός τῶν ὅρων
If one of the terms is affirmative and the other negative, then when the universal is negative and necessary, the conclusion too will be necessary. For if A is possible for no C, and B belongs to some C, A must not belong to some B. But when the affirmative is laid down as necessary, whether universal or particular, or the negative is particular, the conclusion will not be necessary. We shall say the same things as before; the terms, when the universal affirmative is necessary, are waking, animal, man, with man the middle; when the particular affirmative is necessary, waking, animal, white. Animal must belong to some white thing, but waking may belong to none, and it is not necessary that waking does not belong to some animal. When the negative is particular and necessary: two-footed, moving, animal, with animal the middle.

@ch APr.I.12 | Summary on necessary premises
A conclusion that something belongs needs both premises of belonging; a necessary conclusion needs at least one necessary premise. In both, one premise must be like the conclusion.

@p
It is plain, then, that there is no syllogism that something belongs unless both premises are of belonging, but there is a syllogism of the necessary even when only one of them is necessary. In both, whether the syllogisms are affirmative or negative, one of the premises must be like the conclusion. By "like" I mean that if the conclusion is of belonging, the premise is of belonging; if necessary, necessary. So this too is clear, that the conclusion will be neither necessary nor of belonging unless a necessary premise, or a premise of belonging, has been taken. About the necessary, then, how it comes about and how it differs from belonging, enough has been said.

@ch APr.I.13 | What "possible" means; the two kinds of possible
The possible is what is not necessary but, if supposed to hold, involves nothing impossible. So premises of the possible convert into their opposites: "may belong" into "may not belong". Something is possible either as what holds for the most part, by nature, or as what is undetermined and may go either way; science deals with the first.

@p
Next let us say about the possible when, how and through what premises there will be a syllogism. By "to be possible" and "the possible" I mean that which is not necessary, but which, if it is supposed to hold, will involve nothing impossible on that account; for we call the necessary "possible" only in another sense of the word. That this is what the possible is, is plain from the denials and affirmations opposed to one another. "It is not possible that it belongs", "it is impossible that it belongs" and "it is necessary that it does not belong" are either the same or follow one another; so their opposites too, "it is possible that it belongs", "it is not impossible that it belongs" and "it is not necessary that it does not belong", will be either the same or follow one another, since of everything either the affirmation or the denial holds. So the possible will be what is not necessary, and what is not necessary will be possible. It follows that all premises of the possible convert into one another. I do not mean that the affirmatives convert into the negatives, but that those with an affirmative form convert into their opposites: "may belong" into "may not belong", "may belong to all" into "may belong to none" and "may not belong to all", and "may belong to some" into "may not belong to some". It is the same way with the others. Since the possible is not necessary, and what is not necessary may not belong, it is plain that if A may belong to B, it may also not belong; and if it may belong to all, it may also not belong to all. It is likewise with the particular affirmatives, for the proof is the same. Such premises are affirmative and not negative, for "may" is placed like "is", as was said before.
!flag that which is not necessary but, being assumed, results in nothing impossible | Jenkinson's sentence is close to the Greek, but "being assumed" leaves out what is assumed: that the thing holds (τεθέντος δʼ ὑπάρχειν). The test is to suppose that the possible is actually so, and see whether anything impossible follows.
!note This two-sided possibility, "neither necessary nor impossible", is what logicians now call contingency. The one-sided sense, "not impossible", which includes the necessary, is the one Aristotle here calls possible "in another sense of the word" (ὁμωνύμως).

@p
@greek Διωρισμένων δὲ τούτων πάλιν λέγωμεν
With this defined, let us say again that "to be possible" is said in two ways. In one way it is what happens for the most part but falls short of necessity, such as a man's going grey, or growing, or wasting away, or in general what belongs by nature. This does not have its necessity unbroken, because a man does not always exist; but while a man exists, it holds either of necessity or for the most part. In the other way it is the undetermined, which can be thus and not thus, such as an animal's walking, or an earthquake's happening while it walks, or in general what happens by chance; for none of these is by nature more one way than the opposite. Each kind of possible converts into its opposite premise, but not in the same way. What is natural converts because it does not belong of necessity (in this way it is possible that a man does not go grey); the undetermined, because it is no more this way than that. There is no science or demonstrative syllogism of undetermined things, because the middle term is irregular; but there is of natural things, and arguments and inquiries are made, for the most part, about what is possible in this way. A syllogism can be made about the others, but it is not usually sought.
!flag In another sense the expression means the indefinite | Jenkinson's "the indefinite" renders τὸ ἀόριστον, "the undetermined": what is not fixed either way, like chance events. In these chapters "indefinite" also renders a different word, ἀδιόριστος, a premise that does not say "all" or "some"; the modern version keeps them apart.

@p
@greek Ταῦτα μὲν οὖν διορισθήσεται μᾶλλον
These things will be defined more fully in what follows. Now let us say when, how and what syllogism there will be from premises of the possible. "This may belong to that" can be taken in two ways: either "to what that belongs to" or "to what that may belong to". "A is possible of what B is said of" means one of these: either of what B is said of, or of what B may be said of. And "A is possible of what B is said of" is no different from "A may belong to every B". So it is plain that "A may belong to every B" can be said in two ways. First, then, let us say what syllogism, and of what kind, there will be if B is possible of what C is said of, and A of what B is said of; for so both premises are taken as possible. But when A is possible of what B belongs to, one premise is of belonging and the other of the possible. So we must begin from premises of the same form, as in the other cases.

@ch APr.I.14 | Two possible premises in the first figure
With both premises possible and universal, the first figure gives complete syllogisms when the minor is affirmative; when the minor is negative, the premise must first be converted into its affirmative counterpart. With the major premise particular there is never a syllogism.

@p
When A may belong to every B, and B to every C, there will be a complete syllogism that A may belong to every C. This is plain from the definition, for this is what we meant by "may belong to every". Likewise if A may belong to no B and B to every C, it follows that A may belong to no C. For "A is not possible of what B is possible of" meant that none of the things that may fall under B is left out. But when A may belong to every B and B may belong to no C, no syllogism comes about through the premises taken; but when BC is converted, as a premise of the possible, the same syllogism comes about as before. For since B may belong to no C, it may also belong to every C, as was said before. So if B may belong to every C, and A to every B, the same syllogism comes about again. It is likewise if the denial, with "possible", is attached to both premises: I mean, for instance, if A may belong to no B, and B to no C. No syllogism comes about through the premises taken; but when they are converted, there will again be the same one as before. It is plain, then, that when the denial is attached to the minor term, or to both premises, either no syllogism comes about, or one comes about but not a complete one, since the necessity is concluded from the conversion.

@p
@greek Ἐὰν δʼ ἡ μὲν καθόλου τῶν προτάσεων ἡ δʼ ἐν μέρει ληφθῇ
If one premise is taken as universal and the other as particular, then when the universal is placed with the major term there will be a complete syllogism. If A may belong to every B, and B to some C, A may belong to some C; this is plain from the definition of "possible". Again, if A may belong to no B, and B may belong to some C, A must be possible not to belong to some of the Cs; the proof is the same. If the particular premise is taken as negative and the universal as affirmative, with their positions placed likewise (for instance A may belong to every B, and B may not belong to some C), no plain syllogism comes about through the premises taken; but when the particular is converted, and B is laid down as possibly belonging to some C, the conclusion will be the same as before, as in the cases at the start.

@p
@greek Ἐὰν δʼ ἡ πρὸς τὸ μεῖζον ἄκρον ἐν μέρει ληφθῇ
If the premise with the major term is taken as particular and the one with the minor as universal, whether both are laid down as affirmative, or negative, or not of the same form, or both indefinite or particular, there will be no syllogism in any way. For nothing prevents B from reaching beyond A and not being predicated of an equal range. Let C be that part by which B reaches beyond A. To this A may belong neither to all, nor to none, nor to some, nor not to some, since premises of the possible convert, and B may belong to more things than A may. It is plain also from terms, for with the premises so related the first term is both not possible for any of the last and necessarily belongs to all of it. Terms common to all, for belonging of necessity: animal, white, man; for not being possible: animal, white, garment. It is plain, then, that with the terms so related no syllogism comes about. For every syllogism is of belonging, of necessity, or of the possible. It is plain that there is none of belonging or of necessity, since the affirmative is refuted by the negative example and the negative by the affirmative. It remains that it is of the possible; but this is impossible, for it was shown that with the terms so related the first term necessarily belongs to all of the last, and may belong to none. So there could not be a syllogism of the possible, since the necessary was not possible.
!flag so that as predicates they cover unequal areas | Jenkinson's "areas" is a picture of his own; the Greek says only that B need not be "predicated on equal terms" (ἐπ᾿ ἴσων), that is, of the same range of things as A.

@p
@greek Φανερὸν δὲ ὅτι καθόλου τῶν ὅρων ὄντων ἐν ταῖς ἐνδεχομέναις
It is plain that when the terms in premises of the possible are universal, a syllogism always comes about in the first figure, whether they are affirmative or negative; but when they are affirmative it is complete, and when negative incomplete. "Possible" must be taken not as it is said of necessary things, but in the sense defined. Sometimes this goes unnoticed.

@ch APr.I.15 | One premise of belonging and one possible, in the first figure
If the major premise is possible and the minor of belonging, the syllogisms are complete and conclude to the possible. If the minor is possible, they are incomplete and are proved through the impossible, which needs a principle: if B follows from A, then if A is possible, B is possible. Universal premises must be taken without limit of time.

@p
If one premise is taken as belonging and the other as possible, then when the premise with the major term signifies the possible, all the syllogisms will be complete and of the possible in the sense defined. When it is the one with the minor, they will all be incomplete, and the negative syllogisms will not be of the possible in the sense defined, but of "belongs of necessity to none" or "does not belong of necessity to all"; for if something belongs of necessity to none or not to all, we say it is possible that it belongs to none or not to all. Let A be possible for every B, and let B be laid down as belonging to every C. Since C is under B, and A is possible for every B, it is plain that A is possible for every C too. So a complete syllogism comes about. It is likewise if the premise AB is negative and BC affirmative, the first taken as possible and the second as belonging: there will be a complete syllogism that A may belong to no C.

@p
@greek Ὅτι μὲν οὖν τοῦ ὑπάρχειν τιθεμένου
It is plain, then, that when belonging is laid down with the minor term, complete syllogisms come about. That there will be syllogisms when it is the other way round must be shown through the impossible; and at the same time it will be clear that they are incomplete, since the proof is not from the premises taken. First we must say that if B must be when A is, then B will be possible when A is possible. Suppose, with things so related, that what A stands for is possible and what B stands for is impossible. If the possible, when it is possible to be, might come about, and the impossible, when it is impossible, could not come about, and if A were possible and B at the same time impossible, then A could come about without B; and if come about, then be, since what has come about, when it has come about, is. We must take the impossible and possible not only in coming to be, but also in being true and in belonging, and in all the other ways in which "possible" is said; for it will be likewise in all of them. Further, "if A is, B is" must not be understood as if B will be because some one thing, A, is. Nothing follows of necessity from one thing being so, but from two at the least, as when the premises are related as was said for the syllogism. If C is predicated of D, and D of F, C is also of necessity predicated of F; and if each is possible, the conclusion too is possible. So if one laid down A as the premises and B as the conclusion, it would follow not only that if A is necessary B is necessary too, but also that if A is possible, B is possible.
!note This principle, that what follows from the possible is itself possible, is the ground of all of Aristotle's proofs of the modal syllogisms by the impossible.

@p
@greek Τούτου δὲ δειχθέντος
Once this is shown, it is plain that if something false but not impossible is supposed, what follows because of the supposition will also be false but not impossible. If A is false but not impossible, and B is when A is, B too will be false but not impossible. For since it has been shown that if B is when A is, B will be possible when A is possible, and A is supposed to be possible, B will be possible; for if it were impossible, the same thing would be at once possible and impossible.

@p
@greek Διωρισμένων δὴ τούτων ὑπαρχέτω
With these things defined, let A belong to every B, and let B be possible for every C. Then A must be possible for every C. For let it not be possible, and let B be laid down as belonging to every C: this is false, but not impossible. If A is not possible for every C, and B belongs to every C, A is not possible for every B, for a syllogism comes about through the third figure. But it was supposed that A may belong to every B. So A must be possible for every C; for when something false and not impossible was laid down, what follows is impossible. It is also possible to reach the impossible through the first figure, laying down that B belongs to C. For if B belongs to every C, and A may belong to every B, A too might belong to every C. But it was supposed that it may not belong to all.

@p
@greek Δεῖ δὲ λαμβάνειν τὸ παντὶ ὑπάρχον μὴ κατὰ χρόνον
"Belonging to every" must be taken not with a limit of time, such as "now" or "at this time", but without qualification; for it is through such premises that we make syllogisms. If the premise is taken as true now, there will be no syllogism. Nothing perhaps prevents man from belonging at some time to everything in motion, if nothing else were moving; and what is in motion may belong to every horse; but man may belong to no horse. Further, let the first term be animal, the middle in motion, and the last man. The premises will be related likewise, but the conclusion is necessary, not possible, since man is of necessity an animal. It is plain, then, that the universal must be taken without qualification, and not limited by time.
!note A premise true only "now" may be true by accident, as "everything moving is a man" might be if, at some moment, only men were moving. Aristotle requires that a universal premise hold without any such limit.

@p
@greek Πάλιν ἔστω στερητικὴ πρότασις καθόλου
Again, let the premise AB be a universal negative: let A be taken to belong to no B, and let B be possible for every C. With these laid down, A must be possible for no C. For let it not be possible, and let B be laid down as belonging to C, as before. Then A must belong to some B, for a syllogism comes about through the third figure; and this is impossible. So A might belong to no C, for when the false was laid down the result was impossible. This syllogism, then, is not of the possible in the sense defined, but of "belongs of necessity to none", since this is the contradictory of the supposition made: it was laid down that A belongs of necessity to some C, and the syllogism through the impossible is of the opposite statement. Further, it is plain from terms that the conclusion will not be possible. Let A be raven, B thinking, and C man. A belongs to no B, for nothing that thinks is a raven. B is possible for every C, for thinking is possible for every man. But A belongs of necessity to no C, so the conclusion is not possible. Nor is it always necessary. Let A be moving, B knowledge, and C man. A will belong to no B, and B is possible for every C; and the conclusion will not be necessary, for it is not necessary that no man moves; rather, it is not necessary that any man does. It is clear, then, that the conclusion is of "belongs of necessity to none". The terms should be taken better.
!note The modern version keeps Aristotle's own terms: knowledge does not move (the soul's knowing is not a motion), and every man may come to have knowledge; yet it is neither necessary nor impossible that a man is moving. Aristotle adds that "the terms should be taken better".

@p
@greek Ἐὰν δὲ τὸ στερητικὸν τεθῇ πρὸς τὸ ἔλαττον ἄκρον
If the negative, signifying the possible, is laid down with the minor term, there will be no syllogism from the premises taken themselves, but there will be when the premise of the possible is converted, as in the earlier cases. Let A belong to every B, and let B be possible for no C. With the terms so related nothing will be necessary; but if BC is converted and B is taken to be possible for every C, a syllogism comes about as before, since the terms are placed likewise. It is the same way when both relations are negative, if AB signifies "does not belong" and BC "possible for none". Nothing necessary comes about at all through the premises taken; but when the premise of the possible is converted, there will be a syllogism. Let A be taken to belong to no B, and B to be possible for no C. Nothing is necessary through these; but if B is taken to be possible for every C, which is true, and the premise AB stays as it was, the same syllogism will come about again. But if B is laid down as not belonging to every C, and not as possibly not belonging, there will be no syllogism in any way, whether the premise AB is negative or affirmative. Common terms for belonging of necessity: white, animal, snow; for not being possible: white, animal, pitch. It is plain, then, that when the terms are universal, and one premise is taken as belonging and the other as possible, there is always a syllogism when the premise with the minor term is taken as possible; only sometimes it comes from the premises themselves, sometimes when the premise is converted. When each of these happens, and for what reason, we have said.

@p
@greek Ἐὰν δὲ τὸ μὲν καθόλου τὸ δʼ ἐν μέρει ληφθῇ τῶν διαστημάτων
If one of the relations is taken as universal and the other as particular, then when the one with the major term is laid down as universal and possible, whether negative or affirmative, and the particular as affirmative and belonging, there will be a complete syllogism, just as when the terms are universal; the proof is the same as before. When the premise with the major term is universal, but of belonging and not possible, and the other is particular and possible, then whether both are laid down as negative or affirmative, or one negative and one affirmative, there will in every case be an incomplete syllogism. Only, some will be shown through the impossible, and others also through the conversion of the possible premise, as in the earlier cases. There will be a syllogism through conversion when the universal premise with the major term signifies belonging, and the particular one, being negative, takes "possible": for instance if A belongs or does not belong to every B, and B may not belong to some C; for when BC is converted as a premise of the possible, a syllogism comes about. But when the premise laid down as particular takes "does not belong", there will be no syllogism. Terms for belonging: white, animal, snow; for not belonging: white, animal, pitch; for the proof must be taken through the indefinite. If the universal is laid down with the minor term and the particular with the major, whether negative or affirmative, possible or of belonging, either one, there will be no syllogism in any way. Nor when the premises are laid down as particular or indefinite, whether taking "possible" or "belongs" or one of each; not so either. The proof is the same as in the earlier cases. Common terms for belonging of necessity: animal, white, man; for not being possible: animal, white, garment. It is plain, then, that when the universal is laid down with the major term there is always a syllogism, and when with the minor, never any at all.

@ch APr.I.16 | One premise necessary and one possible, in the first figure
With one premise necessary and the other possible, the syllogisms follow the same pattern. The conclusion is of the possible, except that when the necessary premise is a universal negative, it also follows that the predicate does not belong. There is never a conclusion that something of necessity does not belong.

@p
When one premise signifies belonging of necessity and the other being possible, there will be a syllogism with the terms related in the same way, and it will be complete when the necessary premise is laid down with the minor term. The conclusion, when the terms are affirmative, will be of the possible and not of belonging, whether they are laid down universally or not. If one is affirmative and the other negative, then when the affirmative is necessary, the conclusion will be of the possible and not of not belonging; when the negative is necessary, it will be both of possibly not belonging and of not belonging, whether the terms are universal or not. "Possible" in the conclusion must be taken in the same way as before. There will be no syllogism of "of necessity does not belong", for "does not belong of necessity" and "of necessity does not belong" are different.
!note "Does not belong of necessity" says only that the belonging is not necessary; "of necessity does not belong" says that it is impossible for it to belong. The place of "necessity" in the sentence changes the claim.

@p
@greek Ὅτι μὲν οὖν καταφατικῶν ὄντων
That when the terms are affirmative the conclusion does not become necessary is plain. Let A belong of necessity to every B, and let B be possible for every C. There will be an incomplete syllogism that A may belong to every C; that it is incomplete is clear from the proof, for it will be shown in the same way as before. Again, let A be possible for every B, and let B belong of necessity to every C. There will be a syllogism that A may belong to every C, but not that it belongs; and it is complete, not incomplete, since it is completed at once through the premises taken at the start. If the premises are not of the same form, first let the negative be necessary: let A be possible for no B, and let B be possible for every C. Then A must belong to no C. For let it be laid down to belong to every C or to some; but it was supposed to be possible for no B. Since the negative converts, neither is B possible for any A; and A is laid down to belong to every C or to some; so B could belong to no C or not to every C. But it was supposed at the start to be possible for every C. It is plain that a syllogism of "may not belong" also comes about, since one of "does not belong" does. Again, let the affirmative premise be necessary: let A be possible for no B, and let B belong of necessity to every C. The syllogism will be complete, but of "may not belong", not of "does not belong"; for the premise with the major term was taken so, and it cannot be brought to the impossible. If it were supposed that A belongs to some C, and it is laid down that A may belong to no B, nothing impossible follows through these. If the negative is laid down with the minor term, then when it signifies the possible there will be a syllogism through conversion, as in the earlier cases; when not, there will not. Nor when both are laid down as negative and the one with the minor is not possible. The terms are the same: for belonging, white, animal, snow; for not belonging, white, animal, pitch.

@p
@greek Τὸν αὐτὸν δὲ τρόπον ἕξει κἀπὶ τῶν ἐν μέρει συλλογισμῶν. ὅταν μὲν γὰρ ᾖ τὸ στερητικὸν
It will be the same way with particular syllogisms. When the negative is necessary, the conclusion too will be of not belonging. If A is possible for no B, and B may belong to some C, A must not belong to some C. For if it belongs to every C, and is possible for no B, neither is B possible for any A; so if A belongs to every C, B is possible for no C. But it was supposed to be possible for some. When the particular affirmative in the negative syllogism is necessary, such as BC, or the universal in the affirmative syllogism, such as AB, there will be no syllogism of belonging. The proof is the same as before. If the universal is laid down with the minor term, affirmative or negative, as possible, and the particular as necessary, there will be no syllogism. Terms for belonging of necessity: animal, white, man; for not being possible: animal, white, garment. When the universal is necessary and the particular possible, and the universal is negative, terms for belonging: animal, white, raven; for not belonging: animal, white, pitch; when it is affirmative, for belonging: animal, white, swan; for not being possible: animal, white, snow. Nor will there be a syllogism when the premises are taken as indefinite, or both as particular. Common terms for belonging: animal, white, man; for not belonging: animal, white, inanimate. For animal belongs to some white thing, and white to some inanimate thing, both necessarily and not possibly; and likewise with the possible; so the terms are useful for all the cases.

@p
@greek Φανερὸν οὖν ἐκ τῶν εἰρημένων ὅτι ὁμοίως ἐχόντων
It is plain from what has been said, then, that with the terms related likewise, a syllogism comes about or does not both with belonging and with necessary premises; except that when the negative premise was laid down as belonging, the syllogism was of the possible, and when the negative was laid down as necessary, it was both of the possible and of not belonging. It is clear too that all the syllogisms are incomplete, and that they are completed through the figures named before.

@ch APr.I.17 | Two possible premises in the second figure
In the second figure two possible premises give no syllogism. The universal negative of the possible does not convert: "white may belong to no man" is true, but "man may belong to nothing white" is not, since man of necessity does not belong to many white things.

@p
In the second figure, when both premises take "possible", there will be no syllogism, whether they are laid down as affirmative or negative, universal or particular. When one signifies belonging and the other being possible, there will never be one if the affirmative signifies belonging, but always if the universal negative does. It is the same way when one premise is taken as necessary and the other as possible. In these too "possible" in the conclusions must be taken as before.

@p
@greek Πρῶτον οὖν δεικτέον ὅτι οὐκ ἀντιστρέφει
First we must show that the negative of the possible does not convert: if A may belong to no B, it is not necessary that B may belong to no A. Let this be laid down, and let B be possible for no A. Since affirmations of the possible convert into denials, both the contraries and the opposites, and B may belong to no A, it is plain that B might also belong to every A. But this is false; for if this may belong to all of that, it is not necessary that that belongs to all of this. So the negative does not convert. Further, nothing prevents A from being possible for no B while B of necessity does not belong to some A. White, for instance, may not belong to any man (since it may also belong); but it is not true to say that man may belong to nothing white, since man of necessity does not belong to many white things, and the necessary was not possible.

@p
@greek Ἀλλὰ μὴν οὐδʼ ἐκ τοῦ ἀδυνάτου
Nor will it be shown by the impossible that it converts. Suppose one claimed that since it is false that B may belong to no A, it is true that it is not possible for it to belong to none, for these are affirmation and denial; and if so, it is true that it belongs of necessity to some A; so A too belongs to some B; and this is impossible. But if B is not possible for none of A, it is not necessary that it belongs to some. "Not possible for none" is said in two ways: if it belongs of necessity to some, and if it of necessity does not belong to some. For it is not true to say that what of necessity does not belong to some of the As may not belong to all of them, just as it is not true to say that what of necessity belongs to some may belong to all. So if someone claimed that since C is not possible for every D, it of necessity does not belong to some D, he would take something false: it belongs to every D, but because it belongs of necessity to some, we say it is not possible for all. So both "belongs of necessity to some" and "of necessity does not belong to some" are opposed to "may belong to all"; and likewise to "may belong to none". It is clear, then, that in relation to what is possible and not possible in the way we first defined, we must take not "belongs of necessity to some" but "of necessity does not belong to some". When this is taken, nothing impossible follows, so no syllogism comes about. It is plain from what has been said, then, that the negative does not convert.
!note The point is subtle. "B is possible for no A" is false if B belongs of necessity to some A, and also false if B of necessity does not belong to some A. So its denial does not give one definite premise to argue from, and the reduction to the impossible fails.

@p
@greek Τούτου δὲ δειχθέντος κείσθω
With this shown, let A be laid down as possible for no B and for every C. There will be no syllogism through conversion, for it has been said that such a premise does not convert. Nor through the impossible: for if it is laid down that B is not possible for every C, nothing false follows, since A might belong both to all and to none of C. In general, if there is a syllogism, it is clear that it would be of the possible, since neither premise was taken as belonging; and this would be either affirmative or negative. But it can be neither. If it is laid down as affirmative, it will be shown through terms that it is not possible for it to belong; if as negative, that the conclusion is not possible but necessary. Let A be white, B man, and C horse. A, white, may belong to all of one and to none of the other. But B may neither belong nor not belong to C. That it may not belong is plain, for no horse is a man. Nor may it possibly not belong, for it is necessary that no horse is a man, and the necessary was not possible. So no syllogism comes about. It will be shown likewise if the negative is laid down the other way round, and if both are taken as affirmative or as negative, for the proof will be through the same terms; and when one is universal and the other particular, or both particular or indefinite, or in whatever other ways the premises can be changed, the proof will always be through the same terms. It is plain, then, that when both premises are laid down as possible, no syllogism comes about.

@ch APr.I.18 | One premise of belonging and one possible, in the second figure
If the affirmative premise is of belonging and the negative possible, there is no syllogism; if the affirmative is possible and the negative of belonging, there is one, by converting the negative. Two affirmatives give none.

@p
If one premise signifies belonging and the other being possible, then when the affirmative is laid down as belonging and the negative as possible, there will never be a syllogism, whether the terms are taken universally or in part; the proof is the same and through the same terms. But when the affirmative is possible and the negative belonging, there will be a syllogism. Let A be taken to belong to no B and to be possible for every C. When the negative is converted, B will belong to no A; and A was possible for every C; so a syllogism comes about through the first figure that B may belong to no C. It is likewise if the negative is laid down with C. If both are negative, one signifying "does not belong" and the other "possible", nothing necessary follows through the premises taken themselves; but when the premise of the possible is converted, a syllogism comes about that B may belong to no C, as before, for there will again be the first figure. If both are laid down as affirmative, there will be no syllogism. Terms for belonging: health, animal, man; for not belonging: health, horse, man.

@p
@greek Τὸν αὐτὸν δὲ τρόπον ἕξει κἀπὶ τῶν ἐν μέρει συλλογισμῶν. ὅταν μὲν γὰρ ᾖ τὸ καταφατικὸν
It will be the same way with particular syllogisms. When the affirmative is of belonging, whether taken universally or in part, there will be no syllogism (this is shown likewise, and through the same terms as before); when the negative is, there will be one, through conversion, as before. Again, if both relations are taken as negative, and "does not belong" is universal, there will be no necessary conclusion from the premises themselves, but there will be a syllogism when the possible is converted, as before. But if the negative is of belonging and taken in part, there will be no syllogism, whether the other premise is affirmative or negative. Nor when both are taken as indefinite, affirmative or negative, or as particular. The proof is the same and through the same terms.

@ch APr.I.19 | One premise necessary and one possible, in the second figure
If the negative premise is necessary, there is a syllogism both that the predicate may not belong and that it does not belong; if the affirmative is necessary, there is none.

@p
If one premise signifies belonging of necessity and the other being possible, then when the negative is necessary there will be a syllogism, not only that it may not belong but also that it does not belong; but when the affirmative is necessary, there will not. Let A be laid down as belonging of necessity to no B and as possible for every C. When the negative is converted, neither will B belong to any A; and A was possible for every C; so a syllogism comes about again, through the first figure, that B may belong to no C. At the same time it is clear that B will not belong to any C either. For let it be laid down to belong. Then if A is possible for no B, and B belongs to some C, A is not possible for some C; but it was supposed to be possible for every C. It will be shown in the same way if the negative is laid down with C.

@p
@greek Πάλιν ἔστω τὸ κατηγορικὸν
Again, let the affirmative be necessary and the other possible: let A be possible for no B, and let it belong of necessity to every C. With the terms so related there will be no syllogism, for it turns out that B of necessity does not belong to C. Let A be white, B man, and C swan. White belongs of necessity to swan, and may belong to no man; and man of necessity belongs to no swan. That there is no syllogism of the possible is plain, for the necessary was not possible. Nor is there one of the necessary, for the necessary followed either from two necessary premises or from a necessary negative. Further, with these laid down, it is also possible for B to belong to C. For nothing prevents C from being under B, and A from being possible for every B and belonging of necessity to C: for instance, if C were awake, B animal, and A motion. Motion belongs of necessity to what is awake, and is possible for every animal, and everything awake is an animal. It is plain, then, that there is no syllogism of not belonging either, since with the terms so related it is necessary that it belongs; nor of the opposite affirmations. So there will be no syllogism. It will be shown likewise if the affirmative is laid down the other way round.

@p
@greek Ἐὰν δʼ ὁμοιοσχήμονες ὦσιν αἱ προτάσεις
If the premises are of the same form, when they are negative a syllogism always comes about when the premise of the possible is converted, as before. Let A be taken to belong of necessity to no B, and to be possible not to belong to C. When the premises are converted, B belongs to no A, and A is possible for every C; so the first figure comes about. It is the same if the negative is laid down with C. But if they are laid down as affirmative, there will be no syllogism. That there will be none of "does not belong" or "of necessity does not belong" is plain, since no negative premise was taken, either of belonging or of belonging of necessity. Nor will there be one of "may not belong", since with the terms so related B will of necessity not belong to C: for instance if A were laid down as white, B as swan, and C as man. Nor of the opposite affirmations, since it has been shown that B of necessity does not belong to C. So no syllogism comes about at all.

@p
@greek Ὁμοίως δʼ ἕξει κἀπὶ τῶν ἐν μέρει συλλογισμῶν
It will be likewise with particular syllogisms. When the negative is universal and necessary, there will always be a syllogism both of the possible and of not belonging (the proof is through conversion); when the affirmative is, never, for it will be shown in the same way as in the universal cases, and through the same terms. Nor when both are taken as affirmative, for the proof of this too is the same as before. When both are negative, and the one signifying "does not belong" is universal and necessary, there will be no necessary conclusion through the premises taken themselves; but when the premise of the possible is converted there will be a syllogism, as before. If both are laid down as indefinite or particular, there will be no syllogism; the proof is the same and through the same terms. It is plain from what has been said, then, that when the universal negative is laid down as necessary, there is always a syllogism, not only that it may not belong but also that it does not belong; and when the affirmative is, never. It is plain too that with the terms related in the same way, a syllogism comes about or does not both with necessary premises and with premises of belonging. It is clear also that all the syllogisms are incomplete, and that they are completed through the figures named before.

@ch APr.I.20 | Possible premises in the third figure
In the third figure there is a syllogism both when both premises are possible and when one is. With two possible premises, or one possible and one of belonging, the conclusion is possible; with a necessary negative premise, it is that the predicate does not belong.

@p
In the last figure there will be a syllogism both when both premises are possible and when one is. When the premises signify being possible, the conclusion too will be possible; and also when one signifies being possible and the other belonging. When one is laid down as necessary, if it is affirmative the conclusion will be neither necessary nor of belonging; if negative, there will be a syllogism of not belonging, as before. In these too the possible in the conclusions must be taken likewise.

@p
@greek Ἔστωσαν δὴ πρῶτον ἐνδεχόμεναι
First let the premises be possible, and let A and B both be possible for every C. Since the affirmative converts in part, and B is possible for every C, C too might be possible for some B. So if A is possible for every C, and C for some B, A must also be possible for some B, for the first figure comes about. And if A may belong to no C, and B to every C, A must be possible not to belong to some B, for there will again be the first figure through conversion. If both are laid down as negative, there will be no necessary conclusion from the premises taken themselves, but when the premises are converted there will be a syllogism, as before. For if A and B may not belong to C, then when "may belong" is substituted there will again be the first figure through conversion. If one of the terms is universal and the other particular, there will be a syllogism or not with the terms related in the same way as with belonging. Let A be possible for every C, and B belong to some C. There will again be the first figure when the particular premise is converted: if A is possible for every C, and C for some B, A is possible for some B. It is the same if the universal is laid down with BC. It is likewise if AC is negative and BC affirmative, for there will again be the first figure through conversion. If both are laid down as negative, one universal and one particular, there will be no syllogism through the premises taken themselves, but there will be when they are converted, as before. When both are taken as indefinite or particular, there will be no syllogism, for A must belong both to every B and to none. Terms for belonging: animal, man, white; for not belonging: horse, man, white; the middle is white.

@ch APr.I.21 | One premise of belonging and one possible, in the third figure
With one premise of belonging and one possible, the third figure concludes that the predicate may belong, not that it does; with a universal affirmative and a particular negative the proof is through the impossible.

@p
If one premise signifies belonging and the other being possible, the conclusion will be that it may belong, not that it belongs; and there will be a syllogism with the terms related in the same way as before. First let them be affirmative: let A belong to every C, and B be possible for every C. When BC is converted there will be the first figure, and the conclusion that A may belong to some B; for when one of the premises in the first figure signified being possible, the conclusion too was possible. It is likewise if BC is of belonging and AC possible; and if AC is negative and BC affirmative, whichever of them is of belonging, the conclusion will in both cases be possible. For the first figure comes about again, and it has been shown that when one of the premises in it signifies being possible, the conclusion too will be possible. If the negative is laid down with the minor term, or both are taken as negative, there will be no syllogism through the premises laid down themselves, but there will be when they are converted, as before.

@p
@greek Εἰ δʼ ἡ μὲν καθόλου τῶν προτάσεων ἡ δʼ ἐν μέρει
If one of the premises is universal and the other particular, when both are affirmative, or the universal negative and the particular affirmative, the syllogisms will be made in the same way, for all are concluded through the first figure. So it is plain that the syllogism will be of the possible and not of belonging. But if the affirmative is universal and the negative particular, the proof will be through the impossible. Let B belong to every C, and let A be possible not to belong to some C. Then A must be possible not to belong to some B. For if A belongs of necessity to every B, and B is laid down to belong to every C, A will belong of necessity to every C, for this has been shown before. But it was supposed that it may not belong to some C. When both are taken as indefinite or particular, there will be no syllogism. The proof is the same as before, and through the same terms.

@ch APr.I.22 | One premise necessary and one possible, in the third figure
With one premise necessary and one possible, the third figure gives a conclusion of the possible when both are affirmative or the affirmative is necessary; when the negative is necessary, also that the predicate does not belong. All these syllogisms are incomplete and completed through the first figure.

@p
If one premise is necessary and the other possible, then when the terms are affirmative there will always be a syllogism of the possible. When one is affirmative and the other negative, if the affirmative is necessary, it will be of "may not belong"; if the negative is, both of "may not belong" and of "does not belong". There will be no syllogism of "of necessity does not belong", just as in the other figures. First let the terms be affirmative: let A belong of necessity to every C, and let B be possible for every C. Since A of necessity belongs to every C, and C is possible for some B, A too will be possible for some B, and will not simply belong to it; for so it came out in the first figure. It will be shown likewise if BC is laid down as necessary and AC as possible. Again, let one be affirmative and the other negative, and the affirmative necessary: let A be possible for no C, and let B belong of necessity to every C. There will again be the first figure, for the negative premise signifies the possible. It is plain, then, that the conclusion will be possible, since when the premises were so related in the first figure the conclusion too was possible. But if the negative premise is necessary, the conclusion will be both that A may not belong to some B and that it does not belong. Let A be laid down as of necessity not belonging to C, and B as possible for every C. When the affirmative BC is converted, there will be the first figure, and the negative premise is necessary. When the premises were so related, it followed both that A may not belong to some C and that it does not belong; so A too must not belong to some B. When the negative is laid down with the minor term, if it is possible there will be a syllogism when the premise is changed, as before; if it is necessary there will not, for the predicate both necessarily belongs to all and may belong to none. Terms for belonging to all: sleep, sleeping horse, man; for belonging to none: sleep, waking horse, man.
!note "Sleep, sleeping horse, man": sleep belongs of necessity to every sleeping horse, and man of necessity to none; so the major premise "sleep belongs to every sleeping horse" is necessary, and nothing about men follows.

@p
@greek Ὁμοίως δʼ ἕξει καὶ εἰ ὁ μὲν καθόλου
It will be likewise if one of the terms is universal in relation to the middle and the other particular. When both are affirmative there will be a syllogism of the possible and not of belonging; also when one is taken as negative and the other affirmative, and the affirmative is necessary. When the negative is necessary, the conclusion too will be of not belonging; for the way of proof will be the same whether the terms are universal or not, since the syllogisms must be completed through the first figure, so that the same results must follow in these as in those. When the negative, taken universally, is laid down with the minor term, there will be a syllogism through conversion if it is possible, but not if it is necessary. It will be shown in the same way as in the universal cases, and through the same terms. It is plain, then, in this figure too, when and how there will be a syllogism, and when it will be of the possible and when of belonging. It is clear also that all are incomplete, and that they are completed through the first figure.

@ch APr.I.23 | Every syllogism is made in the three figures
Every proof shows that something belongs or does not, universally or in part, either directly or from a hypothesis. To prove A of B one needs a middle term related to both, and there are only three ways of relating it: these are the three figures. Proofs through the impossible, and other proofs from hypotheses, also syllogize in them. So every syllogism is completed through the first figure.

@p
That the syllogisms in these figures are completed through the universal syllogisms of the first figure and brought back to them is clear from what has been said. That every syllogism without exception is so will be plain now, when it has been shown that every one comes about through one of these figures. Every demonstration and every syllogism must prove either that something belongs or that it does not, and this either universally or in part, and further either directly or from a hypothesis. Proof through the impossible is a part of proof from a hypothesis. First, then, let us speak of the direct ones; once these are shown, it will be plain for those that lead to the impossible, and for those from a hypothesis in general.
!note "Directly" renders δεικτικῶς, "by showing": a proof that reaches its conclusion straight from the premises, as against one that supposes the opposite and refutes it.

@p
@greek Εἰ δὴ δέοι τὸ Α κατὰ τοῦ Β
If one had to prove by syllogism that A belongs or does not belong to B, one must take something predicated of something. If A were taken of B, what was to be proved at the start would have been assumed. If A is taken of C, but C of nothing, and nothing else of C, and nothing else of A, there will be no syllogism, for nothing follows of necessity from one thing being taken of one other. So another premise must be taken as well. If A is taken of something else, or something else of A, or something else of C, nothing prevents there being a syllogism, but it will not be about B through what has been taken. Nor, when C is taken of something else, and that of another, and that of another, but the chain does not join on to B, will there be a syllogism about B in this way either. For we said in general that there will never be any syllogism of one thing about another unless some middle term is taken which is related to each by predication. The syllogism without qualification is from premises; the syllogism about this, from premises about this; and the syllogism of this in relation to that, through premises of this in relation to that. It is impossible to take a premise about B if one neither predicates anything of it nor denies anything of it; or again to get a syllogism of A about B if one takes nothing common to them, but predicates or denies things proper to each. So something must be taken as a middle for both, which will join the predications, if there is to be a syllogism of this in relation to that. If, then, one must take something common to both, and this can be done in three ways (by predicating A of C and C of B, or C of both, or both of C), and these are the figures named, it is plain that every syllogism must come about through one of these figures. The account is the same if the joining to B is through more terms, for the figure will be the same with many.

@p
@greek Ὅτι μὲν οὖν οἱ δεικτικοὶ περαίνονται
It is plain, then, that direct syllogisms are concluded through the figures named. That those leading to the impossible are too will be clear from this. All who conclude through the impossible prove the false by syllogism, and prove the original point from a hypothesis, when something impossible follows from laying down its contradictory. For instance, they prove that the diagonal is incommensurable because, if it is laid down as commensurable, odd numbers turn out equal to even ones. That odd numbers become equal to even ones is concluded by syllogism; that the diagonal is incommensurable is proved from a hypothesis, since a falsehood follows because of the contradictory. This is what reasoning through the impossible was: proving something impossible because of the hypothesis made at the start. So since, in reductions to the impossible, there comes about a direct syllogism of the false, and the original point is proved from a hypothesis, and we said before that direct syllogisms are concluded through these figures, it is plain that syllogisms through the impossible too will be through these figures. It is the same with all the others from a hypothesis. In all of them the syllogism comes about in relation to what is substituted, and the original point is concluded through an agreement or some other hypothesis. If this is true, every demonstration and every syllogism must come about through the three figures named. And this shown, it is clear that every syllogism is completed through the first figure and brought back to the universal syllogisms in it.
!note The incommensurability of the diagonal of a square with its side was the classic proof by the impossible: if the two had a common measure, one and the same number would have to be both odd and even. Plato tells the story of its discovery in the Theaetetus (147d).

@ch APr.I.24 | Every syllogism needs a universal and an affirmative premise
In every syllogism one premise must be affirmative and one universal; otherwise there is no syllogism, or it is not about the point in question, or it begs the question. A universal conclusion needs universal premises. One premise must be like the conclusion in quality and in mode.

@p
Further, in every syllogism one of the terms must be affirmative and must belong universally. Without the universal either there will be no syllogism, or it will not be about the point laid down, or it will assume the original point. Let it be laid down that musical pleasure is good. If one claimed that pleasure is good, without adding "every", there will be no syllogism. If one claimed that some pleasure is good, then if it is some other pleasure, it is nothing to the point laid down; if it is this very one, he assumes the original point.

@p
@greek μᾶλλον δὲ γίνεται φανερὸν ἐν τοῖς διαγράμμασιν
It becomes plainer in geometrical proofs, for instance that the angles at the base of an isosceles triangle are equal. Let the lines A and B be drawn to the centre. If one assumed that the angle AC is equal to BD without claiming in general that the angles of semicircles are equal; and again that C is equal to D without taking in addition every angle of a segment; and further that, since the whole angles are equal and the angles taken away are equal, the remaining angles E and F are equal, he will beg the original point, unless he assumes that when equals are taken from equals, equals remain. It is plain, then, that in every syllogism there must be a universal premise; and that a universal conclusion is proved only from terms that are all universal, while a particular one is proved both in this way and in the other. So if the conclusion is universal, the terms too must be universal; but if the terms are universal, the conclusion may not be universal.
!note The diagram is of an isosceles triangle with its apex at the centre of a circle and its base a chord. Aristotle's point is that the proof needs universal principles ("all angles of semicircles are equal", "equals taken from equals leave equals"); the particular equality of these angles cannot be assumed without assuming what was to be proved.

@p
@greek δῆλον δὲ καὶ ὅτι ἐν ἅπαντι συλλογισμῷ
It is clear too that in every syllogism both premises, or one of them, must be like the conclusion. I mean not only in being affirmative or negative, but also in being necessary, of belonging, or possible. The other forms of predication must also be considered. It is plain too, in general, when there will be a syllogism and when not, when it is possible and when complete, and that if there is a syllogism, the terms must be related in one of the ways stated.
!note "Possible" (δυνατός) here means a valid but incomplete syllogism, as in chapter 5: one that can be had, though only with an extra step.

@ch APr.I.25 | Every syllogism has three terms and two premises
Every proof is through three terms and no more; other terms either make further syllogisms or are idle. So a syllogism has two premises. In a chain of syllogisms the terms are always one more than the premises, and the conclusions multiply faster than either.

@p
It is clear too that every demonstration will be through three terms and no more, unless the same conclusion comes about through different sets of terms: for instance through A and B and through C and D, or through A and B and through A, C and D, since nothing prevents there being several middles for the same terms. But then there is not one syllogism but several. Or again, when each of A and B is obtained through a syllogism (A through D and E, and B through F and G), or one by induction and the other by syllogism. But in this way too the syllogisms are several, for the conclusions are several, A and B and C.

@p
@greek Εἰ δʼ οὖν μὴ πλείους ἀλλʼ εἷς
If they are not several but one, the same conclusion may come about through more terms in this way, but not as C does through A and B. Let E be concluded from A, B, C and D. Then some of these must be taken in relation to one another, one as a whole and the other as a part, for it was shown before that if there is a syllogism, some of the terms must be so related. Let A be so related to B. Then there is a conclusion from them. It is either E, or one of C and D, or something else besides these. If it is E, the syllogism would be from A and B alone. If C and D are so related that one is as a whole and the other as a part, there will be something from them too, either E, or one of A and B, or something else. If it is E or one of A and B, either there will be several syllogisms, or it turns out that the same thing is concluded through more terms, as we said it could be. If it is something else, there will be several syllogisms not joined to one another. If C is not so related to D as to make a syllogism, they will have been taken idly, unless for the sake of induction, or concealment, or something else of the kind. If the conclusion from A and B is not E but something else, and from C and D either one of these or something else, the syllogisms are several and are not of the point supposed; for the syllogism was supposed to be of E. If no conclusion comes from C and D, it turns out that they were taken idly and that the syllogism is not of the original point. So it is plain that every demonstration and every syllogism will be through three terms only.
!note "Concealment" (κρύψις): in a debate, one may ask for premises one does not need, so that the answerer does not see where the argument is going. Topics VIII teaches the practice.

@p
@greek Τούτου δʼ ὄντος φανεροῦ
This being plain, it is clear that a syllogism is from two premises and no more, since three terms make two premises, unless something is added, as was said at the start, to complete the syllogisms. It is plain, then, that if in a syllogistic argument the premises through which the main conclusion comes about are not even in number (for some of the earlier conclusions must be premises), this argument either has not concluded or has asked more than is needed for the thesis. Taking syllogisms by their main premises, then, every syllogism will be from premises even in number and from terms odd in number, for the terms are one more than the premises. And the conclusions will be half as many as the premises. When it is concluded through prior syllogisms, or through several continuous middles (as the conclusion AB through C and D), the number of terms will likewise exceed the premises by one, since the term inserted will be placed either outside or in the middle, and either way the relations are one fewer than the terms; and the premises are equal to the relations. But the premises will not always be even and the terms odd; they will alternate. When the premises are even, the terms are odd; when the terms are even, the premises are odd. For with each term one premise is added, wherever the term is added; so since the premises were even and the terms odd, they must change over when the same addition is made. The conclusions will no longer keep the same ratio either to the terms or to the premises. When one term is added, conclusions will be added one fewer than the terms there were before; for it makes no conclusion with the last term alone, but with all the others. If D is added to A, B and C, two conclusions are added at once, the one with A and the one with B. It is likewise with the others. If it is inserted in the middle, it is the same way, for it will make no syllogism with one term only. So the conclusions will be far more than both the terms and the premises.

@ch APr.I.26 | Which conclusions are easy and which hard to prove
A universal affirmative is proved in one figure only, in one way, so it is the hardest to establish and the easiest to overthrow. A particular negative is proved in all three figures. In general it is easier to overthrow than to establish.

@p
Since we know what syllogisms are about, and what kind is proved in each figure and in how many ways, it is also plain to us what kind of problem is hard and what kind easy to attack. What is concluded in more figures and through more moods is easier; what in fewer and through fewer is harder to attack. The universal affirmative is proved through the first figure only, and in only one way. The negative is proved through the first and the middle figures: through the first in one way, through the middle in two. The particular affirmative is proved through the first and the last: in one way through the first, in three through the last. The particular negative is proved in all the figures: in one way in the first, in two in the middle, in three in the last.

@p
@greek φανερὸν οὖν ὅτι τὸ καθόλου κατηγορικόν κατασκευάσαι
It is plain, then, that the universal affirmative is the hardest to establish and the easiest to overthrow. In general, for one who refutes, universals are easier than particulars. A universal is overthrown if the predicate belongs to none or does not belong to some; and of these, "not to some" is proved in all the figures, and "to none" in two. It is the same with negatives: the original claim is overthrown both if the predicate belongs to all and if to some, and this was in two figures. With particulars there is only one way: by proving that the predicate belongs to all or to none. For one who establishes, particulars are easier, since they come in more figures and through more moods. In general we must not forget that one can overthrow through each other, universals through particulars and particulars through universals; but one cannot establish universals through particulars, though one can establish particulars through universals. At the same time it is clear that overthrowing is easier than establishing. How every syllogism comes about, then, and through how many terms and premises, and how they are related to one another; and further what kind of problem is proved in each figure, and what kind in more and in fewer figures, is clear from what has been said.

@ch APr.I.27 | How to find premises: what follows a thing and what it follows
@taylor 26
Now Aristotle turns from the theory of the syllogism to the skill of making one. Individuals are predicated of nothing, the highest kinds have nothing above them, and most arguments are about what lies between. For each term, collect its definition and properties, what follows it, what it follows, and what cannot belong to it; take what follows the whole of it, and not what follows everything.

@p
How we shall be well supplied with syllogisms for whatever is proposed, and by what road we shall find the starting points for each, must now be said. For perhaps we should not only study how syllogisms come about, but also have the power to make them. Of all the things there are, some are such that they are not truly predicated universally of anything else (like Cleon and Callias, the individual and perceptible), though other things are predicated of them (each of these is both a man and an animal). Others are themselves predicated of other things, but nothing prior is predicated of them. Others are both predicated of other things and have other things predicated of them, as man of Callias and animal of man. That some things are by nature said of nothing is clear, for nearly every perceptible thing is such as not to be predicated of anything, except incidentally; for we sometimes say "that white thing is Socrates" or "the one approaching is Callias". That in going upwards the series also stops somewhere we shall say again; for now let this be laid down. Of these highest things, no other predicate can be proved, except as a matter of belief, but they can be proved of other things; and individuals cannot be proved of other things, but other things can be proved of them. What lies between can plainly be taken both ways: they will be said of other things and other things of them. And arguments and inquiries are mostly about these.
!note Aristotle shows that the chain of predicates stops at the top in Posterior Analytics I.19 to 22. The highest predicates are the categories of the Categories, which the Platonists read first.

@p
@greek Δεῖ δὴ τὰς προτάσεις περὶ ἕκαστον
The premises about each thing must be selected in this way. First, lay down the thing itself and its definitions and whatever is proper to it; then after that, what follows the thing; and again what the thing follows; and what cannot belong to it. What it cannot belong to need not be selected, because the negative converts. Among the things that follow, we must distinguish those predicated in what the thing is, those predicated as properties, and those predicated as accidents; and of these, which are so in belief and which in truth. The more of these one has to hand, the sooner one will come upon a conclusion; the truer they are, the more one will demonstrate.
!note "What the thing is" (τὸ τί ἐστι) is its genus and differences, given in its definition; a property (ἴδιον) belongs to it alone but is not its essence, as being able to laugh belongs to man; an accident (συμβεβηκός) may belong or not, as being white. These are the "predicables" that Porphyry's Introduction, read before the Categories, sets out.

@p
@greek Δεῖ δʼ ἐκλέγειν μὴ τὰ ἑπόμενα τινί
One must select not what follows some part of the thing but what follows the whole of it: not what follows some man, but what follows every man, since the syllogism is through universal premises. When a premise is indefinite it is unclear whether it is universal; when it is made definite, this is plain. Likewise one must select what the thing follows as a whole, for the reason given. But one must not take what follows as following in its whole extent, I mean for instance every animal following man, or every science following the musical art; only that it follows, without qualification, as we also put it in our premises. The other is both useless and impossible: that every man is every animal, or justice is all good. "Every" is said of what the thing follows. When the subject whose consequents must be taken is contained in something else, what follows or does not follow the universal should not be selected among them, for it has been taken among those (whatever follows animal also follows man, and likewise with what does not belong); but what is proper to each should be taken, since there are things proper to the species beyond the genus, and other species must have things proper to them. Nor should one select, for the universal, the things the contained term follows: for instance, for animal, the things man follows. For if animal follows man, it must follow all of these too; and these belong more properly to the selection for man. One must also take what follows for the most part, and what the thing follows for the most part; for the syllogism of problems that hold for the most part is also from premises that hold for the most part, either all or some, since the conclusion of each is like its starting points. Further, what follows everything should not be selected, for there will be no syllogism from it; why will be clear in what follows.

@ch APr.I.28 | Which sets of terms give each conclusion
To prove A of every E, find something among the things A follows that is the same as something that follows E; to prove it of some E, something A follows that is the same as something E follows; to prove it of no E, something incompatible with A that follows E, or the reverse. Seeking anything else is idle. The middle must be the same term, not a different or contrary one.

@p
For those who want to establish something about a whole, they must look, for the subject, to the things under it of which it happens to be said; and for the predicate, to what follows it. If any of these is the same, the one must belong to the other. If the aim is to establish not that it belongs to every one but to some, they must look to what each of them follows; for if any of these is the same, it must belong to some. When it must belong to none, they must look, for the subject, to what follows it, and for the predicate, to what cannot be present to it; or the other way round, for the subject, to what cannot be present to it, and for the predicate, to what follows it. If any of these are the same, either way, the one cannot belong to any of the other, for there comes about sometimes the syllogism in the first figure, sometimes the one in the middle. If it must not belong to some, they must look, for the subject, to what it follows, and for the predicate, to what cannot belong to it; for if any of these is the same, it must not belong to some.

@p
@greek Μᾶλλον δʼ ἴσως ὧδ᾿ ἔσται
Perhaps each of these will be plainer in this way. Let the things that follow A be B, the things A itself follows be C, and the things that cannot belong to A be D. Again, let the things that belong to E be F, the things E follows be G, and the things that cannot belong to E be H. If one of the Cs is the same as one of the Fs, A must belong to every E; for F belongs to every E, and A to every C, so A to every E. If C and G are the same, A must belong to some E; for A follows C, and E follows every G. If F and D are the same, A will belong to no E, by a prior syllogism; for since the negative converts and F is the same as D, A will belong to no F, and F belongs to every E. Again, if B and H are the same, A will belong to no E; for B will belong to every A and to no E, since it was the same as H, and H belonged to no E. If D and G are the same, A will not belong to some E; for it will not belong to G, since it does not belong to D; and G is under E; so it will not belong to some of the Es. If B is the same as G, there will be a converted syllogism. E will belong to every A, since B belongs to A and E to B (for B was the same as G); but A need not belong to every E, though it must belong to some, because the universal predication converts into the particular.
!note A "prior syllogism" (προσυλλογισμός) is a syllogism proving one of the premises of the main one: here, the conversion of "no D is A" into "no A is D", which then serves as a premise.

@p
@greek Φανερὸν οὖν ὅτι εἰς τὰ προειρημένα βλεπτέον
It is plain, then, that for each problem we must look to the things named, on each side, since all syllogisms are through these. Among the things that follow, and the things each follows, we must look most of all to the first and most universal: for E, to KF rather than to F alone; for A, to KC rather than to C alone. For if A belongs to KF, it belongs both to F and to E; if it does not follow KF, it may still follow F. Likewise we must consider what A itself follows: if it follows the first ones, it follows those under them too; if not these, it may still follow those under them. It is clear also that the inquiry is through the three terms and the two premises, and that all the syllogisms are through the figures named. A is proved to belong to every E when something the same is taken among the Cs and the Fs. This will be the middle, and A and E the extremes; so the first figure comes about. It is proved to belong to some E when C and G are taken as the same; this is the last figure, for G becomes the middle. To no E, when D and F are the same; in this way there is both the first figure and the middle: the first, because A belongs to no F (since the negative converts) and F to every E; the middle, because D belongs to no A and to every E. Not to some E, when D and G are the same; this is the last figure, for A will belong to no G, and E to every G. It is plain, then, that all syllogisms are through the figures named, and that we must not select what follows everything, since no syllogism comes about from these. To establish from consequents was impossible in any case, and to deny is not possible through what follows everything, since it must belong to one term and not to the other.

@p
@greek Φανερὸν δὲ καὶ ὅτι αἱ ἄλλαι σκέψεις
It is plain too that the other inquiries made in selecting are useless for making a syllogism: for instance whether the things that follow each are the same, or whether the things A follows are the same as the things that cannot belong to E, or what cannot belong to either. No syllogism comes about through these. If the things that follow are the same, as B and F, the middle figure comes about with affirmative premises. If the things A follows are the same as those that cannot belong to E, as C and H, the first figure comes about with its premise at the minor term negative. If what cannot belong to either, as D and H, both premises are negative, either in the first figure or in the middle. In these ways there is no syllogism at all. It is clear too that we must take terms in the inquiry that are the same, not those that are different or contrary. First, because the inquiry is for the sake of the middle, and the middle must be taken as the same, not different. Second, wherever a syllogism does come about by taking contraries, or things that cannot belong to the same thing, everything will be brought back to the moods named. For instance, if B and F are contraries or cannot belong to the same thing: when these are taken there will be a syllogism that A belongs to no E, but not from these themselves; it is from the mood named before. For B will belong to every A and to no E; so B must be the same as some H. Again, if B and G cannot be present to the same thing, there will be a syllogism that A will not belong to some E; for in this way too there will be the middle figure, since B will belong to every A and to no G; so B must be the same as some H. For that B and G cannot belong to the same thing is no different from B being the same as some of the Hs, since all the things that cannot belong to E have been taken. It is plain, then, that no syllogism comes about from these inquiries themselves; but if B and F are contraries, B must be the same as one of the Hs, and the syllogism comes about through these. Those who inquire in this way happen to look for a further road besides the necessary one, because they do not see that B and H are the same.

@ch APr.I.29 | The same method serves for proofs by the impossible and from hypotheses
Proofs through the impossible use the same terms as direct proofs, and whatever is proved one way can be proved the other. In other proofs from hypotheses the search is about the premise substituted. The method holds for necessary and possible premises too, and there is no other road to a syllogism.

@p
Syllogisms leading to the impossible are in the same case as direct ones, for they too come about through what follows each term and what each follows. The inquiry is the same in both. What is proved directly can also be proved through the impossible by the same terms, and what is proved through the impossible can also be proved directly: for instance, that A belongs to no E. Let it be laid down that it belongs to some. Then since B belongs to every A and A to some E, B will belong to some of the Es; but it belonged to none. Again, that it belongs to some: for if A belongs to no E, and E to every G, A will belong to none of the Gs; but it belonged to all. It is likewise with the other problems. In all of them the proof through the impossible will be from what follows and what each follows. And for each problem the inquiry is the same whether one wants to reason directly or to lead to the impossible, since both proofs are from the same terms. For instance, if it has been proved that A belongs to no E, because it follows that B also belongs to some E, which is impossible: if it is taken that B belongs to no E and to every A, it is plain that A will belong to no E. Again, if it has been concluded directly that A belongs to no E, then supposing that it belongs to some, it will be proved through the impossible that it belongs to none. It is likewise with the others. In all of them one must take some common term other than the subjects, to which the syllogism of the falsehood will be related; so that when this premise is converted, and the other stays the same, the syllogism will be direct through the same terms. A direct syllogism differs from one leading to the impossible in that in the direct one both premises are laid down as true, while in the one leading to the impossible one premise is false.

@p
@greek Ταῦτα μὲν οὖν ἔσται μᾶλλον φανερὰ
These things will be plainer from what follows, when we speak about the impossible. For now let this much be clear to us: one must look to the same things whether one wants to reason directly or to lead to the impossible. In the other syllogisms from a hypothesis, such as those by substitution or by quality, the inquiry will be about the subjects, not the original ones but those substituted, and the way of looking will be the same. We must also consider and distinguish in how many ways syllogisms from a hypothesis come about. Each of the problems is proved in this way; but some of them can also be concluded in another way, for instance universals by the inquiry into particulars, from a hypothesis. If C and G were the same, and it were taken that E belongs to the Gs only, A would belong to every E. And again, if D and G are the same, and E is predicated of the Gs only, A will belong to no E. It is plain, then, that one must look in this way too. It is the same way with necessary and possible premises. The inquiry is the same, and the syllogism of the possible and of belonging will be through the same terms in the same order. With possible premises, one must also take what does not belong but can belong, for it has been shown that the syllogism of the possible comes about through these too. It is likewise with the other forms of predication. It is plain from what has been said, then, not only that all syllogisms can come about by this road, but also that they cannot by any other. Every syllogism has been shown to come about through one of the figures named, and these cannot be put together through anything other than what follows and what each term follows; for from these come the premises and the finding of the middle. So no syllogism can come about through anything else.
!note "Syllogisms by quality" (κατὰ ποιότητα) are arguments from what is more, less or alike likely, as the Topics treats them. Aristotle promised to distinguish the kinds of proof from a hypothesis; the promise is not kept in the work as we have it.

@ch APr.I.30 | The method holds in every science; experience gives the starting points
The road is the same in philosophy and in every art and study: gather what belongs to each thing and what it belongs to. For truth, take what truly belongs; for dialectic, what is believed. Most starting points are proper to each science and come from experience, as astronomy's proofs came from observing the appearances.

@p
The road, then, is the same in all cases, in philosophy and in any art or study whatever. One must gather, for each of the two terms, what belongs to it and what it belongs to, and be supplied with as many of these as possible; and one must examine them through the three terms, refuting in one way and establishing in another. For truth, one must argue from what has been set out as truly belonging; for dialectical syllogisms, from premises that are believed. The starting points of syllogisms have been described in general, how they stand and how one must hunt for them, so that we do not look at everything that is said, nor at the same things when establishing and refuting, nor, when establishing something of all or some, and refuting it of all or some, look at everything, but at fewer things and definite ones; and select for each kind of thing that is, such as the good or knowledge. Most are proper to each science. So it is for experience to hand down the starting points about each thing. I mean, for instance, that experience in astronomy gives the starting points of the science of astronomy: when the appearances had been grasped well enough, the astronomical demonstrations were found in this way. It is likewise with any other art and science whatever. So if what belongs to each thing is grasped, it is then our task to set out the demonstrations readily. For if, in our inquiry, none of the things that truly belong to things has been left out, we shall be able, about everything of which there is a demonstration, to find it and demonstrate it; and where by nature there is no demonstration, to make that plain. In general, then, we have said roughly how premises must be selected. We have gone through it precisely in the treatise on dialectic.
!note "Astronomy" renders ἀστρολογία, which in Aristotle's Greek means the study of the stars' motions, not fortune-telling by them. "The treatise on dialectic" is the Topics.

@ch APr.I.31 | Against proof by division
Division by genera, the method of Plato's Sophist and Statesman, is a small part of this method and a weak syllogism. It begs what it should prove: from "every animal is mortal or immortal" and "man is an animal" it follows only that man is mortal or immortal; that he is a mortal animal is simply assumed. It cannot refute, nor prove accidents, properties or genera, nor settle open questions.

@p
That division by genera is a small part of the method described is easy to see. Division is a kind of weak syllogism: it asks for what it should prove, and always concludes something more general. First, this very point had escaped all who used it, and they tried to persuade people that a demonstration could be given of a thing's substance and of what it is. So they did not understand what can be concluded by dividing, nor that it could be done in the way we have said. In demonstrations, when one must conclude that something belongs, the middle through which the syllogism comes about must always be narrower than the first of the extremes, and not universal of it. Division wants the opposite: it takes the universal as the middle. Let animal be A, mortal B, immortal C, and man, whose definition is to be found, D. The divider takes it that every animal is either mortal or immortal: that is, whatever is A is all either B or C. Again, dividing all the time, he lays down that man is an animal, so that he takes A as belonging to D. The syllogism, then, is that every D will be either B or C; so it is necessary that man be either mortal or immortal, but not necessary that he be a mortal animal: this is asked for. And this was what had to be concluded. Again, laying down A as mortal animal, B as footed, C as footless, and man as D, he takes it in the same way that A is either in B or in C (since every mortal animal is either footed or footless), and that A is said of D (for he took it that man is a mortal animal); so man must be either a footed or a footless animal, but that he is footed is not necessary; he takes it. And this was again what had to be shown. Dividing all the time in this way, it happens that they take the universal as the middle, and as the extremes that about which the proof was wanted and the differences.
!flag For division is as it were an imbecile syllogism | Taylor's "imbecile" renders ἀσθενής, "weak" or "feeble". In Taylor's day "imbecile" could mean simply "weak"; today it is an insult, and Aristotle is making a logical point: division assumes the very step it should prove.
!flag either a pedestrious or biped animal | Taylor's "pedestrious" renders ὑπόπουν, "having feet", "footed"; and in this sentence his "biped" stands where the Greek has ἄπουν, "footless", the other side of the division. The modern version says "footed or footless".
!note The method criticised is the division of Plato's Sophist and Statesman, where a kind is cut in two again and again (animals into the mortal and the immortal, and so on) until the thing sought is reached. Aristotle's charge is that each cut chooses one side without proof.

@p
@greek Φανερὸν δʼ ὅτι οὔτʼ ἀνασκευάσαι
In the end, as to "this is man", or whatever the thing sought may be, they say nothing clear that makes it necessary; for they make the whole of the rest of the road too without even supposing that the resources we described exist. It is plain too that by this method one cannot refute, nor conclude about an accident or a property, nor about a genus, nor about things where it is not known whether they are this way or that, for instance whether the diagonal is incommensurable or commensurable. If one takes it that every length is either commensurable or incommensurable, and the diagonal is a length, one has concluded that the diagonal is incommensurable or commensurable. If one takes it as incommensurable, one will take what had to be concluded. So it cannot be proved: the road is this one, and by it it cannot be done. Let incommensurable or commensurable be A, length B, diagonal C. It is plain, then, that this way of inquiring does not suit every inquiry, nor is it useful in the cases where it seems most fitting. From what, then, demonstrations come about and how, and what one must look to for each problem, is plain from what has been said.
!flag division into classes | Jenkinson's "classes" renders γένη, "genera" (kinds), the word for the higher kinds of the Categories and of Porphyry's tree. The division meant is the cutting of a genus into its species.

@ch APr.I.32 | Putting arguments into the figures: find the two premises
Next, how to bring arguments back to the figures. First pick out the two premises, adding one if it is missing and removing what is idle; for an argument may reach something necessary without being a syllogism. Then find the middle term, the one said in both premises: its place shows the figure.

@p
How we shall bring syllogisms back to the figures named should be said next, for this part of the inquiry is still left. If we studied how syllogisms come about, and had the power of finding them, and further could analyse those already made into the figures named, our original undertaking would be complete. It will happen at the same time that what was said before is confirmed and made plainer, that it is so, by what will now be said; for everything true must agree with itself in every way. First, then, one must try to pick out the two premises of the syllogism (it is easier to divide into the larger parts than the smaller, and what is put together is larger than what it is made of); then examine which is universal and which particular; and if both have not been taken, lay down the other oneself. For sometimes people put forward the universal and do not take the premise contained in it, either in writing or in asking; or they put forward these, and leave out the premises through which they are concluded, and ask other things to no purpose. So one must see whether anything superfluous has been taken and anything necessary left out, and lay down the one and remove the other, until one comes to the two premises; without these one cannot bring back arguments asked in this way. With some arguments it is easy to see what is lacking, but others escape notice and seem to syllogize, because something necessary follows from what is laid down. For instance, if it were taken that when what is not substance is destroyed, substance is not destroyed, and that when the things something is made of are destroyed, what is made of them perishes. With these laid down, it is necessary that a part of a substance is a substance; but it has not been concluded through what was taken: premises are lacking. Again, if when man is, animal must be, and when animal is, substance must be, then when man is, substance must be; but it has not yet been concluded, for the premises are not related as we said.
!note The analysis of arguments into the figures gives the work its name: "Analytics" means the art of resolving (ἀναλύειν) arguments into their premises and figures.

@p
@greek Ἀπατώμεθα δʼ ἐν τοῖς τοιούτοις
We are deceived in such cases because something necessary follows from what is laid down, and a syllogism too is necessary. But the necessary is wider than the syllogism: every syllogism is necessary, but not everything necessary is a syllogism. So if something follows when certain things are laid down, one should not try to bring it back at once, but first take the two premises, then divide them into the terms, and lay down as middle the term that is said in both premises, for in all the figures the middle must be present in both.

@p
@greek Ἐὰν μὲν οὖν κατηγορῇ καὶ κατηγορῆται τὸ μέσον
If the middle predicates and is predicated, or predicates and something else is denied of it, the figure will be the first. If it both predicates and is denied of something, it will be the middle figure. If other things are predicated of it, or one is denied and the other predicated, it will be the last; for so the middle was placed in each figure. It is likewise if the premises are not universal, since the middle is marked off in the same way. It is plain, then, that in an argument where the same thing is not said more than once, no syllogism comes about, for no middle has been taken. And since we know which problems are concluded in each figure, and in which the universal and in which the particular, it is plain that we must not look to all the figures, but to the one proper to each problem. Where it is concluded in more than one, we shall know the figure by the place of the middle.

@ch APr.I.33 | Being deceived by the likeness of terms
We are sometimes deceived because the terms look like those of a syllogism when a premise is not universal: "Aristomenes as an object of thought always exists" and "Aristomenes is Aristomenes as an object of thought" do not give "Aristomenes always exists".

@p
We are often deceived about syllogisms, then, because of the necessity, as was said before; and sometimes because of the likeness in the placing of the terms, which we must not fail to notice. For instance, if A is said of B and B of C: it would seem that with the terms so related there is a syllogism, but nothing necessary comes about and no syllogism. Let A be "always existing", B "Aristomenes as an object of thought", and C "Aristomenes". It is true that A belongs to B, for Aristomenes as an object of thought always exists. But B also belongs to C, for Aristomenes is Aristomenes as an object of thought. But A does not belong to C, for Aristomenes is perishable. No syllogism came about with the terms so related; the premise AB had to be taken universally. And this is false, to claim that every Aristomenes as an object of thought always exists, since Aristomenes is perishable. Again, let C be Miccalus, B musical Miccalus, and A "perishing tomorrow". It is true to predicate B of C, for Miccalus is musical Miccalus. And A of B too, for musical Miccalus might perish tomorrow. But A of C is false. This is the same as the former case, for it is not true universally that musical Miccalus perishes tomorrow; and without this being taken, there was no syllogism. This deception, then, comes about in a small difference; for we agree as though it made no difference to say "this belongs to that" and "this belongs to all of that".
!note "Aristomenes as an object of thought" is Aristomenes as he is thought of: the thought, or what it grasps, may be eternal, though the man is not. "Musical Miccalus perishing tomorrow" is perhaps the musician ceasing to be a musician, not the man dying. In both, the premise holds of some but not of all.

@ch APr.I.34 | Being deceived by setting out the terms badly
Terms must be set out as states rather than as the qualities themselves: "health belongs to no disease" and "every man can take disease" seem to prove that health belongs to no man, but with "the healthy" and "the sick" in their place the argument fails.

@p
We shall often be deceived because the terms in the premise are not set out well. For instance, let A be health, B disease, and C man. It is true to say that A cannot belong to any B (for health belongs to no disease), and again that B belongs to every C (for every man can take disease). So it would seem to follow that health cannot belong to any man. The cause of this is that the terms are not well set out in the wording; for if the things that go with the states are substituted, there will be no syllogism: for instance if "healthy" is laid down in place of health and "sick" in place of disease. It is not true to say that being healthy cannot belong to the sick; and if this is not taken, no syllogism comes about, except of the possible, which is not impossible: for it is possible that health belongs to no man.

@p
@greek πάλιν ἐπὶ τοῦ μέσου σχήματος
Again, in the middle figure the falsehood will come about likewise: health may belong to no disease and to every man, so disease would belong to no man. In the third figure the falsehood follows in the case of the possible. Health and disease, knowledge and ignorance, and in general contraries, may belong to the same thing, but not to each other; and this disagrees with what was said before, for when several things could belong to the same thing, they could belong to one another. It is plain, then, that in all of these the deception comes about through setting out the terms; for when the things that go with the states are substituted, nothing false comes about. It is clear, then, that in such premises what goes with the state must always be substituted for the state and laid down as the term.

@ch APr.I.35 | Not every term has a single name
Terms need not be single names; often they are phrases with no name. Looking for a name can mislead, as if there were a syllogism of what has no middle: "two right angles" belongs to the isosceles through "triangle", and to the triangle through nothing.

@p
One should not always seek to set out the terms by a name, for there will often be phrases for which no name is laid down; and so it is hard to bring such syllogisms back. Sometimes one will even be deceived by such a search, thinking for instance that there is a syllogism of immediate things. Let A be "two right angles", B triangle, and C isosceles. A belongs to C through B, but no longer to B through something else, for the triangle has two right angles of itself; so there will be no middle of B, though it is provable. It is plain that the middle must not always be taken as a "this", but sometimes as a phrase, which is what happens in the case mentioned.
!note "Has two right angles": the sum of the angles of a triangle is equal to two right angles (180 degrees). The middle that proves it of every triangle is not a single word but a phrase, the construction in Euclid I.32.

@ch APr.I.36 | "Belongs" is said in as many ways as "is"
A premise need not say that one term is the other; "belongs" is said in as many ways as "is", and in different cases: "there is one science of contraries", "pleasure is not a coming-to-be, since there is a coming-to-be of it". Terms should be set out by the name in the nominative, premises in whatever case the sense needs.

@p
That the first belongs to the middle and this to the last term must not be taken as if they will always be predicated of one another, or the first of the middle likewise as this of the last. It is the same with not belonging. Rather, "belongs" must be thought to signify in as many ways as "is" is said, and as it is true to say that something is. For instance, that there is one science of contraries. Let A be "there is one science", and B "things contrary to one another". A belongs to B not in the sense that the contraries are one science, but in the sense that it is true to say of them that there is one science of them.

@p
@greek Συμβαίνει δʼ ὁτὲ μὲν ἐπὶ τοῦ μέσου
It happens sometimes that the first term is said of the middle, but the middle is not said of the third. For instance, if wisdom is a science, and wisdom is of the good, the conclusion is that there is a science of the good; but the good is not a science, though wisdom is a science. Sometimes the middle is said of the third, but the first is not said of the middle. For instance, if there is a science of every quality or contrary, and the good is both a contrary and a quality, the conclusion is that there is a science of the good; but the good is not a science, nor is the quality or the contrary; rather the good is these. Sometimes neither the first is said of the middle nor this of the third, while the first is sometimes said of the third and sometimes not. For instance, if there is a genus of whatever there is a science of, and there is a science of the good, the conclusion is that there is a genus of the good; but nothing is predicated of anything. If whatever there is a science of is a genus, and there is a science of the good, the conclusion is that the good is a genus; here the first is predicated of the extreme, but they are not said of one another.

@p
@greek Τὸν αὐτὸν δὴ τρόπον καὶ ἐπὶ τοῦ μὴ ὑπάρχειν ληπτέον
Not belonging must be taken in the same way. "This does not belong to that" does not always mean "this is not that", but sometimes "this is not of that" or "for that": for instance, that there is no motion of motion, or coming-to-be of coming-to-be, but there is of pleasure; so pleasure is not a coming-to-be. Or again, that there is a sign of laughter, but no sign of a sign, so laughter is not a sign. It is likewise in the other cases where the problem is refuted because the genus is said in some way in relation to it. Again, that the right moment is not the needed time: for there is a right moment for God, but no needed time, since nothing is useful to God. The terms to lay down are "right moment", "needed time" and "God", but the premise must be taken according to the case of the noun. We say this of all cases without qualification: the terms must always be laid down in the nominative forms of the names, such as man, good, contraries, not "of man", "of good", "of contraries"; but the premises must be taken according to the case each needs: either "to this", as with "equal", or "of this", as with "double", or "this", as with "striking" or "seeing", or "this one", as "man is an animal", or in whatever other case the name falls in the premise.
!note Greek shows by the case-ending of a noun (nominative, genitive, dative, accusative) how it stands to the verb, where English uses "of", "to" and word order. "Equal to this", "double of this", "strikes this", "this man is": Aristotle's point survives in English through the prepositions.

@ch APr.I.37 | Belonging in each category
"Belongs" and "is true of" must be taken in as many ways as the categories are divided, and as holding in some respect or without qualification, simply or in combination.

@p
That this belongs to that, and that this is truly said of that, must be taken in as many ways as the categories have been divided; and these either in some respect or without qualification, and further either simple or combined. It is likewise with not belonging. These things must be examined and defined better.

@ch APr.I.38 | Where a repeated qualification goes
A qualification repeated in a premise, such as "that it is good", belongs with the major term, not with the middle: "there is knowledge of justice, that it is good" is proved with "knowledge that it is good" as the major and "good" as the middle.

@taylor 37
@p
What is repeated in the premises must be placed with the first extreme, not with the middle. I mean, for instance, if there were a syllogism that there is knowledge of justice, that it is good, then "that it is good", or "as good", must be placed with the first term. Let A be "knowledge that it is good", B good, and C justice. It is true to predicate A of B, for of the good there is knowledge that it is good. And B of C too, for justice is just what is good. In this way, then, the analysis comes about. But if "that it is good" were placed with B, there will be none. For A will be true of B, but B will not be true of C, since to predicate "good, that it is good" of justice is false and not intelligible. It is likewise if it were shown that the healthy is knowable as good, or the goat-stag as not being, or man perishable as perceptible; in all these cases of added predicates, the repetition must be placed with the extreme.
!note The goat-stag (τραγέλαφος) is the imaginary beast of On Interpretation, chapter 1: it can be known "as not being", as something that does not exist.

@p
@greek Οὐχ ἡ αὐτὴ δὲ θέσις τῶν ὅρων
The placing of the terms is not the same when something is concluded without qualification and when it is concluded to be "this" or in some respect or some way: I mean, for instance, when the good is shown to be knowable, and when it is shown to be knowable that it is good. If it has been shown to be knowable without qualification, being must be laid down as the middle; if that it is good, being something. Let A be "knowledge that it is something", B "something", and C good. It is true to predicate A of B, for there was knowledge of something, that it is something. And B of C too, for C is something. So A will be of C too. There will be knowledge, then, of the good, that it is good; for "something" was the sign of its own substance. But if being were laid down as the middle, and being without qualification, not "being something", were said with the extreme, there would not be a syllogism that there is knowledge of the good, that it is good, but that it is: for instance with A "knowledge that it is", B being, and C good. It is plain, then, that in particular syllogisms the terms must be taken in this way.

@ch APr.I.39 | Substituting equivalent expressions
Words may be exchanged for phrases that mean the same, and a single name taken in place of a phrase, since the terms are then easier to set out.

@p
One must also substitute things that have the same force: names for names, phrases for phrases, a name for a phrase and a phrase for a name; and always take the name rather than the phrase, for setting out the terms is easier. For instance, if it makes no difference to say "the supposable is not the genus of the believable" or "the believable is not just a kind of supposable" (for what is meant is the same), then instead of the phrase stated, the supposable and the believable should be laid down as the terms.

@ch APr.I.40 | "Good" and "the good"
"Pleasure is good" and "pleasure is the good" are different claims, so the terms must be set out differently for each.

@p
Since "pleasure is good" and "pleasure is the good" are not the same, the terms must not be laid down likewise. If the syllogism is that pleasure is the good, the term is "the good"; if that it is good, "good". It is so with the others too.
!note The question whether pleasure is the good, or a good, is the question of Plato's Philebus, read in the Platonists' course, and of Nicomachean Ethics VII and X.

@ch APr.I.41 | "Of all of which B is said" and "of all of what B is said of all of"
"A belongs to all of what B belongs to" differs from "A belongs to all of what B belongs to all of". Setting out an instance, like the geometer's line "a foot long", uses nothing false in the proof: it is like pointing to something for the learner.

@p
It is not the same, either in being or in saying, that A belongs to all of what B belongs to, and that A belongs to all of that to all of which B belongs. For nothing prevents B from belonging to C, but not to all of it. Let B be beautiful, and C white. If beautiful belongs to some white thing, it is true to say that beautiful belongs to white, but perhaps not to all of it. If, then, A belongs to B, but not to all of what B is said of, then whether B belongs to all of C or only belongs to it, it is not necessary that A belongs, still less to all. But if A belongs to all of whatever B is truly said of, it will follow that A is said of all of whatever B is said of all of. If A is said of whatever B is said of all of, nothing prevents B from belonging to C while A does not belong to all of C, or does not belong at all. With three terms, then, it is clear that "A is said of all of what B is said of all of" means "A is said of all the things B is said of". If B is said of all, so is A; if not of all, A need not be said of all.

@p
@greek παρὰ τὸ ἐκτίθεσθαί τι συμβαίνειν
We must not think that anything absurd follows from setting something out. We make no use of its being this particular thing; it is like the geometer who says that this line is a foot long, or straight, or without breadth, when it is not, but who does not use these things as though he were reasoning from them. In general, what is not related as a whole to a part, with something else related to it as a part to a whole, is not used by the one proving to prove anything; so no syllogism comes about from it either. We use setting out as we use perception, speaking to the learner; not as though it were impossible to prove without these, as it is without the things the syllogism is from.
!note The drawn figure in geometry is only an aid: the proof does not depend on the chalk line being exactly a foot long or perfectly straight. Plato makes the same point in Republic VI (510d): geometers reason about the square itself, not the one they draw.

@ch APr.I.42 | One argument, several figures
In one argument the conclusions need not all come through one figure; analyse each part in its own figure, which the conclusion shows.

@p
We must not fail to notice that in the same syllogism not all the conclusions are through one figure, but one through this and another through another. It is clear, then, that the analyses must be made in this way too. And since not every problem is proved in every figure, but each in its fixed figures, it is plain from the conclusion in which figure it must be sought.

@ch APr.I.43 | Arguments against a definition
When an argument is about one part of a definition, set out only that part as the term, not the whole definition.

@p
In arguments against a definition, which happen to be argued against one of the things in it, what has been argued against should be laid down as the term, not the whole phrase; for there will be less confusion because of the length. For instance, if someone has shown that water is a drinkable liquid, "drinkable" and "water" should be laid down as the terms.

@ch APr.I.44 | Arguments from a hypothesis cannot be analysed into the figures
Arguments from an agreed hypothesis, and the hypothetical part of proofs through the impossible, cannot be brought back to the figures; only the syllogism inside them can. In the first, the hypothesis is agreed beforehand; in the second, the falsity is plain enough to need no agreement.

@p
Further, one should not try to bring back syllogisms from a hypothesis, for one cannot bring them back from what is laid down. They have not been proved through a syllogism, but all have been agreed by compact. For instance, if someone supposed that if there is not one capacity for contraries, there is not one science of them either, and then argued that not every capacity is for contraries, for instance for the healthy and the sick, since the same thing would then be at once healthy and sick. That there is not one capacity for all contraries has been shown; that there is not one science has not. Yet one must agree to it, not from a syllogism but from a hypothesis. This argument cannot be brought back; but that there is not one capacity can, for this perhaps was the syllogism, and the other a hypothesis.

@p
@greek Ὁμοίως δὲ καὶ ἐπὶ τῶν διὰ τοῦ ἀδυνάτου
It is likewise with those concluded through the impossible. These cannot be analysed either; the reduction to the impossible can, since it is proved by a syllogism, but the other part cannot, since it is concluded from a hypothesis. They differ from those just mentioned in that, in those, one must agree beforehand if one is going to grant the point (for instance, that if one capacity for contraries is shown, the science of them is also the same); but here people grant it without agreeing beforehand, because the falsity is plain, as that odd numbers are equal to even ones if the diagonal is laid down as commensurable. Many others too are concluded from a hypothesis, which must be examined and marked out clearly. What the differences among them are, and in how many ways a hypothesis is used, we shall say later. For now let this much be plain to us: such syllogisms cannot be analysed into the figures. And we have said why.

@ch APr.I.45 | Bringing syllogisms from one figure into another
Some syllogisms can be moved from one figure into another by converting a premise, the one with the minor term. Those that cannot be brought into the first figure (Baroco, Bocardo) cannot be moved into the others either; they are the ones proved through the impossible.

@p
Problems that are proved in more than one figure, if concluded in one, can have their syllogism brought back into the other: a negative one in the first figure into the second, and one in the middle figure into the first; not all, but some. This will be plain from what follows. If A belongs to no B, and B to every C, A belongs to no C. So this is the first figure; but if the negative is converted, it will be the middle, for B belongs to no A and to every C. It is likewise if the syllogism is not universal but particular: for instance if A belongs to no B and B to some C; when the negative is converted, there will be the middle figure.

@p
@greek Τῶν δʼ ἐν τῷ δευτέρῳ συλλογισμῶν οἱ μὲν καθόλου
Of the syllogisms in the second figure, the universal ones will be brought back into the first, but of the particular only one. Let A belong to no B and to every C. When the negative is converted, there will be the first figure: B will belong to no A, and A to every C. If the affirmative is with B and the negative with C, C must be laid down as the first term; for C belongs to no A, and A to every B, so C belongs to no B. So neither does B belong to any C, since the negative converts. If the syllogism is particular, then when the negative is with the major term it will be brought back into the first: for instance if A belongs to no B and to some C. When the negative is converted, there will be the first figure, for B belongs to no A, and A to some C. But when the affirmative is, it will not be analysed: for instance if A belongs to every B and not to every C. For AB does not admit of conversion, and if it is converted there will be no syllogism.

@p
@greek Πάλιν οἱ μὲν ἐν τῷ τρίτῳ σχήματι
Again, the syllogisms in the third figure will not all be analysed into the first, but those in the first will all be analysed into the third. Let A belong to every B and B to some C. Since the particular affirmative converts, C will belong to some B; and A belonged to every B; so the third figure comes about. It is the same if the syllogism is negative, since the particular affirmative converts; so A will belong to no B, and C to some B. Of the syllogisms in the last figure, only one is not analysed into the first, when the negative is not laid down universally; all the others are. Let A and B be predicated of every C. Then C will convert in part with each; so it belongs to some B. So there will be the first figure, if A belongs to every C and C to some B. If A belongs to every C and B to some C, the argument is the same, since C converts with B. If B belongs to every C and A to some C, B must be laid down as the first term; for B belongs to every C and C to some A, so B belongs to some A; and since the particular converts, A will belong to some B. If the syllogism is negative, with the terms universal, it must be taken likewise. Let B belong to every C and A to none; then C will belong to some B, and A to no C, so C will be the middle. It is likewise if the negative is universal and the affirmative particular: A will belong to no C, and C to some B. But if the negative is taken as particular, there will be no analysis: for instance if B belongs to every C and A does not belong to some C. When BC is converted, both premises will be particular.

@p
@greek Φανερὸν δὲ καὶ ὅτι πρὸς τὸ ἀναλύειν
It is plain too that, to analyse the figures into one another, the premise with the minor term must be converted in both figures, since the change came about when this was moved. Of the syllogisms in the middle figure, one is analysed into the third and the other is not. When the universal is negative, it is analysed: if A belongs to no B and to some C, both convert likewise with A, so that B belongs to no A, and C to some A; so A is the middle. But when A belongs to every B and does not belong to some C, there will be no analysis, since neither of the premises is universal after conversion. Those from the third figure too will be analysed into the middle, when the negative is universal: for instance if A belongs to no C, and B to some or all of C; for C will belong to no A and to some B. If the negative is particular, it will not be analysed, since the particular negative does not admit of conversion. It is plain, then, that the same syllogisms are not analysed in these figures as were not analysed into the first either; and that when syllogisms are brought back into the first figure, these alone are concluded through the impossible. How syllogisms must be brought back, then, and that the figures are analysed into one another, is plain from what has been said.

@ch APr.I.46 | "Is not good" and "is not-good"
"Not to be white" and "to be not-white" differ: the first is the denial of "to be white", the second an affirmation of a negative term. The four expressions (good, not good, not-good, not not-good) are ordered so that each pair has one true of everything; mistaking them leads to false analyses.

@p
It makes some difference, in establishing or refuting, whether one supposes that "not to be this" and "to be not-this" mean the same or something different: for instance "not to be white" and "to be not-white". They do not mean the same, and the denial of "to be white" is not "to be not-white" but "not to be white". The account of this is as follows. "He can walk" stands to "he can not-walk" likewise as "it is white" to "it is not-white", and as "he knows the good" is to "he knows the not-good". There is no difference between "he knows the good" and "he is knowing the good", or between "he can walk" and "he is able to walk"; so neither is there between their opposites, "he cannot walk" and "he is not able to walk". If, then, "he is not able to walk" means the same as "he is able not-to-walk" or "able not to walk", these will belong at once to the same person (for the same man can both walk and not walk, and has knowledge both of the good and of the not-good); but an affirmation and a denial that are opposed do not belong at once to the same thing. So just as "not to know the good" and "to know the not-good" are not the same, neither are "to be not-good" and "not to be good". For of things in proportion, if one pair is different, so is the other. Nor are "to be not-equal" and "not to be equal" the same. One has something underlying it, what is not-equal, and this is the unequal; the other has nothing. So not everything is either equal or unequal, but everything is either equal or not equal. Again, "it is a not-white log" and "it is not a white log" do not belong together. For if it is a not-white log, it will be a log; but what is not a white log need not be a log at all. So it is plain that "it is not-good" is not the denial of "it is good". If, then, of every single thing either the affirmation or the denial is true, and this is not a denial, it is clear that it would be a kind of affirmation. But every affirmation has a denial; so the denial of this too is "it is not not-good".
!note This chapter returns to On Interpretation, chapter 10, on "indefinite" names like "not-man": "is not-good" affirms a negative term; "is not good" denies a positive one.

@p
@greek Ἔχει δὲ τάξιν τήνδε
They are ordered towards one another in this way. Let "to be good" be A, "not to be good" B, "to be not-good" C, placed under B, and "not to be not-good" D, placed under A. Then either A or B will belong to everything, and never both to the same thing; and either C or D, and never both to the same thing. And to whatever C belongs, B must belong; for if it is true to say "it is not-white", it is true also to say "it is not white", since it is impossible at once to be white and to be not-white, or to be a not-white log and a white log; so if the affirmation does not belong, the denial will. But C does not always go with B, since what is not a log at all will not be a not-white log either. Conversely, then, to whatever A belongs, D belongs; for either C or D belongs, and since it is not possible to be at once not-white and white, D will belong; for of what is white it is true to say that it is not not-white. But A is not true of all of D; for of what is not a log at all it is not true to say A, that it is a white log; so D is true, but A, that it is a white log, is not. It is clear too that A and C can never belong to the same thing, while B and D can belong to the same thing. Privations stand likewise to the positive terms in this arrangement: let A be equal, B not equal, C unequal, D not unequal.
!note Privation (στέρησις) is the lack of something a thing would naturally have: "unequal" is a privation, and like "not-equal" it needs a subject, while "not equal" can be said of anything at all.

@p
@greek Καὶ ἐπὶ πολλῶν δέ
In many things too, where the same thing belongs to some and not to others, the denial would be true likewise: "not all are white", or "not each is white"; but that each is not-white, or that all are not-white, is false. Likewise the denial of "every animal is white" is not "every animal is not-white" (both are false), but "not every animal is white".
!flag the proper negation is ‘every animal is not white’ | Jenkinson's "every animal is not white" renders οὐκ ἔστι πᾶν ζῷον λευκόν. In today's English "every animal is not white" sounds like "no animal is white", which is false; the Greek denial is the contradictory, "not every animal is white", as the modern version has it. The same holds of his "all are not white".

@p
@greek Ἐπεὶ δὲ δῆλον ὅτι ἕτερον σημαίνει
Since it is clear that "it is not-white" and "it is not white" mean different things, and one is an affirmation and the other a denial, it is plain that the way of proving each is not the same: for instance, that whatever is an animal is not white, or may not be white, and that it is true to call it not-white, for this is to be not-white. But "it is true to call it white" and "it is true to call it not-white" are proved in the same way, since both are proved constructively through the first figure; for "it is true" is placed likewise with "it is". For the denial of "it is true to call it white" is not "it is true to call it not-white" but "it is not true to call it white". If, then, it is to be true to say that whatever is a man is musical or is not-musical, it must be taken that whatever is an animal is musical or is not-musical, and it has been proved. But that whatever is a man is not musical is proved destructively, in the three ways mentioned.

@p
@greek Ἁπλῶς δʼ ὅταν οὕτως ἔχῃ
In general, when A and B are so related that they cannot belong to the same thing together, but one of them must belong to everything, and C and D again likewise, and A follows C but does not convert with it, then D will follow B and will not convert with it; and A and D can belong to the same thing, but B and C cannot. First, that D follows B is plain from this. Since one of C and D must belong to everything, and C cannot belong to what B belongs to, since it brings A along with it, and A and B cannot belong to the same thing, it is plain that D will follow. Again, since C does not convert with A, and either C or D belongs to everything, A and D can belong to the same thing. But B and C cannot, since A goes with C; for something impossible follows. It is plain, then, that B does not convert with D either, since D and A can belong together.

@p
@greek Συμβαίνει δʼ ἐνίοτε καὶ ἐν τῇ τοιαύτῃ τάξει
Sometimes in such an arrangement of terms people are deceived because they do not take rightly the opposites, one of which must belong to everything. For instance, if A and B cannot belong to the same thing together, and one must belong to whatever the other does not, and C and D again likewise, and A follows everything C belongs to: it will follow that B belongs of necessity to whatever D belongs to, which is false. Let F be taken as the denial of A and B, and H again of C and D. Then either A or F must belong to everything, since either the affirmation or the denial does; and again either C or H, since they are affirmation and denial. And A was supposed to belong to everything C belongs to; so H belongs to everything F belongs to. Again, since one of F and B belongs to everything, and likewise one of H and D, and H follows F, B too will follow D; for we know this. So if A follows C, B follows D. But this is false, for in things so related the following was the other way round. The reason is that it is perhaps not necessary that either A or F belongs to everything, nor either F or B; for F is not the denial of A. The denial of "good" is "not good"; and "not good" is not the same as "neither good nor not good". It is likewise with C and D, for the denials taken are two.

@treatise APr.II | Prior Analytics, Book II
Book II studies what syllogisms can do once they are made, and how they go wrong. A syllogism proves more than its conclusion (chapter 1). True conclusions can follow from false premises, though never false ones from true (2 to 4). Proof in a circle, conversion of a syllogism, and reduction to the impossible are compared, figure by figure (5 to 14); then syllogisms from opposite premises (15), begging the question (16), the false cause (17 and 18), how to argue and how to guard against an opponent (19 and 20), and error, which can stand beside knowledge (21). Chapter 22 treats convertible terms and preference. The last chapters show that other kinds of argument are syllogisms too, or are reduced to them: induction (23), the example (24), reduction (25), objection (26), and the argument from likelihoods and signs, which ends with physiognomy (27).

@ch APr.II.1 | A syllogism proves more than its conclusion
Universal syllogisms, and particular affirmative ones, prove several things at once, since the conclusion converts, and whatever falls under the middle or the conclusion is proved too; particular negatives prove only the conclusion.

@p
In how many figures, through what kind and number of premises, when and how a syllogism comes about; further, what to look to when refuting and when establishing, and how to inquire about what is proposed in any method whatever; and further by what road we shall get the starting points about each thing, we have now gone through. Since some syllogisms are universal and some particular, the universal ones all always conclude more than one thing; of the particular ones, the affirmative conclude more than one, but the negative only the conclusion. The other premises convert, but the particular negative does not; and the conclusion is something said of something. So the other syllogisms conclude more than one thing: for instance, if A has been proved to belong to every B or to some, B must also belong to some A; and if A to no B, neither does B belong to any A, which is different from the former. But if A does not belong to some B, it is not necessary that B also does not belong to some A, for it may belong to all of it.

@p
@greek Αὕτη μὲν οὖν κοινὴ πάντων αἰτία
This, then, is the reason common to all, both universal and particular. But about the universal ones there is also another thing to say. Of everything that is under the middle or under the conclusion, there will be the same syllogism, if the first are placed in the middle and the second in the conclusion. For instance, if AB is concluded through C, A must be said of everything under B or C. For if D is in B as in a whole, and B in A, D will also be in A; again, if E is in C as in a whole, and C in A, E will also be in A. It is likewise if the syllogism is negative. In the second figure, it will only be possible to conclude about what is under the conclusion. For instance, if A belongs to no B and to every C, the conclusion is that B belongs to no C. If D is under C, it is plain that B does not belong to it. That it does not belong to the things under A is not clear through the syllogism. Yet it does not belong to E, if E is under A; but that B belongs to no C has been proved through the syllogism, while that it does not belong to E has been taken without proof; so it is not because of the syllogism that B does not belong to E. With particular syllogisms, there will be no necessary conclusion about what is under the conclusion (for no syllogism comes about when this premise is taken as particular), but there will be about everything under the middle, though not through the syllogism. For instance, if A belongs to every B and B to some C: if something is laid down under C, there will be no syllogism, but if something under B, there will, though not through the syllogism already made. It is likewise with the other figures. About what is under the conclusion there will be none; about the other there will be, though not through the syllogism, just as in the universal cases what was under the middle was proved from an unproved premise. So either there will be none there, or there will be here too.

@ch APr.II.2 | True conclusions from false premises, in the first figure
@taylor 1
False conclusions never follow from true premises, but true ones can follow from false: from two false premises, or from one false, though if the major is wholly false and the minor true, the conclusion will not be true. Such a conclusion gives the fact, not the reason why.

@p
The premises through which a syllogism comes about may be true, or false, or one true and the other false. The conclusion is of necessity either true or false. From true premises it is not possible to conclude something false; from false ones it is possible to conclude something true, but only the fact, not the reason why, for there is no syllogism of the reason why from false premises. Why this is so will be said in what follows.
!note "The fact" and "the reason why" (τὸ ὅτι, τὸ διότι) are the subject of Posterior Analytics I.13: a demonstration of the reason why proves a thing through its cause.

@p
@greek Πρῶτον μὲν οὖν ὅτι ἐξ ἀληθῶν
First, that it is not possible to conclude something false from true premises is clear from this. If when A is, B must be, then when B is not, A must not be. So if A is true, B must be true, or else the same thing will at once be and not be; and this is impossible. Let it not be thought, because A is laid down as one term, that something can follow of necessity from some one thing being so; for it cannot. What follows of necessity is the conclusion, and the fewest things through which it comes about are three terms, and two relations or premises. If, then, it is true that A belongs to everything B belongs to, and B to everything C belongs to, A must belong to everything C belongs to, and this cannot be false, for the same thing will at once belong and not belong. So A is laid down as though it were one thing, two premises taken together. It is likewise with negatives: it is not possible to prove something false from true premises.

@p
@greek Ἐκ ψευδῶν δʼ ἀληθὲς ἔστι συλλογίσασθαι
From false premises it is possible to conclude something true, both when both premises are false and when one is; but then not whichever it may be, but the second, if it is taken as wholly false; if it is not taken as wholly false, either may be. Let A belong to the whole of C, and to no B, and let B belong to no C. This can be so: animal belongs to no stone, and stone to no man. If, then, A is taken to belong to every B and B to every C, A will belong to every C; so from two false premises the conclusion is true, for every man is an animal. The negative likewise. It is possible for neither A nor B to belong to any C, while A belongs to every B: for instance if, with the same terms taken, man is laid down as the middle. For neither animal nor man belongs to any stone, and animal belongs to every man. So if one takes it that A belongs to none of what it belongs to, and belongs to all of what it does not belong to, the conclusion from two false premises will be true. It will be shown likewise if each is taken as false in part.

@p
@greek Ἐὰν δʼ ἡ ἑτέρα τεθῇ ψευδής
If one is laid down as false, then when the first is wholly false, say AB, the conclusion will not be true; but when BC is, it will. By wholly false I mean the contrary: if what belongs to none is taken as belonging to all, or what belongs to all as belonging to none. Let A belong to no B, and B to every C. If I take the premise BC as true, and the premise AB as wholly false, that A belongs to every B, the conclusion cannot be true; for A belonged to none of the Cs, since A belongs to nothing B belongs to, and B to every C. Likewise if A belongs to every B and B to every C, and the premise BC was taken as true, and AB as wholly false, that A belongs to nothing B belongs to, the conclusion will be false; for A will belong to every C, since A belongs to everything B belongs to, and B to every C. It is plain, then, that when the first premise is taken as wholly false, whether affirmative or negative, and the other as true, the conclusion does not come out true. But when it is not taken as wholly false, it will. If A belongs to every C and to some B, and B to every C (as animal belongs to every swan and to some white thing, and white to every swan), then if A is taken to belong to every B and B to every C, A will truly belong to every C, for every swan is an animal. It is likewise if AB is negative. A can belong to some B and to no C, while B belongs to every C: as animal belongs to some white thing and to no snow, and white to all snow. If, then, A were taken to belong to no B, and B to every C, A will belong to no C.

@p
@greek Ἐὰν δʼ ἡ μὲν Α Β πρότασις ὅλη ληφθῇ ἀληθής
If the premise AB is taken as wholly true and BC as wholly false, the syllogism will be true. For nothing prevents A from belonging to every B and every C, while B belongs to no C: for instance species of the same genus that are not under one another. Animal belongs both to horse and to man, but horse to no man. If, then, A is taken to belong to every B, and B to every C, the conclusion will be true, though the premise BC is wholly false. It is likewise when the premise AB is negative. A can belong to no B and no C, and B to no C: as a genus does to the species of another genus. Animal belongs neither to the musical art nor to medicine, nor the musical art to medicine. If, then, A is taken to belong to no B and B to every C, the conclusion will be true. And if BC is not wholly false but false in part, the conclusion will be true in this way too. Nothing prevents A from belonging to the whole of B and of C, while B belongs to some C: as the genus does to the species and the difference. Animal belongs to every man and to every footed thing, and man to some footed things but not to all. If, then, A were taken to belong to every B, and B to every C, A will belong to every C, which was true. It is likewise if the premise AB is negative. A can belong to no B and no C, while B belongs to some C: as the genus does to the species and difference of another genus. Animal belongs to no wisdom and to no theoretical thing, and wisdom to some theoretical thing. If, then, A were taken to belong to no B, and B to every C, A will belong to no C, which was true.

@p
@greek Ἐπὶ δὲ τῶν ἐν μέρει συλλογισμῶν ἐνδέχεται
With particular syllogisms, the conclusion can be true when the first premise is wholly false and the other true; when the first is false in part and the other true; when the first is true and the particular one false; and when both are false. Nothing prevents A from belonging to no B and to some C, and B to some C: as animal belongs to no snow but to some white thing, and snow to some white thing. If, then, snow were laid down as the middle and animal as the first, and A were taken to belong to the whole of B, and B to some C, AB is wholly false and BC true, and the conclusion true. It is likewise if the premise AB is negative. A can belong to the whole of B and not belong to some C, while B belongs to some C: as animal belongs to every man, does not follow some white thing, and man belongs to some white thing. So if, with man laid down as the middle, A were taken to belong to no B, and B to belong to some C, the conclusion will be true though the premise AB is wholly false. And if the premise AB is false in part, the conclusion will be true. Nothing prevents A from belonging to some B and some C, and B to some C: as animal belongs to some beautiful thing and some large thing, and beautiful to some large thing. If, then, A is taken to belong to every B, and B to some C, the premise AB will be false in part, BC true, and the conclusion true. It is likewise if the premise AB is negative, for the same terms will serve, placed in the same way, for the proof.

@p
@greek Πάλιν εἰ ἡ μὲν Α Β ἀληθὴς ἡ δὲ Β Γ ψευδής
Again, if AB is true and BC false, the conclusion will be true. Nothing prevents A from belonging to the whole of B and to some C, while B belongs to no C: as animal belongs to every swan and to some black thing, and swan to no black thing. So if A were taken to belong to every B and B to some C, the conclusion will be true though BC is false. It is likewise when the premise AB is taken as negative. A can belong to no B and not belong to some C, while B belongs to no C: as a genus does to the species of another genus and to an accident of its own species. Animal belongs to no number and to some white thing, and number to nothing white. If, then, number is laid down as the middle, and A is taken to belong to no B and B to some C, A will not belong to some C, which was true; and the premise AB is true, BC false. And if AB is false in part, and BC false too, the conclusion will be true. Nothing prevents A from belonging to some B and to some C, each, while B belongs to no C: for instance if B is contrary to C, and both are accidents of the same genus. Animal belongs to some white thing and some black thing, and white to nothing black. If, then, A is taken to belong to every B and B to some C, the conclusion will be true. It is the same if AB is taken as negative, for the same terms will be laid down in the same way for the proof. And when both premises are false the conclusion will be true. A can belong to no B and to some C, while B belongs to no C: as a genus does to the species of another genus and to an accident of its own species. Animal belongs to no number and to some white thing, and number to nothing white. If, then, A is taken to belong to every B and B to some C, the conclusion is true and both premises false. It is likewise if AB is negative. Nothing prevents A from belonging to the whole of B and not belonging to some C, nor B from belonging to no C: as animal belongs to every swan and does not belong to some black thing, and swan to nothing black. So if A were taken to belong to no B, and B to some C, A will not belong to some C. The conclusion is true, but the premises false.

@ch APr.II.3 | True conclusions from false premises, in the second figure
In the second figure a true conclusion can follow from false premises in every combination, both wholly and partly false, in universal and particular syllogisms alike.

@p
In the middle figure it is possible in every way to conclude something true through false premises: when both premises are taken as wholly false, and when each is false in part; when one is true and the other false, whichever is laid down as false; and in both universal and particular syllogisms. If A belongs to no B and to every C (as animal belongs to no stone and to every horse), then if the premises are laid down the opposite way, and A is taken to belong to every B and to no C, from premises wholly false the conclusion will be true. It is likewise if A belongs to every B and to no C, for the syllogism will be the same.

@p
@greek Πάλιν εἰ ἡ μὲν ἑτέρα ὅλη ψευδὴς
Again, if one premise is wholly false and the other wholly true. Nothing prevents A from belonging to every B and every C, while B belongs to no C: as a genus does to species not under one another. Animal belongs to every horse and every man, and no man is a horse. If, then, A is taken to belong to all of one and none of the other, one premise will be wholly false and the other wholly true, and the conclusion true, whichever term the negative is placed with. And if one is false in part and the other wholly true. A can belong to some B and to every C, while B belongs to no C: as animal belongs to some white thing and to every raven, and white to no raven. If, then, A is taken to belong to no B and to the whole of C, the premise AB is false in part, AC wholly true, and the conclusion true. It is likewise when the negative is moved, for the proof is through the same terms. And if the affirmative premise is false in part and the negative wholly true. Nothing prevents A from belonging to some B and not belonging to the whole of C, and B to no C: as animal belongs to some white thing and to no pitch, and white to no pitch. So if A is taken to belong to the whole of B and to no C, AB is false in part, AC wholly true, and the conclusion true. And if both premises are false in part, the conclusion will be true. A can belong to some B and some C, while B belongs to no C: as animal belongs to some white thing and some black thing, and white to nothing black. If, then, A is taken to belong to every B and to no C, both premises are false in part, and the conclusion true. It is likewise when the negative is moved, through the same terms.

@p
@greek Φανερὸν δὲ καὶ ἐπὶ τῶν ἐν μέρει συλλογισμῶν· οὐδὲν
It is plain with particular syllogisms too. Nothing prevents A from belonging to every B and to some C, while B does not belong to some C: as animal belongs to every man and to some white thing, but man will not belong to some white thing. If, then, A is laid down to belong to no B and to some C, the universal premise is wholly false, the particular true, and the conclusion true. It is the same when AB is taken as affirmative. A can belong to no B and not belong to some C, while B does not belong to some C: as animal belongs to nothing inanimate and to some white thing, and inanimate will not belong to some white thing. If, then, A is laid down to belong to every B and not to belong to some C, the universal premise AB is wholly false, AC true, and the conclusion true. And when the universal premise is laid down as true and the particular as false. Nothing prevents A from following neither B nor C at all, while B does not belong to some C: as animal belongs to no number and to nothing inanimate, and number does not follow some inanimate thing. If, then, A is laid down to belong to no B and to some C, the conclusion will be true and so will the universal premise, but the particular will be false. It is the same when the universal is laid down as affirmative. A can belong to the whole of B and of C, while B does not follow some C: as a genus does to the species and the difference; for animal follows every man and the whole of what is footed, but man not everything footed. So if A is taken to belong to the whole of B and not to belong to some C, the universal premise is true, the particular false, and the conclusion true. It is plain too that from two false premises the conclusion will be true, if A can belong to the whole of B and of C while B does not follow some C. For if A is taken to belong to no B and to some C, both premises are false, but the conclusion is true. It is likewise when the universal premise is affirmative and the particular negative. A can follow no B and every C, while B does not belong to some C: as animal follows no knowledge and every man, and knowledge does not follow every man. If, then, A is taken to belong to the whole of B and not to follow some C, the premises are false, but the conclusion true.

@ch APr.II.4 | True conclusions from false premises, in the third figure; why the false cannot follow from the true
In the third figure too a true conclusion can follow from false premises in every combination. So a false conclusion shows that some premise is false, but a true conclusion shows nothing about the premises; for the same thing cannot follow of necessity both from something's being and from its not being.

@taylor 3
@p
In the last figure too there will be a true conclusion through false premises: when both are wholly false, when each is false in part, when one is wholly true and the other false, when one is false in part and the other wholly true, and the reverse, and in whatever other ways the premises can be changed. Nothing prevents neither A nor B from belonging to any C, while A belongs to some B: as neither man nor footed follows anything inanimate, yet man belongs to some footed thing. If, then, A and B are taken to belong to every C, the premises are wholly false, but the conclusion is true. It is the same when one is negative and the other affirmative. B can belong to no C and A to every C, while A does not belong to some B: as black belongs to no swan and animal to every swan, and animal not to everything black. So if B is taken to belong to every C and A to none, A will not belong to some B; and the conclusion is true, but the premises false. And if each is false in part, the conclusion will be true. Nothing prevents both A and B from belonging to some C, and A to some B: as white and beautiful belong to some animal, and white to something beautiful. If, then, A and B are laid down to belong to every C, the premises are false in part, but the conclusion is true. It is likewise when AC is laid down as negative. Nothing prevents A from not belonging to some C, and B from belonging to some, while A does not belong to every B: as white does not belong to some animal and beautiful belongs to some, and white not to everything beautiful. So if A is taken to belong to no C and B to every C, both premises are false in part, but the conclusion is true.

@p
@greek Ὡσαύτως δὲ καὶ τῆς μὲν ὅλης ψευδοῦς
It is the same when one is taken as wholly false and the other as wholly true. Both A and B can follow every C, while A does not belong to some B: as animal and white follow every swan, but animal does not belong to everything white. With such terms laid down, if B is taken to belong to the whole of C and A not to belong to the whole of it, BC will be wholly true, AC wholly false, and the conclusion true. It is likewise if BC is false and AC true, for the same terms serve for the proof. And also if both are taken as affirmative. Nothing prevents B from following every C, and A from not belonging to the whole of it, while A belongs to some B: as animal belongs to every swan, black to no swan, and black belongs to some animal. So if A and B are taken to belong to every C, BC is wholly true, AC wholly false, and the conclusion true. It is likewise when AC is taken as true, for the proof is through the same terms.

@p
@greek Πάλιν τῆς μὲν ὅλης ἀληθοῦς οὔσης
Again, when one is wholly true and the other false in part. B can belong to every C and A to some C, while A belongs to some B: as two-footed belongs to every man, beautiful not to every man, and beautiful belongs to some two-footed thing. If, then, A and B are taken to belong to the whole of C, BC is wholly true, AC false in part, but the conclusion true. It is likewise when AC is taken as true and BC as false in part, for the proof will be with the same terms moved. And when one is negative and the other affirmative. Since B can belong to the whole of C and A to some C, and when they are so, A does not belong to every B: if, then, B is taken to belong to the whole of C, and A to none of it, the negative is false in part, the other wholly true, and so is the conclusion. Again, since it has been shown that when A belongs to no C and B to some C, A may not belong to some B, it is plain that when AC is wholly true and BC false in part, the conclusion can be true. For if A is taken to belong to no C and B to every C, AC is wholly true and BC false in part. It is plain with particular syllogisms too that there will in every way be something true through false premises. The same terms must be taken as when the premises are universal: affirmative ones in affirmative syllogisms, negative ones in negative. For it makes no difference to setting out the terms whether what belongs to none is taken to belong to all, or what belongs to some is taken to belong universally. It is likewise with negatives.

@p
@greek Φανερὸν οὖν ὅτι ἂν μὲν ᾖ τὸ συμπέρασμα ψεῦδος
It is plain, then, that if the conclusion is false, the things the argument is from must be false, either all or some of them; but when it is true, it is not necessary that any or all of them be true. Even when none of the premises in the syllogism is true, the conclusion can likewise be true, though not of necessity. The reason is that when two things are so related that if one is, the other must be, then if the second is not, neither will the first be; but if the second is, the first need not be. But it is impossible that the same thing should be necessary both when something is and when it is not. I mean, for instance, that if A is white, B must be large, and if A is not white, B must be large. For when, if this, A, is white, that, B, must be large, and if B is large, C must not be white, then if A is white, C must not be white. And when, of two things, if one is the other must be, then if the second is not, the first must not be. So if B is not large, A cannot be white. But if, when A is not white, B must be large, it follows of necessity that if B is not large, B itself is large. And this is impossible. For if B is not large, A will of necessity not be white; and if, when A is not white, B will be large, it follows, as through three terms, that if B is not large, it is large.
!note This is the principle behind "a false conclusion refutes a premise, but a true conclusion does not confirm the premises". The last argument shows that if B followed both from A and from not-A, a contradiction would follow: B large if not large.

@ch APr.II.5 | Circular proof in the first figure
A proof in a circle takes the conclusion, with one premise converted, to prove the other premise. It works fully only with terms that convert with one another, and even then it uses the conclusion to prove itself.

@p
To prove in a circle, or from one another, is to take the conclusion, and one premise converted in its predication, and to conclude the remaining premise, which was taken in the other syllogism. For instance, if one had to prove that A belongs to every C, and proved it through B, and then proved that A belongs to B by taking A as belonging to C and C to B (whereas before B was taken, the other way round, as belonging to C). Or if one has to prove that B belongs to C, one takes A as said of C, which was the conclusion, and B as belonging to A (whereas before A was taken, the other way round, as said of B). It is not possible to prove them from one another in any other way. If one takes another middle, it is not in a circle, since none of the same terms is taken; if one takes one of these, it must be only one of them, for if both, the conclusion will be the same, and it must be different.
!note Circular proof is criticised in Posterior Analytics I.3: a proof in a circle amounts to saying "if A is, A is", which proves anything.

@p
@greek Ἐν μὲν οὖν τοῖς μὴ
In terms that do not convert, the syllogism comes from one premise left unproved, for it is not possible to prove through these terms that the third belongs to the middle or the middle to the first. In terms that convert, all can be proved through one another: for instance if A, B and C convert with one another. Let AC be proved through B as the middle; and again AB through the conclusion and the premise BC converted; and likewise BC through the conclusion and the premise AB converted. But one must prove the premises CB and BA, for only these have we used unproved. If, then, B is taken to belong to every C and C to every A, there will be a syllogism of B in relation to A. Again, if C is taken to belong to every A, and A to every B, C must belong to every B. In both these syllogisms the premise CA has been taken unproved, for the others were proved. So if we prove this, all will have been proved through one another. If C is taken to belong to every B, and B to every A, both premises taken have been proved, and C must belong to A. It is plain, then, that only in terms that convert can proofs come about in a circle and through one another; in the others, as we said before. And it turns out in these too that we use the very thing proved for the proof. C is proved of B, and B of A, by taking C as said of A; and C is proved of A through these premises; so we use the conclusion for the proof.

@p
@greek Ἐπὶ δὲ τῶν στερητικῶν συλλογισμῶν ὧδε
With negative syllogisms, proof from one another goes like this. Let B belong to every C, and A to no B; the conclusion is that A belongs to no C. If, then, one has to conclude again that A belongs to no B, which was taken before, let A belong to no C, and C to every B; for so the premise is reversed. If one has to conclude that B belongs to C, AB must no longer be converted in the same way (for "B belongs to no A" and "A belongs to no B" are the same premise), but one must take it that B belongs to everything A belongs to none of. Let A belong to no C, which was the conclusion, and let B be taken to belong to everything A belongs to none of; then B must belong to every C. So with three propositions, each has become a conclusion; and proving in a circle is this: taking the conclusion, and one premise reversed, and concluding the remaining one.

@p
@greek Ἐπὶ δὲ τῶν ἐν μέρει
With particular syllogisms, the universal premise cannot be proved through the others, but the particular one can. That the universal cannot be proved is plain, for a universal is proved through universals, and the conclusion is not universal, while one must prove from the conclusion and the other premise. Further, no syllogism comes about at all when the premise is converted, since both premises become particular. But the particular premise can be proved. Let A be proved of some C through B. If, then, B is taken to belong to every A, and the conclusion stays, B will belong to some C; for the first figure comes about, with A as the middle. If the syllogism is negative, the universal premise cannot be proved, for the reason said before; the particular one can, if AB is converted likewise as with the universal ones, by an added premise: for instance, that B belongs to some of what A does not belong to some of. Otherwise no syllogism comes about, because the particular premise is negative.

@ch APr.II.6 | Circular proof in the second figure
In the second figure only the negative conclusion can be proved in a circle, and of particular syllogisms only the particular premise, when the universal premise is affirmative.

@p
In the second figure the affirmative cannot be proved in this way, but the negative can. The affirmative is not proved, because the premises are not both affirmative: the conclusion is negative, and the affirmative was proved from two affirmatives. The negative is proved like this. Let A belong to every B and to no C; the conclusion is that B belongs to no C. If, then, B is taken to belong to every A, A must belong to no C; for the second figure comes about, with B as the middle. If AB was taken as negative and the other as affirmative, the first figure will come about. For C belongs to every A and B to no C, so B belongs to no A, and so A to no B. So no syllogism comes about through the conclusion and one premise, but there will be one when another premise is added. If the syllogism is not universal, the universal premise is not proved, for the reason we gave before, but the particular one is, when the universal is affirmative. Let A belong to every B and not to every C; the conclusion is BC. If, then, B is taken to belong to every A and not to every C, A will not belong to some C, with B as the middle. If the universal premise is negative, AC will not be proved when AB is converted, for it turns out that either both premises or one of them becomes negative, so there will be no syllogism. But it will be proved likewise as with universals, if it is taken that A belongs to some of what B does not belong to some of.

@ch APr.II.7 | Circular proof in the third figure
In the third figure the universal premise can never be proved in a circle, and the particular only sometimes. A summary sets out which figures each circular proof passes through.

@p
In the third figure, when both premises are taken as universal, they cannot be proved through one another. A universal is proved through universals, and the conclusion in this figure is always particular; so it is plain that the universal premise cannot be proved at all through this figure.

@p
@greek Ἐὰν δʼ ἡ μὲν ἦ καθόλου ἡ δʼ ἐν μέρει
If one is universal and the other particular, sometimes it can be done and sometimes not. When both are taken as affirmative and the universal is put with the minor extreme, it can; when with the other, it cannot. Let A belong to every C and B to some C; the conclusion is AB. If, then, C is taken to belong to every A, it has been proved that C belongs to some B, but not that B belongs to some C. Yet if C belongs to some B, B must belong to some C. But it is not the same for this to belong to that and that to this; one must add that if this belongs to some of that, the other belongs to some of this. And once this is taken, the syllogism no longer comes from the conclusion and the other premise. But if B belongs to every C and A to some C, AC can be proved, when it is taken that C belongs to every B and A to some B. For if C belongs to every B and A to some B, A must belong to some C, with B as the middle. And when one premise is affirmative and the other negative, and the affirmative is universal, the other will be proved. Let B belong to every C, and A not belong to some C; the conclusion is that A does not belong to some B. If, then, it is added that C belongs to every B, A must not belong to some C, with B as the middle. But when the negative premise becomes universal, the other is not proved, unless as before it is taken that B belongs to some of what A does not belong to some of. For instance, if A belongs to no C and B to some C, the conclusion is that A does not belong to some B. If, then, it is taken that C belongs to some of what A does not belong to some of, C must belong to some B. Otherwise the other premise cannot be proved by converting the universal one, for there will be no syllogism at all.

@p
@greek Φανερὸν οὖν ὅτι ἐν μὲν τῷ πρώτῳ σχήματι
It is plain, then, that in the first figure the proof through one another comes about through the third figure and the first: through the first when the conclusion is affirmative, through the last when it is negative; for it is taken that the other belongs to all of what this belongs to none of. In the middle figure, when the syllogism is universal, it comes about through the figure itself and the first; when particular, through itself and the last. In the third, all are through itself. It is plain too that in the third and the middle figure the syllogisms that do not come about through the figure itself are either not by circular proof or are incomplete.
!note This summary is bracketed in Ross's text as a later addition.

@ch APr.II.8 | Converting a syllogism in the first figure
To convert a syllogism is to reverse its conclusion and, keeping one premise, to refute the other. Reversing the conclusion to its contrary and to its contradictory gives different results, and particular syllogisms behave differently from universal ones.

@p
To convert is to transpose the conclusion and make the syllogism that either the extreme will not belong to the middle, or the middle to the last term. For if the conclusion is reversed and one premise stays, the other must be done away with; for if it held, the conclusion would hold too. It makes a difference whether the conclusion is reversed to its contradictory or to its contrary, for the same syllogism does not come about either way; this will be clear from what follows. By contradictory I mean "to all" against "not to all", and "to some" against "to none"; by contrary, "to all" against "to none", and "to some" against "not to some". Let A have been proved of C through B as the middle. If A is taken to belong to no C and to every B, B will belong to no C. And if A belongs to no C and B to every C, A will not belong to every B, though not simply to none; for the universal was not proved through the last figure. In general, the premise at the major extreme cannot be refuted universally by conversion, for it is always done away with through the third figure, since both premises must be taken in relation to the last extreme. It is the same if the syllogism is negative. Let A have been proved to belong to no C through B. Then if A is taken to belong to every C and to no B, B will belong to no C. And if A and B belong to every C, A will belong to some B; but it belonged to none.

@p
@greek Ἐὰν δʼ ἀντικειμένως ἀντιστραφῇ τὸ συμπέρασμα
If the conclusion is reversed to its contradictory, the syllogisms will be contradictory too, and not universal; for one premise becomes particular, and so the conclusion will be particular. Let the syllogism be affirmative and be converted this way. Then if A does not belong to every C and belongs to every B, B will not belong to every C; and if A does not belong to every C and B belongs to every C, A will not belong to every B. It is likewise if the syllogism is negative. If A belongs to some C and to no B, B will not belong to some C, not simply to none; and if A belongs to some C and B to every C, as was taken at the start, A will belong to some B.

@p
@greek Ἐπὶ δὲ τῶν ἐν μέρει συλλογισμῶν ὅταν μὲν
With particular syllogisms, when the conclusion is reversed to its contradictory, both premises are done away with; when to its contrary, neither. For it no longer turns out, as in universals, that one does away with a premise while the conclusion falls short in the conversion; one does not do away with it at all. Let A have been proved of some C. Then if A is taken to belong to no C and B to some C, A will not belong to some B; and if A belongs to no C and B to every C, B will belong to no C. So both are done away with. But if it is reversed to its contrary, neither is. If A does not belong to some C and belongs to every B, B will not belong to some C; but the original premise is not yet done away with, for it is possible for it to belong to some and not to belong to some. And of the universal premise, AB, no syllogism comes about at all; for if A does not belong to some C and B belongs to some C, neither premise is universal. It is likewise if the syllogism is negative. If A is taken to belong to every C, both premises are done away with; if to some, neither. The proof is the same.

@ch APr.II.9 | Converting a syllogism in the second figure
In the second figure the premise at the major extreme cannot be refuted by the contrary, while the other premise is refuted in the same way as the conclusion is reversed.

@taylor 8
@p
In the second figure the premise at the major extreme cannot be done away with by the contrary, whichever way the conversion is made; for the conclusion will always be in the third figure, and there was no universal syllogism in that. The other premise we shall do away with likewise as the conversion: by "likewise" I mean that if the conversion is to the contrary, it is done away with by the contrary, and if to the contradictory, by the contradictory. Let A belong to every B and to no C; the conclusion is BC. If, then, B is taken to belong to every C and AB stays, A will belong to every C, for the first figure comes about. And if B belongs to every C and A to no C, A will not belong to every B, in the last figure. If BC is reversed to its contradictory, AB will be proved likewise, and AC by its contradictory. For if B belongs to some C and A to no C, A will not belong to some B. Again if B belongs to some C and A to every B, A will belong to some C, so the syllogism becomes contradictory. It will be proved likewise if the premises are the other way round.

@p
@greek ἐπὶ μέρους ὁ συλλογισμός, ἐναντίως
If the syllogism is particular, when the conclusion is reversed to its contrary neither premise is done away with, as in the first figure either, but when to its contradictory, both are. Let A be laid down to belong to no B and to some C; the conclusion is BC. If, then, B is laid down to belong to some C and AB stays, the conclusion will be that A does not belong to some C; but the original premise has not been done away with, for it is possible for A to belong and not belong to some. Again if B belongs to some C and A to some C, there will be no syllogism, for neither of the premises taken is universal; so AB is not done away with. But if the reversal is to the contradictory, both are done away with. For if B belongs to every C and A to no B, A will belong to no C; but it belonged to some. Again if B belongs to every C and A to some C, A will belong to some B. The proof is the same if the universal premise is affirmative.

@ch APr.II.10 | Converting a syllogism in the third figure
In the third figure the contrary of the conclusion refutes neither premise, while the contradictory refutes both. A summary says through which figures each premise is refuted in each figure.

@p
In the third figure, when the conclusion is reversed to its contrary, neither premise is done away with in any of the syllogisms; when to its contradictory, both are, in all of them. Let A have been proved to belong to some B, with C taken as the middle, and let the premises be universal. Then if A is taken not to belong to some B, and B to belong to every C, no syllogism of A and C comes about. Nor if A does not belong to some B and belongs to every C will there be a syllogism of B and C. It will be proved likewise if the premises are not universal. For either both must be particular through the conversion, or the universal must be put with the minor extreme, and in that way there was no syllogism either in the first figure or in the middle one. But if the reversal is to the contradictory, both premises are done away with. For if A belongs to no B and B to every C, A will belong to no C. Again if A belongs to no B and to every C, B will belong to no C. And it is the same if one is not universal. For if A belongs to no B and B to some C, A will not belong to some C; and if A belongs to no B and to every C, B will belong to no C.

@p
@greek Ὁμοίως δὲ καὶ εἰ στερητικὸς ὁ συλλογισμός. δεδείχθω
It is likewise if the syllogism is negative. Let A have been proved not to belong to some B, and let BC be affirmative and AC negative, for that is how the syllogism came about. When the contrary of the conclusion is taken, there will be no syllogism. For if A belongs to some B and B to every C, there was no syllogism of A and C. Nor if A belongs to some B and to no C was there a syllogism of B and C. So the premises are not done away with. But when the contradictory is taken, they are. For if A belongs to every B and B to every C, A will belong to every C; but it belonged to none. Again if A belongs to every B and to no C, B will belong to no C; but it belonged to every C. It is proved likewise if the premises are not universal. For AC becomes universal and negative, and the other particular and affirmative. If A belongs to every B and B to some C, it turns out that A belongs to some C; but it belonged to none. Again if A belongs to every B and to no C, B will belong to no C; but it was laid down to belong to some. If A belongs to some B and B to some C, no syllogism comes about; nor if A belongs to some B and to no C. So in the one way the premises are done away with, and in the other they are not.

@p
@greek Φανερὸν οὖν διὰ τῶν εἰρημένων
It is plain, then, from what has been said, how a syllogism comes about in each figure when the conclusion is converted, and when it is contrary to the premise and when contradictory; and that in the first figure the syllogisms come about through the middle and the last figure, the premise at the minor extreme always being done away with through the middle figure and that at the major through the last; in the second figure, through the first and the last, the premise at the minor extreme always through the first figure and that at the major through the last; and in the third, through the first and the middle, the premise at the major always through the first, and that at the minor through the middle.

@ch APr.II.11 | Reduction to the impossible in the first figure
A syllogism through the impossible lays down the contradictory of the conclusion and adds another premise. In the first figure every conclusion except the universal affirmative can be proved this way, and the hypothesis must always be the contradictory, not the contrary.

@p
What converting is, how it works in each figure, and what syllogism comes about, is plain. A syllogism through the impossible is proved when the contradictory of the conclusion is laid down and another premise is added. It comes about in all the figures, for it is like conversion, except that it differs to this extent: one converts when a syllogism has already come about and both premises have been taken, while one leads back to the impossible when the opposite has not been agreed beforehand but it is plain that it is true. The terms stand likewise in both, and both are taken the same way. For instance, if A belongs to every B, and C is the middle, then if A is supposed either not to belong to every B or to belong to none, and to belong to every C (which was true), C must belong either to no B or not to every B. But this is impossible, so the supposition is false, and its opposite true. It is likewise in the other figures: whatever admits of conversion admits also of the syllogism through the impossible.

@p
@greek Τὰ μὲν οὖν ἄλλα προβλήματα πάντα δείκνυται
All the other problems are proved through the impossible in all the figures, but the universal affirmative is proved in the middle and the third figure, and not in the first. Let A be supposed not to belong to every B, or to belong to none, and let another premise be added on either side: either that C belongs to every A, or that B belongs to every D; for so the first figure would come about. If, then, it is supposed that A does not belong to every B, no syllogism comes about, whichever way the premise is taken. If it is supposed that A belongs to no B, then when BD is added there will be a syllogism of something false, but the thing proposed is not proved. For if A belongs to no B, and B to every D, A belongs to no D. Let this be impossible; then it is false that A belongs to no B. But if "to none" is false, it does not follow that "to all" is true. If CA is added, no syllogism comes about, nor when it is supposed that A does not belong to every B. So it is plain that "belongs to all" is not proved in the first figure through the impossible.

@p
@greek Τὸ δέ γε τινὶ καὶ τὸ μηδενὶ
But "to some", "to none" and "not to all" are proved. Let A be supposed to belong to no B, and B taken to belong to every or some C. Then A must belong to no C, or not to every C. But this is impossible (let it be true and plain that A belongs to every C); so if the supposition is false, A must belong to some B. If the other premise is taken with A, there will be no syllogism. Nor when the contrary of the conclusion is supposed, for instance "does not belong to some". So it is plain that the contradictory must be supposed. Again, let A be supposed to belong to some B, and C taken to belong to every A. Then C must belong to some B. Let this be impossible, so that the supposition is false; and if so, it is true that A belongs to none. It is likewise if CA was taken as negative. If the premise taken is with B, there will be no syllogism. If the contrary is supposed, there will be a syllogism and the impossible, but what was proposed is not proved. Let A be supposed to belong to every B, and C taken to belong to every A. Then C must belong to every B. But this is impossible, so it is false that A belongs to every B. But it is not yet necessary that if not to all, then to none. It is likewise if the other premise were taken with B: there will be a syllogism and the impossible, but the hypothesis is not done away with. So the contradictory must be supposed. To prove that A does not belong to every B, one must suppose that it belongs to every B. For if A belongs to every B and C to every A, C belongs to every B; so if this is impossible, the supposition is false. It is likewise if the other premise were taken with B; and the same if CA were negative, for a syllogism comes about that way too. But if the negative is with B, nothing is proved. If it is supposed to belong not to all but to some, what is proved is not that it belongs not to all but that it belongs to none. For if A belongs to some B and C to every A, C will belong to some B. So if this is impossible, it is false that A belongs to some B, and so "to none" is true. But when this is proved, the truth is done away with as well, for A belonged to some B and not to some. Further, nothing impossible follows from the hypothesis; for then it would be false, since one cannot deduce what is false from truths; but in fact it is true, for A does belong to some B. So one must not suppose that it belongs to some, but to all. It is likewise if we were proving that A does not belong to some B; for if "not to belong to some" and "not to belong to all" are the same, the proof of both is the same.

@p
@greek Φανερὸν οὖν ὅτι οὐ τὸ ἐναντίον ἀλλὰ τὸ ἀντικείμενον
It is plain, then, that in all the syllogisms what must be supposed is not the contrary but the contradictory. For in this way the conclusion will be necessary and the claim reputable. If either the affirmation or the denial holds of everything, then when it is proved that the denial does not, the affirmation must be true. Again, if one does not lay down that the affirmation is true, it is reputable to claim the denial. But to claim the contrary suits neither purpose: it is not necessary that if "to none" is false, "to all" is true, nor is it reputable that if one is false, the other is true.
!note "Reputable" renders ἔνδοξον, what is held by people generally or by the wise; it is the word the Topics uses for the premises of dialectic.

@ch APr.II.12 | Reduction to the impossible in the second figure
In the middle figure all four kinds of conclusion, the universal affirmative included, can be proved through the impossible.

@p
It is plain, then, that in the first figure all the other problems are proved through the impossible, but the universal affirmative is not. In the middle and the last figures this too is proved. Let A be laid down not to belong to every B, and A be taken to belong to every C. Then if A does not belong to every B and belongs to every C, C does not belong to every B. But this is impossible (let it be plain that C belongs to every B), so the supposition is false, and it is true that A belongs to every B. If the contrary is supposed, there will be a syllogism and the impossible, but what was proposed is not proved. For if A belongs to no B and to every C, C belongs to no B. This is impossible, so it is false that A belongs to none. But if this is false, it does not follow that "to all" is true. To show that A belongs to some B, let A be supposed to belong to no B, and to belong to every C. Then C must belong to no B; so if this is impossible, A must belong to some B. If it is supposed not to belong to some, the result will be the same as in the first figure. Again, let A be supposed to belong to some B, and to no C. Then C must not belong to some B. But it belonged to all, so the supposition is false, and A will belong to no B. To show that A does not belong to every B, let it be supposed to belong to every B, and to no C. Then C must belong to no B. This is impossible, so it is true that A does not belong to every B. It is plain, then, that all the syllogisms come about through the middle figure.

@ch APr.II.13 | Reduction to the impossible in the third figure
In the last figure too every problem can be proved through the impossible. In every such proof the contradictory must be supposed, and so the middle figure proves the affirmative in a way, and the last the universal.

@p
It is likewise through the last figure. Let A be laid down not to belong to some B, and C to belong to every B; then A does not belong to some C. If this is impossible, it is false that A does not belong to some B, so it is true that it belongs to all. If it is supposed to belong to none, there will be a syllogism and the impossible, but what was proposed is not proved; for if the contrary is supposed, the result will be the same as in the earlier cases. But this hypothesis must be taken to prove that it belongs to some. For if A belongs to no B, and C to some B, A will not belong to every C. If this is false, it is true that A belongs to some B. To show that A belongs to no B, let it be supposed to belong to some, and C be taken to belong to every B. Then A must belong to some C. But it belonged to none, so it is false that A belongs to some B. If A is supposed to belong to every B, what was proposed is not proved; this hypothesis must be taken to prove that it does not belong to all. For if A belongs to every B and C to every B, A belongs to some C. But this was not so, so it is false that it belongs to all. And if so, "not to all" is true. If it is supposed to belong to some, the result will be the same as in the cases already stated.

@p
@greek Φανερὸν οὖν ὅτι ἐν ἅπασι τοῖς διὰ τοῦ ἀδυνάτου
It is plain, then, that in all reasoning through the impossible the contradictory must be supposed. It is clear too that the affirmative is proved in a way in the middle figure, and the universal in the last.

@ch APr.II.14 | Proof through the impossible and direct proof
Proof through the impossible differs from direct proof in laying down what it means to refute. Whatever is proved one way can be proved the other way with the same terms, though in a different figure.

@p
Proof leading to the impossible differs from direct proof in that it lays down what it wants to do away with, and leads back to something agreed to be false; while direct proof begins from positions agreed. Both take two premises agreed; but the one takes those the syllogism comes from, the other takes one of these and, as the other, the contradictory of the conclusion. In the one case the conclusion need not be known beforehand, nor need one assume in advance whether it holds or not; in the other one must assume that it does not. It makes no difference whether the conclusion is an affirmation or a denial; it is likewise with both.
!flag a demonstration leading to the impossible differs from an ostensive demonstration | Taylor and Jenkinson both say "ostensive" for δεικτική, a word no modern reader uses; this version says "direct proof", the term used in logic textbooks today.

@p
@greek Ἅπαν δὲ τὸ δεικτικῶς περαινόμενον
Everything concluded directly can also be proved through the impossible, and what is proved through the impossible can be proved directly, through the same terms (though not in the same figures). When the syllogism comes about in the first figure, the truth will be in the middle or the last figure: the negative in the middle, the affirmative in the last. When the syllogism is in the middle figure, the truth is in the first, for all problems. When the syllogism is in the last figure, the truth is in the first and the middle: affirmatives in the first, negatives in the middle. Let it have been proved through the first figure that A belongs to no B, or not to every B. Then the hypothesis was that A belongs to some B, and C was taken to belong to every A and to no B; for that is how the syllogism and the impossible came about. But this is the middle figure, if C belongs to every A and to no B. And it is plain from these that A belongs to no B. It is likewise if it has been proved that A does not belong to every B. The hypothesis is that it belongs to every B, and C was taken to belong to every A and not to every B. And it is the same if CA were taken as negative, for so too the middle figure comes about. Again, let it have been proved that A belongs to some B. The hypothesis, then, is that it belongs to none, and B was taken to belong to every C, and A to every or some C; for so the impossible will come about. But this is the last figure, if A and B belong to every C. And it is plain from these that A must belong to some B. It is likewise if B or A were taken to belong to some C.

@p
@greek Πάλιν ἐν τῷ μέσῳ σχήματι δεδείχθω
Again, in the middle figure let it have been proved that A belongs to every B. The hypothesis was that A does not belong to every B, and A has been taken to belong to every C, and C to every B; for so the impossible will come about. But this is the first figure, A belonging to every C and C to every B. It is likewise if A has been proved to belong to some B: the hypothesis was that A belongs to no B, and A has been taken to belong to every C, and C to some B. If the syllogism is negative, the hypothesis is that A belongs to some B, and A has been taken to belong to no C and C to every B, so the first figure comes about. And if the syllogism is not universal, but A has been proved not to belong to some B, it is the same: the hypothesis is that A belongs to every B, and A has been taken to belong to no C and C to some B; for so the first figure comes about.

@p
@greek Πάλιν ἐν τῷ τρίτῳ σχήματι δεδείχθω
Again, in the third figure let it have been proved that A belongs to every B. The hypothesis was that A does not belong to every B, and C has been taken to belong to every B and A to every C; for so the impossible will come about. But this is the first figure. It is the same if the proof concerns some: the hypothesis is that A belongs to no B, and C has been taken to belong to some B and A to every C. If the syllogism is negative, the hypothesis is that A belongs to some B, and C has been taken to belong to no A and to every B; and this is the middle figure. It is likewise if the proof is not universal: the hypothesis will be that A belongs to every B, and C has been taken to belong to no A and to some B; and this is the middle figure.

@p
@greek Φανερὸν οὖν ὅτι διὰ τῶν αὐτῶν ὅρων
It is plain, then, that each of the problems can be proved directly through the same terms as through the impossible. Likewise, when the syllogisms are direct, it will be possible to lead them back to the impossible in the terms taken, when the premise contradictory to the conclusion is taken. For the same syllogisms come about as through conversion, so that we have at once the figures through which each will come about. It is clear, then, that every problem is proved in both ways, through the impossible and directly, and it is not possible to separate the one from the other.

@ch APr.II.15 | Syllogisms from opposed premises
Premises can be opposed in wording in four ways but in truth in three. No syllogism comes from opposed premises in the first figure; in the second both kinds are possible, in the third only negative ones. Such a syllogism always concludes against the thing itself.

@taylor 14
@p
In which figure one can deduce from opposed premises, and in which one cannot, will become plain in this way. I say that premises are opposed in wording in four ways: "to all" against "to none", "to all" against "not to all", "to some" against "to none", and "to some" against "not to some"; but in truth in three, since "to some" and "not to some" are opposed only in wording. Of these I call the universal ones contrary, "to belong to all" against "to belong to none" (for instance, that every kind of knowledge is good against that none is), and the others opposed.

@p
@greek Ἐν μὲν οὖν τῷ πρώτῳ σχήματι οὐκ ἔστιν ἐξ ἀντικειμένων
In the first figure there is no syllogism from opposed premises, neither affirmative nor negative: not affirmative, because both premises must be affirmative, and opposed premises are an affirmation and a denial; not negative, because opposed premises affirm and deny the same thing of the same thing, while the middle in the first figure is not said of both extremes: something else is denied of it, and it is itself predicated of something else; and these premises are not opposed.

@p
@greek Ἐν δὲ τῷ μέσῳ σχήματι καὶ ἐκ τῶν ἀντικειμένων
In the middle figure a syllogism can come about both from opposed and from contrary premises. Let A stand for good, and B and C for knowledge. If one has taken every kind of knowledge to be good and none to be, A belongs to every B and to no C, so B belongs to no C: no knowledge, then, is knowledge. It is likewise if, having taken every kind of knowledge to be good, one took medicine not to be good; for A belongs to every B and to no C, so some knowledge will not be knowledge. And if A belongs to every C and to no B, where B is knowledge, C medicine and A belief: for having taken no knowledge to be belief, one has taken some knowledge to be belief. It differs from the case before in converting the terms; before, the affirmative was with B, now it is with C. And it is the same if the other premise is not universal; for the middle is always what is denied of one term and affirmed of the other. So opposites can be concluded, though not always or in every way, but only if the terms under the middle are either the same or related as whole to part. Otherwise it is impossible, for the premises will in no way be either contrary or opposed.

@p
@greek Ἐν δὲ τῷ τρίτῳ σχήματι καταφατικός μὲν
In the third figure there will never be an affirmative syllogism from opposed premises, for the reason given with the first figure; but a negative one there will be, whether the terms are universal or not. Let B and C stand for knowledge, and A for medicine. If one took every kind of medicine to be knowledge and no medicine to be knowledge, one has taken B to belong to every A and C to none, so some knowledge will not be knowledge. It is likewise if the premise BA is not taken universally; for if some medicine is knowledge and again no medicine is knowledge, it turns out that some knowledge is not knowledge. When the terms are taken universally the premises are contrary; when one is particular, opposed.

@p
@greek Δεῖ δὲ κατανοεῖν ὅτι ἐνδέχεται μὲν οὕτω
One should notice that it is possible to take the opposites in the way we said, that every kind of knowledge is good and again that none is, or that some is not; which does not usually escape notice. But one can also deduce one of them through other questions, or take it in the way described in the Topics. Since there are three oppositions of affirmations, it turns out that opposites can be taken in six ways: "to all" and "to none", "to all" and "not to all", "to some" and "to none"; and each of these can be converted in respect of the terms: A to every B and to no C, or to every C and to no B, or to every one and not to every other, and this again converted in respect of the terms. It is likewise with the third figure. So it is plain in how many ways and in which figures a syllogism can come about through opposed premises.

@p
@greek Φανερὸν δὲ καὶ ὅτι ἐκ ψευδῶν μὲν
It is plain too that while one can deduce something true from false premises, as was said before, one cannot from opposed ones. For the syllogism always turns out contrary to the thing itself: for instance, if something is good, that it is not good, or if it is an animal, that it is not an animal; because the syllogism is from a contradiction and the terms underlying it are either the same or related as whole and part. It is clear too that in fallacies nothing prevents the contradictory of the hypothesis from coming about: for instance, if something is odd, that it is not odd. For the syllogism from opposed premises was contrary; so if one takes such premises, the contradictory of the hypothesis will come about. But one should notice that one cannot conclude contraries from a single syllogism in such a way that the conclusion is that what is not good is good, or anything of that kind, unless such a premise is taken straight away (for instance, that every animal is white and not white, and that man is an animal). Either one must add the contradiction (for instance, that all knowledge is belief, and then take it that medicine is knowledge but no medicine is belief, as refutations come about), or one must use two syllogisms. That the things taken should be contrary in truth is not possible in any other way than this, as was said before.

@ch APr.II.16 | Assuming the point at issue
Assuming the point at issue is one way of failing to prove what was put forward: trying to prove through itself what is not known through itself. Aristotle shows in which figures it can happen.

@taylor 14
@p
To ask for and assume the point at issue is, to take it in its kind, a failure to prove what was put forward. This happens in many ways: if one does not deduce at all; if one deduces through things less known than the conclusion, or likewise unknown; or if one proves what is prior through what is posterior; for proof is from things more convincing and prior. None of these is assuming the point at issue. But since some things are by nature known through themselves and others through other things (the starting points through themselves, what falls under the starting points through other things), when someone tries to prove through itself what is not known through itself, then he assumes the point at issue. One can do this by claiming what was put forward straight away; but one can also pass to other things that are by nature proved through it, and prove the point at issue through them. For instance, if A were proved through B, and B through C, and C were by nature proved through A; for it turns out that those who deduce this way prove A through itself. This is what those do who think they are drawing parallels; for they do not notice that they are themselves assuming things that cannot be proved if the parallels do not exist. So it turns out that those who deduce in this way say that each thing is, if it is; and in this way everything will be known through itself, which is impossible.
!flag begging the question from the beginning | "Begging the question" is the old Latin-based name (petitio principii) and is still Jenkinson's; today it is often heard as "raising the question". The Greek says "asking for what was at the start", so this version says "assuming the point at issue".
!note The "parallels" are probably attempts of the time to prove the parallel postulate of geometry; such proofs quietly assume something that holds only if parallels already exist.

@p
@greek Εἰ οὖν τις ἀδήλου ὄντος
If, then, it being unclear that A belongs to C, and likewise that A belongs to B, one asked for it to be granted that A belongs to B, it is not yet clear whether one assumes the point at issue, but it is clear that one is not proving; for what is likewise unclear is not a starting point of proof. If, however, B is so related to C that they are the same, or plainly convert, or one is contained in the other, then one assumes the point at issue. For one could also prove through those terms that A belongs to B, if they converted (as it is, this is prevented, but not by the way of proving). If one did that, one would be doing what was described, and converting through three terms. It is the same if one took B to belong to C, this being likewise unclear as whether A does: one does not yet assume the point at issue, but one does not prove. But if A and B are the same, either by converting or by A following B, one assumes the point at issue for the same reason. For we have said what assuming the point at issue amounts to: proving through itself what is not clear through itself.

@p
@greek Εἰ οὖν ἐστι τὸ ἐν ἀρχῇ
If, then, assuming the point at issue is proving through itself what is not clear through itself, that is, failing to prove, when what is proved and what proves it are likewise unclear, either because the same things belong to the same thing or the same thing to the same things, then in the middle and third figures assuming the point at issue could happen both ways, and in an affirmative syllogism in the third figure and the first. When the syllogism is negative, it happens when the same things are denied of the same thing; and the two premises do not behave alike (and it is the same in the middle figure), because the terms do not convert in negative syllogisms. Assuming the point at issue concerns, in demonstrations, things that are so in truth; in dialectical arguments, things that are so in opinion.

@ch APr.II.17 | The objection "the false does not come from this"
"The falsehood does not come from this" is said properly only of syllogisms leading to the impossible, when the impossible would follow whether or not the hypothesis held. The impossible must be connected with the original terms.

@taylor 15
@p
"The falsehood does not come from this", which we are often in the habit of saying in arguments, arises first in syllogisms leading to the impossible, when it is said against the contradictory of what was proved by the reduction to the impossible. For unless one has contradicted it, one will not say "not because of this", but that something false was laid down earlier; nor in a direct proof, since that does not lay down what it contradicts. Again, when something has been done away with directly through BC, one cannot say that the syllogism has not come about because of what was laid down. For we say "not because of this" when the syllogism is concluded no less even if this is done away with; which is not the case in direct proofs, since if the position is done away with, the syllogism leading to it will not hold either. It is plain, then, that "not because of this" is said in arguments leading to the impossible, and when the original hypothesis is so related to the impossible that the impossible follows no less whether it holds or not.
!flag he will not say, ‘False cause’ | Jenkinson renders the objection as "False cause", the name it later had in logic manuals; the Greek is the plain phrase "not because of this", which this version keeps.

@p
@greek Ὁ μὲν οὖν φανερώτατος τρόπος
The most plain way in which the falsehood is not because of the position is when the syllogism leading from the middle terms to the impossible is unconnected with the hypothesis, as was said in the Topics too. For this is to put down as a cause what is not a cause: for instance, if someone wanting to prove that the diagonal is incommensurable tried Zeno's argument that motion is impossible, and led back to that impossibility; for the falsehood is in no way at all connected with the original assertion. Another way is if the impossible is connected with the hypothesis but does not result from it. This can happen whether one takes the connection upward or downward. For instance, if A is laid down to belong to B, B to C, and C to D, and it is false that B belongs to D: if, when A is taken away, B still belongs no less to C and C to D, the falsehood would not be due to the original hypothesis. Or again if one takes the connection upward: for instance, if A belongs to B, E to A and F to E, and it is false that F belongs to A; for in this way too the impossible would hold no less when the original hypothesis is done away with. But one must connect the impossible with the original terms, for so it will be due to the hypothesis. Taking the connection downward, it must be connected with the term that is predicated (for if it is impossible that A belongs to D, when A is taken away the falsehood will no longer hold); taking it upward, with the term it is predicated of (for if F cannot belong to B, when B is taken away the impossible will no longer hold). It is likewise when the syllogisms are negative.

@p
@greek Φανερὸν οὖν ὅτι τοῦ ἀδυνάτου μὴ πρὸς
It is plain, then, that when the impossible is not connected with the original terms, the falsehood does not come from the position. Or will the falsehood not always be due to the hypothesis even so? For if A was laid down to belong not to B but to K, and K to C, and C to D, the impossible stays the same this way too (and likewise when one takes the terms upward); so since the impossible follows whether this holds or not, it would not be due to the position. Or must "the falsehood comes about no less when this does not hold" not be understood so that something else is laid down and the impossible follows; but rather when, this being taken away, the same impossibility is concluded through the remaining premises? For it is perhaps nothing strange that the same falsehood should follow from several hypotheses: for instance, that parallels meet, both if the interior angle is less than the exterior and if a triangle has more than two right angles.

@ch APr.II.18 | Where a false argument begins
A false argument rests on the first falsehood in it: if the conclusion is false, one of the premises, or one of the premises behind them, is false.

@taylor 16
@p
A false argument comes about because of the first falsehood in it. Every syllogism is from two premises or from more. If from two, one or both of them must be false, for there was no false syllogism from true premises. If from more, for instance C through A and B, and these through D, E, F and G, one of these higher premises will be false, and the argument will be false because of it; for A and B are concluded through those. So the conclusion and the falsehood follow from one of them.

@ch APr.II.19 | Guarding against being trapped in an argument
In debate the answerer should watch that the same term is not granted twice, since the middle is what is said more than once; the questioner, in turn, should hide where the argument is going.

@taylor 17
@p
To avoid having a syllogism drawn against us, we must watch, when the questioner puts the argument without the conclusions, that the same term is not granted twice in the premises; since we know that there is no syllogism without a middle, and the middle is what is said more than once. How one should watch for the middle with each conclusion is plain from knowing what kind is proved in each figure. And this will not escape us, because we know how we are defending the argument.

@p
@greek ὅπερ φυλάττεσθαι παραγγέλλομεν
What we instruct answerers to guard against, we should ourselves try to hide when we are attacking. This will happen, first, if the conclusions are not drawn in advance but are unclear once the necessary premises have been taken; and further, if one asks not about premises close together but as far as possible about premises with no middle between them. For instance, let A have to be concluded of F, with B, C, D and E as middles. One must ask whether A belongs to B, and next not whether B belongs to C but whether D belongs to E, and after that whether B belongs to C, and so on. And if the syllogism comes about through one middle, one should start from the middle; for in this way it would most escape the answerer.

@ch APr.II.20 | When a refutation is possible
A refutation is a syllogism concluding the contradictory of the answerer's thesis, so it can come about only when there can be a syllogism.

@taylor 18
@p
Since we know when and with the terms related how a syllogism comes about, it is plain also when there will and will not be a refutation. When everything is granted, or the answers are given in turn (for instance, one negative and the other affirmative), a refutation can come about. For there was a syllogism with the terms related in either way; so if what was laid down is contrary to the conclusion, a refutation must come about, since a refutation is a syllogism of the contradictory. But if nothing is granted, a refutation cannot come about, for there was no syllogism when all the terms were negative, and so no refutation either: if there is a refutation, there must be a syllogism, but if there is a syllogism, there need not be a refutation. It is the same if nothing is laid down universally in the answer; for the definition of refutation and of syllogism will be the same.
!flag when there will not be an elenchus | Taylor keeps the Greek word ἔλεγχος as "elenchus"; in this chapter it means simply a refutation of what the answerer has granted, so this version says "refutation".

@ch APr.II.21 | Error in belief, and knowing in more than one way
One can be in error while in a way knowing the truth, for knowledge can be universal, particular, or actually exercised. So a man may know that every mule is barren and still think this mule is pregnant, without holding contrary beliefs.

@taylor 19
@p
It happens sometimes that, just as we are deceived in the arrangement of the terms, so error comes about in belief: for instance, if the same thing can belong primarily to several things, and someone fails to notice one and thinks it belongs to none, while knowing the other. Let A belong to B and to C in themselves, and these likewise to every D. If, then, someone thinks that A belongs to every B, and B to D, but A to no C, and C to every D, he will have knowledge and ignorance of the same thing in the same respect. Again, if someone were deceived about things in the same series: for instance, if A belongs to B, B to C and C to D, but he believed that A belongs to every B and again to no C; for he will at once know that A belongs to D and believe that it does not. Is he then claiming anything else from these premises than not to believe what he knows? For he knows in a way that A belongs to C through B, as the particular is known by the universal; so he claims not to believe at all what he knows in a way; which is impossible.

@p
@greek Ἐπὶ δὲ τοῦ πρότερον λεχθέντος
In the case mentioned first, if the middle is not from the same series, one cannot believe both premises with each of the two middles: for instance, that A belongs to every B and to no C, and both of these to every D. For it turns out that the first premise is taken as contrary, either simply or in part. If he believes that A belongs to everything B belongs to, and knows that B belongs to D, he knows also that A belongs to D. So if again he thinks that A belongs to nothing C belongs to, he thinks that A does not belong to something B belongs to. But to think that A belongs to everything B belongs to and again not to something B belongs to is contrary, either simply or in part.

@p
@greek Οὕτω μὲν οὖν οὐκ ἐνδέχεται ὑπολαβεῖν
In this way, then, one cannot believe it; but nothing prevents believing one premise with each middle, or both with one of them: for instance, that A belongs to every B and B to D, and again that A belongs to no C. For such an error is like the way we are deceived about particulars. For instance, if A belongs to everything B belongs to, and B to every C, A will belong to every C. If, then, someone knows that A belongs to everything B belongs to, he knows also that it belongs to C. But nothing prevents him from not knowing that C exists: for instance, if A is "having angles equal to two right angles", B triangle, and C a perceptible triangle. Someone might believe that C does not exist, while knowing that every triangle has angles equal to two right angles; so he will at once know and not know the same thing. For knowing of every triangle that its angles equal two right angles is not simple: one way is to have universal knowledge, another to have knowledge of the particular. So in the universal way he knows of C that its angles equal two right angles, but in the particular way he does not; so he will not hold contraries. Likewise with the argument in the Meno, that learning is recollection. It never turns out that we know the particular in advance, but that along with the induction we acquire knowledge of the particulars, as if recognising them. Some things we know at once: for instance, that a figure has angles equal to two right angles, if we see that it is a triangle. It is likewise with other things.
!note In Plato's Meno Socrates argues that learning is recollection of what the soul knew before birth. Aristotle accepts the observation but explains it by universal knowledge applied to a case.

@p
@greek Τῇ μὲν οὖν καθόλου θεωροῦμεν
By universal knowledge, then, we observe the particulars, but we do not know them by the knowledge proper to them; so we can be deceived about them, but not in a contrary way: we have the universal knowledge but are deceived in the particular. It is likewise in the cases described before; for the error about the middle is not contrary to the knowledge from the syllogism, nor is the belief about each of the two middles. Nothing prevents someone who knows both that A belongs to the whole of B and that B belongs to C from thinking that A does not belong to C: for instance, knowing that every mule is barren and that this is a mule, and thinking that this one is pregnant. For he does not know that A belongs to C, since he does not consider the two together. So it is clear that if he knows one and not the other, he will be deceived; and this is how universal knowledge stands to particular. For we know nothing perceptible once it has passed out of perception, not even if we happen to have perceived it, except by universal knowledge and by having the proper knowledge, not by exercising it. For knowing is spoken of in three ways: universally, by the proper knowledge, or by exercising it; so being deceived is spoken of in as many ways. Nothing prevents someone from both knowing and being deceived about the same thing, but not in a contrary way. This also happens to someone who knows each premise but has not considered them together beforehand. For in believing that the mule is pregnant he does not have knowledge in the sense of exercising it, nor yet, because of his belief, an error contrary to the knowledge; for the error contrary to universal knowledge is a syllogism.

@p
@greek ὑπολαμβάνων τὸ ἀγαθῷ εἶναι κακῷ
Someone who believes that being good is being bad will believe that the same thing is being good and being bad. Let A stand for being good, B for being bad, and C again for being good. Since he believes that B and C are the same, he will believe that C is B, and again likewise that B is A, so that C is A. For just as, if it were true that B holds of what C holds of, and A of what B holds of, it was true also that A holds of C, so it is with believing. It is likewise with being: if C and B are the same, and again B and A, C was the same as A; so it is likewise with opinion. Is this, then, necessary, if one grants the first point? But perhaps that is false, that anyone believes that being good is being bad, except incidentally; for one can believe this in many ways. This needs to be looked into more fully.

@ch APr.II.22 | Converting terms, and choosing between goods
If the extremes convert, so does the middle. The chapter adds rules for terms that divide everything between them, and for what is more choiceworthy; a lover, for instance, should choose being loved over intercourse.

@taylor 20
@p
When the extremes convert, the middle must convert with both. For if A belongs to C through B, then if they convert and C belongs to everything A belongs to, B too will convert with A and belong to everything A belongs to, through C as the middle; and C will convert with B through A as the middle. It is likewise with not belonging: for instance, if B belongs to C and A does not belong to B, neither will A belong to C. If, then, B converts with A, C too will convert with A. Let B not belong to A; then neither will C, for B belonged to every C. And if C converts with B, A will convert too; for C holds of everything B holds of. And if C converts with A, B will convert too; for C does not belong to what B belongs to, and A does not belong to what C does. This alone begins from the conclusion; the others do not do so likewise as in the affirmative syllogism.

@p
@greek Πάλιν εἰ τὸ καὶ
Again, if A and B convert, and C and D likewise, and A or C must belong to everything, then B and D will be so related that one or the other belongs to everything. For since B belongs to what A belongs to, and D to what C belongs to, and A or C belongs to everything and not both together, it is plain that B or D belongs to everything and not both together; for two syllogisms are combined. Again, if A or B belongs to everything, and C or D, but not together, then if A and C convert, B and D convert too. For if B does not belong to something D belongs to, it is clear that A belongs to it; and if A, then C, for they convert; so C and D belong together, which is impossible. For instance, if the ungenerated is imperishable and the imperishable ungenerated, the generated must be perishable and the perishable generated.

@p
@greek Ὅταν δὲ τὸ Α ὅλῳ τῷ Β
When A belongs to the whole of B and of C and is predicated of nothing else, and B belongs to every C, A and B must convert. For since A is said of B and C alone, and B is predicated both of itself and of C, it is plain that B will be said of everything A is said of, except A itself. Again, when A and B belong to the whole of C, and C converts with B, A must belong to every B; for since A belongs to every C, and C to B because they convert, A too belongs to every B.

@p
@greek Ὅταν δὲ δυοῖν ὄντοιν
When, of two opposed things, A is more choiceworthy than B, and likewise D than C, then if A and C together are more choiceworthy than B and D, A is more choiceworthy than D. For A is to be pursued just as much as B is to be avoided (they are opposed), and C likewise with D (for these too are opposed). If, then, A is as choiceworthy as D, B is as much to be avoided as C; for each is to each likewise, the thing to be avoided to the thing to be pursued. So both A and C together equal B and D together. But since they are more choiceworthy, they cannot be equal; for then B and D would be equal too. If D is more choiceworthy than A, B is less to be avoided than C; for the lesser is opposed to the lesser. But a greater good and a lesser evil are more choiceworthy than a lesser good and a greater evil; so B and D together would be more choiceworthy than A and C. But in fact this is not so. So A is more choiceworthy than D, and C is less to be avoided than B. If, then, every lover, by his love, would choose A, being such as to grant favours, and not granting them, C, rather than granting them, D, and not being such as to grant them, B, it is clear that A, being such, is more choiceworthy than granting the favour. So being loved is more choiceworthy, in love, than intercourse. Love, then, is more for affection than for intercourse; and if it is most for this, this is its end. So intercourse is either not an end at all or is for the sake of being loved. And so it is with the other desires and arts too.

@ch APr.II.23 | Induction
All conviction comes through syllogism or induction. Induction proves the major term of the middle through the particular cases, when the cases taken all together convert with the middle. It is clearer to us, though syllogism is prior by nature.

@taylor 21
@p
How the terms stand with respect to conversions, and to being more choiceworthy or more to be avoided, is plain. We must now say that not only dialectical and demonstrative syllogisms come about through the figures described, but rhetorical ones too, and in general any conviction whatever, by whatever method. For we are convinced of everything either through syllogism or from induction.

@p
@greek Ἐπαγωγὴ μὲν οὖν ἐστι
Induction, then, or the syllogism from induction, is deducing one extreme of the middle through the other extreme: for instance, if B is the middle of A and C, proving through C that A belongs to B; for this is how we make inductions. Let A be long-lived, B lacking bile, and C the particular long-lived animals, such as man, horse and mule. A belongs to the whole of C, for everything C is long-lived. But B, lacking bile, also belongs to every C. If, then, C converts with B, and the middle does not extend further, A must belong to B. For it was proved earlier that if two things belong to the same thing, and the extreme converts with one of them, the other predicate will belong to the one that converts. But one must understand C as made up of all the particulars; for induction is through all of them.
!note The biology is Aristotle's; in his day animals without a gall bladder were thought to be long-lived.

@p
@greek ὁ τοιοῦτος συλλογισμός τῆς πρώτης
Such a syllogism is of the first and immediate premise. Where there is a middle, the syllogism is through the middle; where there is none, through induction. And in a way induction is opposed to syllogism: syllogism proves the extreme of the third term through the middle, induction proves the extreme of the middle through the third term. By nature, then, the syllogism through the middle is prior and better known, but to us the syllogism through induction is clearer.

@ch APr.II.24 | Example
An example argues from one like case to another: it shows that the major term belongs to the middle through a case similar to the one in question. It differs from induction in that it does not use all the cases and connects its result with the extreme.

@taylor 22
@p
An example, the argument from a pattern case, is when the extreme is shown to belong to the middle through something like the third term. One must know both that the middle belongs to the third term and that the first belongs to the like case. For instance, let A be bad, B making war on neighbours, C Athenians against Thebans, and D Thebans against Phocians. If, then, we want to prove that it is bad to make war on the Thebans, we must take it that it is bad to make war on neighbours. Conviction of this comes from like cases, for instance that the war against the Phocians was bad for the Thebans. Since, then, war against neighbours is bad, and war against the Thebans is against neighbours, it is plain that war against the Thebans is bad. That B belongs to C and D is plain (for both are making war on neighbours), and that A belongs to D (for the war against the Phocians did not turn out well for the Thebans); but that A belongs to B will be proved through D. It is the same if conviction of the relation of the middle to the extreme came through several like cases. It is plain, then, that an example is neither as part to whole nor as whole to part, but as part to part, when both fall under the same thing and one is known. It differs from induction in that induction proved from all the individual cases that the extreme belongs to the middle, and did not connect the syllogism with the extreme; while the example connects it, and does not prove from all the cases.
!note "Example" renders παράδειγμα, a model or pattern to follow; in rhetoric it is the argument from a past case to a present one, as Aristotle explains in the Rhetoric.

@ch APr.II.25 | Reduction
Reduction is when the first premise is clear and the second, though not clear, is at least as credible as the conclusion, or needs few middles; either way we come nearer to knowledge.

@taylor 23
@p
Reduction is when it is clear that the first term belongs to the middle, and unclear that the middle belongs to the last, but this is likewise credible or more credible than the conclusion; or again if the middles between the last term and the middle are few; for in every way it turns out that we come nearer to knowledge. For instance, let A be what can be taught, B knowledge, and C justice. That knowledge can be taught is plain; whether virtue is knowledge is unclear. If, then, BC is as credible or more credible than AC, there is a reduction; for we are nearer to knowing, through having taken in the knowledge of AB, which we did not have before. Or again if the middles between B and C are few; for in this way too we are nearer to knowing. For instance, if D were being squared, E rectilinear figure, and F circle: if there were only one middle between E and F, that the circle becomes equal to a rectilinear figure with the help of lunes, one would be near to knowing. But when BC is not more credible than AC, nor the middles few, I do not call it reduction; nor when BC is immediate, for such a thing is knowledge.
!flag Abduction, however, is, when it is evident that the first | Taylor's "abduction" renders ἀπαγωγή, "leading away"; since Peirce, "abduction" names inference to the best explanation, which is something else. This version says "reduction", as Jenkinson does.
!note The squaring of the circle by lunes refers to the work of Hippocrates of Chios, who squared certain lunes in the hope of squaring the circle.

@ch APr.II.26 | Objection
An objection is a premise contrary to a premise. It can be universal or particular, and is brought only through the first and third figures, since only these give opposite conclusions.

@taylor 24
@p
An objection is a premise contrary to a premise. It differs from a premise in that an objection can be particular, while a premise either cannot be at all or not in universal syllogisms. An objection is brought in two ways and through two figures: in two ways, because every objection is universal or particular; through two figures, because objections are brought as opposites of the premise, and opposites are concluded only in the first and third figures. For when someone claims that something belongs to all, we object either that it belongs to none or that it does not belong to some; and of these, "to none" comes from the first figure, "not to some" from the last. For instance, let A be "there is one knowledge", and B contraries. When someone puts forward that there is one knowledge of contraries, we object either that there is not the same knowledge of opposites at all, and contraries are opposites, so that the first figure comes about; or that there is not one knowledge of the knowable and the unknowable, and this is the third figure. For of C, the knowable and the unknowable, it is true that they are contraries, but false that there is one knowledge of them. It is the same with the negative premise. When someone claims that there is not one knowledge of contraries, we say either that there is the same knowledge of all opposites, or of some contraries, such as the healthy and the diseased: "of all" from the first figure, "of some" from the third.

@p
@greek Ἁπλῶς γὰρ ἐν πᾶσι καθόλου
In general, in all cases, someone objecting universally must state the contradictory in relation to the universal of the things put forward: for instance, if someone claims there is not the same knowledge of contraries, he must say that there is one knowledge of all opposites. In this way the first figure must come about, for the universal becomes the middle in relation to the original term. Objecting particularly, one must relate it to that which is universal with respect to what the premise is said of: for instance, that there is not the same knowledge of the knowable and the unknowable, since contraries are universal in relation to these. And the third figure comes about, for the middle is what is taken particularly, such as the knowable and the unknowable. For we try to state objections from the premises from which one can deduce the contrary; and this is why we bring them from these figures only, since only in these are there opposite syllogisms; for through the middle figure there was no affirmative conclusion. Further, an objection through the middle figure would need more argument: for instance, if someone did not grant that A belongs to B because C does not follow B. For this is made clear through other premises; but an objection should not turn aside to other things, but should have its other premise plain at once.

@p
@greek Ἐπισκεπτέον δὲ καὶ περὶ τῶν ἄλλων
We must also look into the other kinds of objection, such as those from the contrary, from likeness, and from opinion, and whether one can take a particular objection from the first figure or a negative one from the middle.

@ch APr.II.27 | Likelihoods, signs and the art of reading character
An enthymeme is a syllogism from likelihoods or signs. A sign can be taken in each of the three figures, but only a sign in the first figure is conclusive. Reading character from the body is possible if body and soul change together and each sign marks one thing.

@taylor 25
@p
An enthymeme is a syllogism from likelihoods or signs. A likelihood and a sign are not the same. A likelihood is a reputable premise: what people know to happen or not happen, to be or not be, for the most part, that is a likelihood, such as that the envious hate, or that those who are loved love in return. A sign is meant to be a demonstrative premise, either necessary or reputable: that whose being or coming about brings it about that the thing is, or has come about before or after, is a sign of the thing's having come about or being. A sign is taken in three ways, as many as the ways the middle is placed in the figures: as in the first, as in the middle, or as in the third. For instance, proving that a woman is pregnant because she has milk is from the first figure, for having milk is the middle: A is being pregnant, B having milk, C woman. That the wise are good, since Pittacus is good, is through the last figure: A is good, B the wise, C Pittacus. It is true to predicate both A and B of C; but people do not state the one, because they know it, while they assume the other. That a woman is pregnant because she is pale is meant to be through the middle figure: since paleness follows pregnant women, and also follows this woman, people think it has been proved that she is pregnant. A is pale, B being pregnant, C woman.
!flag The consentaneous, however, and a sign, are not the same | Taylor's "consentaneous" for εἰκός, "what is likely", is a word now unknown; Jenkinson says "probability", and this version says "likelihood", which keeps it apart from mathematical probability.

@p
@greek Ἐὰν μὲν οὖν ἡ μία λεχθῇ πρότασις
If one premise is stated, there is only a sign; if the other is added too, a syllogism: for instance, that Pittacus is generous, since the ambitious are generous and Pittacus is ambitious; or again that the wise are good, since Pittacus is good, and also wise. In this way, then, syllogisms come about; but the one through the first figure cannot be refuted, if it is true (for it is universal); the one through the last figure can be refuted, even if the conclusion is true, because the syllogism is not universal and not to the point. For if Pittacus is good, it does not follow that the other wise men are good. The one through the middle figure can always be refuted, in every way, for no syllogism ever comes about with the terms so related: it does not follow, if a pregnant woman is pale and this woman is pale, that she is pregnant. So truth will be found in all signs, but they have the differences described.

@p
@greek Ἢ δὴ οὕτω διαιρετέον τὸ σημεῖον
Either, then, one should divide signs in this way, and take the middle one among them as a proof (for people say that a proof is what produces knowledge, and the middle is most of all of this kind); or one should call the arguments from the extremes signs, and the one from the middle a proof; for what is through the first figure is most reputable and most of all true.
!note "Proof" renders τεκμήριον, a conclusive sign, as distinct from σημεῖον, a mere sign. The Rhetoric draws the same line.

@p
@greek Τὸ δὲ φυσιογνωμονεῖν δυνατόν ἐστιν
Reading character from the body is possible if one grants that body and soul change together in all natural affections. Someone who has become a musician has perhaps changed something in his soul, but this is not one of the affections natural to us; anger and desires are, among the natural movements. If, then, this were granted, and that each sign marks one thing, and we could grasp the affection and sign proper to each kind, we shall be able to read character from the body. For if there is an affection belonging properly to some indivisible kind, as courage does to lions, there must be some sign of it too; for it is assumed that body and soul are affected together. Let this be having large extremities, which can belong also to other kinds, though not as wholes. For a sign is proper in this way, that it is proper to the whole kind, and not proper to it alone, as we usually say. So this will be found in another kind as well, and man, or some other animal, will be brave; so it will have the sign, for each sign was of one thing. If this is so, and we can collect such signs in those animals that have only one proper affection, and each has a sign, since it must have one, we shall be able to read character from the body. But if the whole kind has two proper affections, as the lion is brave and generous, how shall we know which of the signs that properly go with it is the sign of which? Perhaps if both belong to some other kind, not as a whole, and in those kinds where each is found, not as a whole, one is present and the other not: for if a man is brave but not generous, and has one of the two signs, it is clear that in the lion too this is the sign of courage.

@p
@greek Ἔστι δὴ τὸ φυσιογνωμονεῖν
To read character from the body is, in the first figure, for the middle to convert with the first extreme but to extend beyond the third and not convert with it. For instance, let A be courage, B large extremities, and C lion. B belongs to everything C belongs to, but to other things too; A belongs to everything B belongs to and to no more, but converts with it. Otherwise one sign will not mark one thing.
