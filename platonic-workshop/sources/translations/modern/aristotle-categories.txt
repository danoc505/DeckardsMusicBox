@treatise Cat | The Categories
The first of Aristotle's works that the Platonists read, after Porphyry's Introduction. It sorts the simple things we say, words not yet joined into sentences, into ten kinds: substance, quantity, quality, relative, where, when, being in a position, having, doing and being affected. It first sets out words used with one meaning or with several, and the two ways something can belong to a subject: being said of it, as "man" is said of Socrates, and being in it, as a colour is in a body. Then it treats the first four kinds at length, above all substance, where the individual man or horse is substance in the first and fullest sense and his species and genus are substance in a second sense. The last chapters, which the commentators called the "Postpredicaments", explain the four kinds of opposite, prior and simultaneous, the six kinds of change, and the senses of "having".

@ch Cat.1 | Homonyms, synonyms, paronyms
Things are homonymous when they share a name but the account of what each is differs; synonymous when they share both the name and the account; paronymous when they take their name from something else with a change of ending.

@p
Things are called homonymous when they have only a name in common, and the account of their being that goes with the name is different. A man and a painted figure, for instance, are both called "animal". They have only the name in common, and the account of being that goes with the name is different: if you were to say what being an animal is for each of them, you would give a separate account for each. Things are called synonymous when both the name is common and the account of being that goes with the name is the same. A man and an ox, for instance, are both "animal". Each of them is called by the common name "animal", and the account of their being is the same: if you were to give the account of each, saying what being an animal is for each of them, you would give the same account. Things are called paronymous when they get their name from something else, differing from it only in the ending. So the grammarian gets his name from grammar, and the brave man from bravery.
!note "Painted figure": the Greek τὸ γεγραμμένον is a figure drawn or painted, and ζῷον, "animal", was also the ordinary Greek word for a picture. That is why a man and a picture can share the name.
!flag the reason of essence according to the name is different | Taylor renders λόγος τῆς οὐσίας as "the reason of essence". The Greek means the account (or definition) of what each thing is; the modern version says "the account of their being". In the rest of the work οὐσία is the category, "substance".

@ch Cat.2 | Things said with and without combination; said of a subject, and in a subject
Some things are said in combination ("man runs"), some without ("man", "runs"). Of the things there are, some are said of a subject but are in none (man); some are in a subject but said of none (this white); some are both (knowledge); and some are neither (this man, this horse).

@p
Of things that are said, some are said in combination, others without combination. Things said in combination are, for instance, "man runs" and "man wins"; things said without combination are "man", "ox", "runs", "wins".

@p
@greek Τῶν ὄντων τὰ μὲν καθ᾿ ὑποκειμένου
Of things there are, some are said of a subject but are not in any subject. "Man", for instance, is said of a subject, the individual man, but is not in any subject. Some are in a subject but are not said of any subject. (By "in a subject" I mean what is in something, not as a part of it, and cannot exist apart from what it is in.) The individual piece of grammatical knowledge, for instance, is in a subject, the soul, but is not said of any subject; and the individual white is in a subject, the body (for every colour is in a body), but is not said of any subject. Some are both said of a subject and in a subject. Knowledge, for instance, is in a subject, the soul, and is said of a subject, grammar. And some are neither in a subject nor said of a subject, such as the individual man and the individual horse: nothing of this kind is either in a subject or said of a subject. To put it simply, things that are individual and one in number are not said of any subject, but nothing prevents some of them from being in a subject: the individual piece of grammatical knowledge is one of the things in a subject, and it is not said of any subject.
!note The four classes are the ground of the whole work: what is said of a subject but in none is a secondary substance (a species or genus of substance); what is neither is a primary substance (chapter 5); what is in a subject is one of the other categories, as an accident.
!flag Thus science is in the soul as in a subject | Taylor's "science" is ἐπιστήμη, knowledge of any kind; here the example is grammar, which is a kind of knowledge, not a science in today's sense.

@ch Cat.3 | What is said of the predicate is said of the subject; differences of genera
Whatever is said of a predicate is also said of its subject. Genera that differ and are not ranged one under the other have differences of different kinds; genera ranged one under the other can share the same differences.

@p
When one thing is predicated of another as of a subject, everything that is said of what is predicated will also be said of the subject. For instance, "man" is predicated of the individual man, and "animal" of "man"; so "animal" will be predicated of the individual man too, since the individual man is both a man and an animal.

@p
@greek Τῶν ἑτερογενῶν καὶ μὴ ὑπ᾿ ἄλληλα
The differences of genera that are different and not ranged one under the other are themselves different in kind, as with animal and knowledge. The differences of animal are footed, two-footed, winged and living in water; but none of these is a difference of knowledge, for one kind of knowledge does not differ from another by being two-footed. But nothing prevents genera ranged one under the other from having the same differences, for the higher genera are predicated of those below them. So all the differences of the predicate will be differences of the subject too.
!flag differences arc also different in species | Taylor's "in species" renders τῷ εἴδει, which here means only "in kind": Aristotle is not yet using εἶδος in its technical sense of a species under a genus.

@ch Cat.4 | The ten categories
Each thing said without combination signifies substance, quantity, quality, a relative, where, when, being in a position, having, doing or being affected. None of these is true or false by itself; truth and falsity come with combination.
@taylor 3

@p
Of things said without any combination, each signifies either substance, or quantity, or quality, or a relative, or where, or when, or being in a position, or having, or doing, or being affected. To give a rough idea: substance is, for instance, man, horse; quantity, two feet long, three feet long; quality, white, grammatical; a relative, double, half, larger; where, in the Lyceum, in the market place; when, yesterday, last year; being in a position, is lying, is sitting; having, has shoes on, has armour on; doing, cutting, burning; being affected, being cut, being burned. None of these is said, by itself, in any affirmation or denial; an affirmation or denial comes about when they are combined with one another. For every affirmation and denial is thought to be either true or false, but of things said without any combination none is either true or false, such as "man", "white", "runs", "wins".
!note The Lyceum was the gymnasium outside Athens where Aristotle taught; the example is his own place.

@ch Cat.5 | Substance
Primary substance is the individual, neither said of a subject nor in one; its species and genera are secondary substances. Without the primary substances nothing else could exist. Substance is not in a subject, is said synonymously, signifies a "this", has no contrary, does not admit of more and less, and, above all, can receive contraries while staying one and the same.

@p
Substance, in the most proper, primary and fullest sense, is what is neither said of any subject nor in any subject: for instance, the individual man or the individual horse. Secondary substances are the species in which the things called substances in the primary sense are present, and also the genera of these species. The individual man, for instance, belongs in a species, man, and the genus of the species is animal. So these are called secondary substances: man, for instance, and animal. It is clear from what has been said that when something is said of a subject, both its name and its account must be predicated of the subject. "Man", for instance, is said of the individual man as a subject, and the name is predicated of him, since you will predicate "man" of the individual man; and the account of man will be predicated of him too, since the individual man is both a man and an animal. So both the name and the account will be predicated of the subject. But with things that are in a subject, in most cases neither the name nor the account is predicated of the subject. In some cases nothing prevents the name from being predicated of the subject sometimes, but the account cannot be. White, for instance, which is in a body as a subject, is predicated of the subject (the body is called white), but the account of white will never be predicated of the body. All the other things are either said of the primary substances as subjects, or are in them as subjects. This is clear from particular cases. "Animal", for instance, is predicated of man, and so it will be predicated of the individual man too; for if it were predicated of none of the individual men, it would not be predicated of man at all. Again, colour is in body, and so in an individual body too; for if it were not in some individual body, it would not be in body at all. So all the other things are either said of the primary substances as subjects or are in them as subjects. If, then, the primary substances did not exist, it would be impossible for any of the other things to exist.
!flag nor is any subject | The Greek says "nor is in any subject" (μήτ᾿ ἐν ὑποκειμένῳ τινί ἐστιν); Taylor's English, as the scan reads it, has lost "in", and so says the opposite of what Aristotle means.
!note The Platonists read this chapter with care: here the individual is called substance "most of all", and the commentators (Porphyry, Simplicius) explain that Aristotle speaks of substance as it is known by the senses and named by us, not of the intelligible substances that are prior by nature.

@p
@greek Τῶν δὲ δευτέρων οὐσιῶν μᾶλλον οὐσία
Of the secondary substances, the species is more truly substance than the genus, since it is nearer to the primary substance. If you are to say what the primary substance is, you will give a clearer and more fitting answer by giving the species than the genus. In saying what the individual man is, for instance, you will be clearer if you say "man" than if you say "animal", since "man" is more proper to the individual man, and "animal" more general. In the same way, in saying what the individual tree is, you will be clearer if you say "tree" than if you say "plant". Again, the primary substances are called substances most of all because they underlie all the other things, and all the other things are predicated of them or are in them. And as the primary substances stand to all the other things, so the species stands to the genus: the species underlies the genus, since the genera are predicated of the species, but the species are not predicated of the genera in return. So for this reason too the species is more truly substance than the genus. But of the species themselves, those that are not genera, one is no more substance than another: you will give no more fitting an answer for the individual man by saying "man" than for the individual horse by saying "horse". In the same way, of the primary substances, one is no more substance than another: the individual man is no more substance than the individual ox.

@p
@greek Εἰκότως δὲ μετὰ τὰς πρώτας οὐσίας
It is reasonable that, after the primary substances, the species and genera alone of all other things should be called secondary substances, since they alone, of all that is predicated, reveal the primary substance. If you are to say what the individual man is, you will answer fittingly by giving the species or the genus, and you will make your answer clearer by saying "man" rather than "animal". But anything else you might give would be off the point: if you said "white" or "runs" or anything of that kind. So it is reasonable that these alone, of all other things, should be called substances. Again, the primary substances are called substances most properly because they underlie all the other things. And as the primary substances stand to everything else, so the species and genera of the primary substances stand to all the rest, since all the rest are predicated of them. You will call the individual man grammatical, and so you will call both man and animal grammatical too. And so with the other cases.

@p
@greek Κοινὸν δὲ κατὰ πάσης οὐσίας
It is common to every substance not to be in a subject. The primary substance is neither in a subject nor said of a subject. As for the secondary substances, it is clear in the following way too that they are not in a subject. "Man" is said of the individual man as a subject, but is not in a subject: man is not in the individual man. In the same way, "animal" is said of the individual man as a subject, but animal is not in the individual man. Further, with things in a subject, nothing prevents the name from being predicated of the subject sometimes, but the account cannot be. But with the secondary substances, both the account and the name are predicated of the subject: you will predicate the account of man of the individual man, and the account of animal likewise. So substance cannot be one of the things that are in a subject. This is not peculiar to substance, however: the difference too is not in a subject. "Footed" and "two-footed" are said of man as a subject, but are not in a subject, since footed and two-footed are not in man. The account of the difference, too, is predicated of whatever the difference is said of. If "footed", for instance, is said of man, the account of footed will also be predicated of man, since man is footed. We should not be troubled by the parts of substances, as though they were in the wholes as in subjects, and fear that we shall be forced to say that they are not substances. For by "things in a subject" we did not mean things that belong in something as its parts.

@p
@greek Ὑπάρχει δὲ ταῖς οὐσίαις καὶ ταῖς διαφοραῖς
It belongs to substances and to differences that everything called from them is called so synonymously. All the predicates taken from them are predicated either of the individuals or of the species. From the primary substance there is no predicate, since it is said of no subject. Of the secondary substances, the species is predicated of the individual, and the genus both of the species and of the individual. In the same way the differences are predicated both of the species and of the individuals. And the primary substances take the account of their species and of their genera, and the species takes the account of its genus, since whatever is said of the predicate will also be said of the subject. In the same way the species and the individuals take the account of the differences. But synonymous things, as we said, are those with both the name in common and the same account. So everything called from substances and from differences is called so synonymously.

@p
@greek Πᾶσα δὲ οὐσία δοκεῖ τόδε τι σημαίνειν
Every substance seems to signify a "this something". With the primary substances it is beyond dispute and true that each signifies a "this something", for what it reveals is individual and one in number. With the secondary substances it looks, from the form of the name, as if they too, like the primary ones, signify a "this something", when one says "man" or "animal". But this is not true; rather, they signify a certain kind of thing, since the subject is not one, as the primary substance is: "man" and "animal" are said of many. Yet they do not signify simply a quality, as "white" does. "White" signifies nothing but a quality, whereas the species and the genus mark off a quality with respect to substance: they signify substance of a certain kind. The genus marks off more widely than the species: whoever says "animal" takes in more than whoever says "man".
!note "This something" (τόδε τι) is Aristotle's phrase for an individual that can be pointed at.

@p
@greek Ὑπάρχει δὲ ταῖς οὐσίαις καὶ τὸ μηδὲν αὐταῖς ἐναντίον
It also belongs to substances that nothing is contrary to them. What could be contrary to a primary substance, such as the individual man or the individual animal? There is nothing. Nor is anything contrary to man or to animal. This is not peculiar to substance, however, but holds of many other things too, for instance of quantity. There is no contrary to two feet long or three feet long, or to ten, or to anything of that kind, unless someone were to say that many is contrary to few, or large to small. But of definite quantities none is contrary to any other.

@p
@greek Δοκεῖ δὲ ἡ οὐσία μὴ ἐπιδέχεσθαι
Substance seems not to admit of more and less. I do not mean that one substance is not more a substance than another, for we have said that it is. I mean that each substance is not said to be more or less that very thing which it is. If this substance is a man, for instance, it will not be more or less a man, either than itself or than another man. One man is not more a man than another, as one white thing is more or less white than another, and one beautiful thing is said to be more or less beautiful than another. And a thing is said to be more or less so than itself: the body, being white, is said to be whiter now than before, and being hot, to be more or less hot. But substance is not said to be more or less: a man is not said to be more a man now than before, nor is any of the other things that are substances. So substance does not admit of more and less.

@p
@greek Μάλιστα δὲ ἴδιον τῆς οὐσίας δοκεῖ εἶναι
Most distinctive of substance seems to be that what is one and the same in number can receive contraries. Among the other things, those that are not substances, you could not bring forward anything that, being one in number, can receive contraries. A colour that is one and the same in number will not be both black and white, and an action that is one and the same in number will not be both bad and good; and so with the other things that are not substances. But a substance, being one and the same in number, can receive contraries. The individual man, for instance, being one and the same, becomes now white, now black, hot and cold, bad and good. Nothing like this is seen in any other case, unless someone were to object that a statement and a belief can receive contraries. The same statement seems to be both true and false: if the statement that someone is sitting is true, the same statement will be false when he has stood up. The same holds of belief: if someone truly believes that a man is sitting, he will believe falsely when the man has stood up, if he holds the same belief about him. But even if one were to accept this, there is still a difference in the way it happens. The things in the case of substances can receive contraries by changing themselves: what has become cold from hot has changed (it has been altered), and so has what has become black from white, and good from bad. In the same way with the others: each of them can receive contraries by undergoing change itself. But the statement and the belief themselves remain entirely unchanged in every way; it is because the thing changes that the contrary comes to be about them. The statement that someone is sitting stays the same, but when the thing has changed it is now true, now false. So too with belief. So it would be distinctive of substance, at least in the way it happens, that it can receive contraries by a change in itself. But if someone were to accept this too, that a statement and a belief can receive contraries, it is not true. A statement and a belief are said to be able to receive contraries not because they themselves receive something, but because something has happened to something else. It is because the thing is or is not so that the statement is said to be true or false, not because the statement itself can receive contraries. Simply, nothing at all changes either the statement or the belief. So they cannot receive contraries, since nothing happens in them. But a substance is said to be able to receive contraries because it receives the contraries itself: it receives sickness and health, whiteness and blackness, and because it receives each of these itself, it is said to be able to receive contraries. So it would be distinctive of substance that what is one and the same in number can receive contraries by a change in itself. So much for substance.
!note First1KGreek's text here reads "οὐκ ἀριθμῷ οὐκ ἔσται", where Bekker's and the other editions have "καὶ ἡ πρᾶξις ἡ μία τῷ ἀριθμῷ οὐκ ἔσται": the words for "and an action that is one in number" have dropped out. The modern version follows the editions.

@ch Cat.6 | Quantity
Quantity is either discrete (number, speech) or continuous (line, surface, body, time, place), and made either of parts with a position towards each other or of parts without one. Only these are quantities strictly; others are quantities by accident. Quantity has no contrary (large and small are relatives), does not admit of more and less, and is most of all distinctive in being called equal and unequal.

@p
Of quantities, some are discrete and others continuous; and some are made up of parts that have a position in relation to one another, others of parts that have none. Discrete quantities are, for instance, number and speech; continuous ones are line, surface, body, and besides these, time and place. The parts of a number have no common boundary at which they join. If five, for instance, is a part of ten, the two fives do not join at any common boundary but are separate; nor do three and seven join at any common boundary. In general, you could never find a common boundary between the parts of a number: they are always separate. So number is one of the discrete quantities. Speech, too, is one of the discrete quantities. That speech is a quantity is plain, since it is measured in long and short syllables; I mean speech that is spoken aloud. Its parts do not join at any common boundary: there is no common boundary at which the syllables join, but each is separate, on its own. A line, on the other hand, is continuous: you can find a common boundary at which its parts join, a point; and for a surface, a line, since the parts of a plane join at a common boundary. In the same way with a body you could find a common boundary, a line or a surface, at which the parts of the body join. Time and place are also of this kind. The present time joins both the past and the future. Place, again, is one of the continuous quantities. The parts of a body occupy a certain place, and they join at a common boundary; so the parts of the place, which each of the parts of the body occupies, also join at the same boundary as the parts of the body. So place too would be continuous, since its parts join at one common boundary.
!flag Discrete quantity is, as number and sentence | Owen's "sentence" renders λόγος, which here means spoken speech measured in long and short syllables, as Aristotle says a few lines on; not a sentence, nor "reason" or "account", as λόγος is elsewhere in the work.

@p
@greek Ἔτι δὲ τὰ μὲν ἐκ θέσιν ἐχόντων
Further, some quantities are made up of parts that have a position in relation to one another, others of parts that have none. The parts of a line, for instance, have a position in relation to one another: each lies somewhere, and you could pick out and say where each lies in the plane, and which of the other parts it joins. In the same way the parts of a plane have a certain position: you could say likewise where each lies, and which join one another. So too with the parts of a solid and the parts of a place. But with number you could not show that its parts have any position in relation to one another, or lie anywhere, or which parts join one another. Nor could you with the parts of time, since none of the parts of time lasts, and how could what does not last have any position? You would rather say that they have a certain order, in that one part of time is earlier and another later. The same holds of number: one is counted before two, and two before three, and in this way number would have a certain order, but you would hardly find a position. Speech, too, is the same: none of its parts lasts; once a part has been said, it can no longer be caught. So its parts could have no position, since none of them lasts. Some quantities, then, are made up of parts with a position, others of parts without one.

@p
@greek Κυρίως δὲ ποσὰ ταῦτα μόνα λέγεται
Only the things we have named are called quantities strictly; all the others are called so by accident, since it is with an eye to these that we call the others quantities too. White is called large, for instance, because the surface is large; an action long, because the time is long; and a movement, too, is called long. None of these is called a quantity in its own right. If you are to say how long an action is, you will define it by the time, saying that it lasts a year or something of that sort. And in saying how much white there is, you will define it by the surface: you would say the white is as large as the surface is. So only the things we have named are called quantities strictly and in their own right; none of the others is so in its own right, but, if at all, by accident.

@p
@greek Ἔτι τῷ ποσῷ οὐδέν ἐστιν ἐναντίον
Further, nothing is contrary to a quantity. With definite quantities it is clear that nothing is contrary, as to two feet long or three feet long, or to a surface, or to anything of that kind: nothing is contrary to them. Unless, perhaps, one were to say that many is contrary to few, or large to small. But none of these is a quantity; they are relatives. Nothing is called large or small in its own right, but by being referred to something else. A mountain, for instance, is called small and a grain of millet large, because the grain is larger than others of its kind and the mountain smaller than others of its kind. So the reference is to something else, since if a thing were called small or large in its own right, the mountain would never be called small or the grain large. Again, we say that there are many people in a village but few in Athens, though there are many times more of them there; and many in a house but few in the theatre, though there are far more. Further, "two feet long", "three feet long" and each thing of that kind signify a quantity, but "large" and "small" do not signify a quantity; rather, a relative, since large and small are looked at in relation to something else. So it is clear that these are relatives. Further, whether one counts them as quantities or not, they have no contrary. For how could anything be contrary to what cannot be taken by itself but only in reference to something else? Further, if large and small are to be contraries, it will follow that the same thing admits contraries at the same time, and that things are contrary to themselves. For it sometimes happens that the same thing is at once large and small: it is small in relation to this, and the very same thing is large in relation to that. So it follows that the same thing is both large and small at the same time, and so admits contraries at the same time. But nothing is thought to admit contraries at the same time. Take substance: it is thought able to receive contraries, but nobody is ill and well at the same time, and nothing is white and black at the same time; nor is there anything else that admits contraries at the same time. And it follows that things would be contrary to themselves. For if large is contrary to small, and the same thing is at once large and small, it would be contrary to itself. But it is impossible for a thing to be contrary to itself. So large is not contrary to small, nor many to few. So even if someone says that these are not relatives but quantities, they will have no contrary.

@p
@greek Μάλιστα δὲ ἡ ἐναντιότης τοῦ ποσοῦ
It is above all in the case of place that there seems to be contrariety of quantity. People take up to be contrary to down, calling the region towards the centre "down", because the centre is at the greatest distance from the limits of the world. And it seems that they draw their definition of the other contraries from these too: they define as contraries the things in the same genus that are furthest apart from each other.
!note The "centre" is the centre of the world, which in Greek astronomy was the earth's centre; "up" is the outermost heaven.

@p
@greek Οὐ δοκεῖ δὲ τὸ ποσὸν ἐπιδέχεσθαι
Quantity does not seem to admit of more and less: two feet long, for instance; one thing is not more two feet long than another. Nor with number: three is no more three than five is five, nor five more five than three is three; nor is one time said to be more a time than another. Nor, in general, is more and less said of any of the things we have named. So quantity does not admit of more and less.
!flag nor is one set of three more truly three than another set | The Greek shown, Bekker's, says "nor five more five than three is three" (οὐδὲ τὰ πέντε τῶν τριῶν); Edghill's clause renders another text of the Greek, not this one.

@p
@greek Ἴδιον δὲ μάλιστα τοῦ ποσοῦ
Most distinctive of quantity is that it is called equal and unequal. Each of the quantities we have named is called equal and unequal: a body is called equal and unequal, and a time equal and unequal, and so with each of the others. But of the rest, those that are not quantities, it would hardly seem that they are called equal and unequal. A condition, for instance, is hardly called equal and unequal, but rather like; and white is hardly called equal and unequal, but like. So it would be most distinctive of quantity that it is called equal and unequal.

@ch Cat.7 | Relatives
Relatives are things said to be what they are of something else, or in some other way in relation to another. Some have contraries, some admit of more and less; all have correlatives, if they are given properly; most are simultaneous by nature, but the knowable seems prior to knowledge and the perceptible to perception. Is any substance a relative? Not on a stricter definition: whoever knows a relative definitely knows definitely what it is relative to, and this is not so with a head or a hand.

@p
We call relatives all such things as are said to be just what they are of something else, or in some other way in relation to something else. The larger, for instance, is said to be what it is of something else, since it is called larger than something; and the double is said to be what it is of something else, since it is called double of something. So with all other such things. These too are relatives: state, condition, perception, knowledge, position. Each of these is said to be just what it is of something else, and not something else: a state is called a state of something, knowledge knowledge of something, a position the position of something, and so with the others. Relatives, then, are things that are said to be just what they are of something else, or in some other way in relation to something else. A mountain, for instance, is called large in relation to something else, since the mountain is called large relatively to something; and the like is called like something, and the other things of this kind are likewise said in relation to something. Lying down, standing and sitting are particular positions, and position is a relative. But to lie, to stand or to sit are not themselves positions; they are named paronymously from the positions we have mentioned.
!flag being either said to be of something else or related to something else | Edghill's "of something else" is literal. The Greek genitive can be "of", "than" or "relative to", as in "larger than something" and "double of something"; the modern version uses whichever English the example needs.

@p
@greek Ὑπάρχει δὲ καὶ ἐναντιότης ἐν τοῖς πρός τι
There is contrariety among relatives too: virtue, for instance, is contrary to vice, each of them being a relative, and knowledge to ignorance. But not every relative has a contrary: nothing is contrary to double or to triple, or to anything of that kind.
!note Virtue and vice are counted as relatives here because each is a state, and states were named as relatives above. In chapter 8 Aristotle counts them under quality too, and he takes up the overlap at the end of that chapter.

@p
@greek Δοκεῖ δὲ καὶ τὸ μᾶλλον καὶ τὸ ἧττον
Relatives also seem to admit of more and less. Like and unlike are called so more and less, and equal and unequal are called so more and less, each of them being a relative, since the like is called like something, and the unlike unlike something. But not all relatives admit of more and less: double is not called more and less double, nor is anything of that kind.

@p
@greek Πάντα δὲ τὰ πρός τι πρὸς ἀντιστρέφοντα
All relatives are spoken of in relation to correlatives that reciprocate. The slave is called the slave of a master, and the master the master of a slave; the double, double of a half, and the half, half of a double; the larger, larger than a smaller, and the smaller, smaller than a larger. So with the other cases too, except that sometimes the wording will differ in its ending: knowledge is called knowledge of the knowable, and the knowable knowable by knowledge; perception, perception of the perceptible, and the perceptible perceptible by perception. Sometimes, however, they will not seem to reciprocate, if the thing a relative is related to is not given properly, but the one who gives it makes a mistake. If a wing, for instance, is given as the wing of a bird, bird does not reciprocate as the bird of a wing. The first was not given properly, "wing of a bird", since it is not as a bird that a bird has a wing, but as a winged thing: many other things have wings that are not birds. So if it is given properly, it reciprocates too: the wing is the wing of a winged thing, and the winged thing is winged by a wing. Sometimes it may even be necessary to coin a name, if no name exists for what the thing would properly be given in relation to. If a rudder, for instance, is given as the rudder of a boat, the giving is not proper, since it is not as a boat that a boat has a rudder: there are boats that have no rudders. So they do not reciprocate, since the boat is not called the boat of a rudder. The giving would perhaps be more proper if it were made in some such way as "the rudder is the rudder of a ruddered thing", or in some other way, since there is no name for it. And if it is given properly, they do reciprocate: the ruddered thing is ruddered by a rudder. So with the other cases: the head would be given more properly as the head of a headed thing than as the head of an animal, since it is not as an animal that an animal has a head: many animals have no head. This is perhaps the easiest way to find names for things that have none: to take names from the first of the pair and give them to the correlatives that reciprocate with them, as in the cases above "winged" was taken from "wing" and "ruddered" from "rudder". All relatives, then, if they are given properly, are spoken of in relation to correlatives that reciprocate; but if they are given in relation to some chance thing, and not to the very thing they are spoken of in relation to, they do not reciprocate. I mean that even among things that are agreed to be spoken of in relation to correlatives that reciprocate, and that have names, none reciprocates if it is given in relation to something accidental and not to the very thing it is spoken of in relation to. If the slave, for instance, is given as the slave not of a master but of a man, or of a two-footed thing, or of anything of that kind, they do not reciprocate, since the giving is not proper. Further, if something has been given properly in relation to what it is spoken of in relation to, then, when everything else that is accidental is stripped away and only that is left in relation to which it was properly given, it will always be spoken of in relation to that. If the slave, for instance, is spoken of in relation to a master, then, when everything accidental to the master is stripped away (being two-footed, being able to receive knowledge, being a man) and only his being a master is left, the slave will always be spoken of in relation to that, since the slave is called the slave of a master. But if it is not given properly in relation to what it is spoken of in relation to, then, when everything else is stripped away and only what it was given in relation to is left, it will not be spoken of in relation to that. Let the slave be given as the slave of a man, and the wing as the wing of a bird, and let his being a master be stripped away from the man: the slave will no longer be spoken of in relation to a man, since if there is no master there is no slave either. In the same way, let being winged be stripped away from the bird: the wing will no longer be a relative, since if there is no winged thing the wing will not be the wing of anything. So one must give each relative in relation to what it is properly spoken of in relation to. If there is a name for it, the giving is easy; if there is none, it may be necessary to coin one. Given in this way, it is clear that all relatives are spoken of in relation to correlatives that reciprocate.

@p
@greek Δοκεῖ δὲ τὰ πρός τι ἅμα τῇ φύσει εἶναι
Relatives seem to be simultaneous by nature, and in most cases this is true. A double and a half exist together: when there is a half there is a double, and when there is a master there is a slave, and when there is a slave there is a master; and likewise with the others. They also do away with each other: if there is no double there is no half, and if there is no half there is no double; and so with other such things. But it does not seem to be true of all relatives that they are simultaneous by nature. The knowable would seem to be prior to knowledge, since for the most part we acquire knowledge of things that already exist: in few cases, if in any, would one find knowledge coming into being together with what is knowable. Further, doing away with the knowable does away with knowledge, but doing away with knowledge does not do away with the knowable. If there is no knowable there is no knowledge, since there will be nothing for knowledge to be of; but if there is no knowledge, nothing prevents there being something knowable. The squaring of the circle, for instance, if it is knowable, is not yet known, but it is itself knowable. Further, if animal is done away with there will be no knowledge, but many of the knowable things may still exist. The case of perception is likewise. The perceptible seems to be prior to perception. Doing away with the perceptible does away with perception, but doing away with perception does not do away with the perceptible. For perceptions are about body and in body, and if the perceptible is done away with, body is done away with too (body is one of the perceptibles); and if there is no body, perception is done away with too. So the perceptible does away with perception along with itself. But perception does not do away with the perceptible: if animal is done away with, perception is done away with, but the perceptible will remain, such as body, hot, sweet, bitter and all the other perceptibles. Further, perception comes into being together with what perceives, since perception comes into being together with the animal; but the perceptible exists even before there is an animal or perception, since fire and water and such things, of which the animal itself is made up, exist before there is any animal at all or any perception. So the perceptible would seem to be prior to perception.
!note "Simultaneous by nature" is taken up again in chapter 13. The squaring of the circle was an open problem in Aristotle's day; it was proved impossible with ruler and compass only in 1882.

@p
@greek Ἔχει δὲ ἀπορίαν πότερον οὐδεμία οὐσία
It is a puzzle whether, as is thought, no substance is spoken of as a relative, or whether this is possible with some secondary substances. With primary substances it is true: neither the wholes nor the parts are spoken of as relatives. The individual man is not called the individual man of something, nor the individual ox the individual ox of something. So too with the parts: the individual hand is not called the individual hand of something, but someone's hand; and the individual head is not called the individual head of something, but someone's head. So too with secondary substances, at least most of them: man is not called the man of something, nor ox the ox of something, nor log the log of something; it is called someone's property. With such things, then, it is clear that they are not relatives. But with some secondary substances there is room for dispute: a head is called someone's head, and a hand someone's hand, and so with each such thing, so these would seem to be relatives. If the definition of relatives given above was adequate, it is very difficult, or impossible, to show that no substance is spoken of as a relative. But if it was not adequate, and relatives are those things for which being is the same as being somehow related to something, then perhaps something may be said in answer. The earlier definition does apply to all relatives, but being said to be just what they are of other things is not the same as their being relatives. From this it is clear that if someone knows any relative definitely, he will also know definitely what it is relative to. This is plain from the definition itself. If someone knows that a this is a relative, and being, for relatives, is the same as being somehow related to something, he knows that to which it is somehow related; for if he does not know at all that to which it is somehow related, he will not know whether it is somehow related to something. It is clear in particular cases too. If someone knows definitely that this is double, he also knows at once, definitely, what it is double of; for if he does not know it to be double of anything definite, he does not know whether it is double at all. In the same way, if someone knows that this is more beautiful, he must, for these reasons, know at once, definitely, what it is more beautiful than. He will not know indefinitely that this is more beautiful than something worse; that would be a supposition, not knowledge, since he will no longer know exactly that it is more beautiful than something worse: it might so happen that there is nothing worse than it. So it is clear that whoever knows any relative definitely must also know definitely what it is relative to. But a head, a hand and each such thing, which are substances, can be known definitely for what they are, without necessarily knowing what they are relative to: you cannot know definitely whose this head is, or whose this hand. So these would not be relatives. And if these are not relatives, it would be true to say that no substance is a relative. It is perhaps hard to make firm statements on such questions without having looked into them many times; but to have gone through the puzzles about each of them is not without use.
!note The second definition, "those things for which being is the same as being somehow related to something", is discussed at length by the Greek commentators, Simplicius among them, with the question whether the parts of a substance are relatives.

@ch Cat.8 | Quality
Quality is what things are called "such" by. It has four kinds: states and conditions (knowledge, virtue; heat, sickness); natural capacities and incapacities (a good boxer, a healthy constitution); affective qualities and affections (sweetness, colour, a temper); and shape and figure. Things named from qualities are mostly named paronymously. Qualities can have contraries and admit of more and less, but not all do; what is distinctive of quality is that things are called like and unlike by it. A state can be both a relative and a quality.

@p
By a quality I mean that in virtue of which things are said to be such and such. Quality is one of the things spoken of in several ways. One kind of quality, then, let us call states and conditions. A state differs from a condition in being far more lasting and stable. Of this kind are the branches of knowledge and the virtues. Knowledge seems to be one of the things that stay and are hard to move, even if one has acquired it only to a modest degree, unless some great change comes about through illness or something else of that kind. In the same way virtue, such as justice and self-control and each of the others, seems not easy to move or easy to change. We call conditions the things that are easily moved and change quickly, such as heat and cold, sickness and health, and other such things. A man is in a certain condition with respect to these, but he changes quickly, becoming cold after being hot, and passing from health to sickness; and so with the others, unless one of these has in fact, through length of time, become part of a man's nature and is incurable, or very hard to move, and then one might call it a state. It is clear that people mean by states those that are more lasting and harder to move. They say that those who do not have a firm hold on their branches of knowledge, but are easily moved, do not have a state, although they are in some condition with respect to that knowledge, either worse or better. So a state differs from a condition in that a condition is easily moved, whereas a state is more lasting and harder to move. States are also conditions, but conditions are not necessarily states: those who have states are also in some condition in respect of them, but those who are in a condition do not always have a state as well.
!flag But quality is among the number of things which are multifariously multifiiri | The scan of Taylor's page doubles a word ("multifariously multifiiri"); the Greek says only that quality is "spoken of in several ways".
!note Edghill renders ἕξις "habit" and διάθεσις "disposition", as Taylor does. "State" and "condition" are the modern renderings; ἕξις is the word Aristotle uses for a virtue in the Ethics.

@p
@greek Ἕτερον δὲ γένος ποιότητος καθ᾿ ὃ πυκτικοὺς
Another kind of quality is that in virtue of which we call people good at boxing or good at running, or healthy or sickly: in short, whatever is said by way of a natural capacity or incapacity. Each such thing is called a quality not because it is in some condition, but because it has a natural capacity or incapacity to do something easily or to be unaffected. People are called boxers or runners not because they are in some condition, but because they have a natural capacity to do something easily; they are called healthy because they have a natural capacity to be unaffected easily by whatever happens to them, and sickly because they have a natural incapacity to be unaffected easily by whatever happens to them. Hard and soft are likewise: the hard is so called because it has the capacity not to be divided easily, the soft because it has an incapacity for that same thing.

@p
@greek Τρίτον δὲ γένος ποιότητος παθητικαὶ ποιότητες
A third kind of quality is affective qualities and affections. Examples are sweetness, bitterness, sourness and everything akin to these, and also heat and coldness, whiteness and blackness. That these are qualities is plain, since the things that have received them are called such and such in virtue of them: honey is called sweet because it has received sweetness, and the body white because it has received whiteness; and so with the others. They are called affective qualities not because the things that have received them have been affected in some way: honey is not called sweet because it has been affected in some way, nor is anything else of this kind. Likewise heat and coldness are called affective qualities not because the things that have received them have been affected in some way; it is because each of the qualities we have named produces an affection in the senses that they are called affective qualities. Sweetness produces an affection in taste, and heat in touch; and likewise with the others. But whiteness and blackness and the other colours are not called affective qualities in the same way as those we have named, but because they themselves come about from an affection. That many changes of colour come about through an affection is clear: when someone is ashamed he goes red, and when he is afraid he goes pale, and so on. So if someone has by nature undergone some affection of this kind, through some natural combination of circumstances, it is likely that he will have the like colour. For the state of the body that came about just now in being ashamed could also come about through one's natural make-up, so that the like colour is produced by nature too. Whenever such things have their origin in certain affections that are hard to move and lasting, they are called affective qualities. Whether pallor or darkness has come about in one's natural make-up, they are called qualities (since we are called such and such in virtue of them); or whether this same pallor or darkness has resulted from a long illness or from sunburn, and does not easily go away, or even lasts for life, these too are called qualities, since we are likewise called such and such in virtue of them. But those that come about from things that are easily dissolved and quickly go away are called affections, not qualities, since people are not called such and such in virtue of them. The man who goes red because he is ashamed is not called a red-faced man, nor is the man who goes pale through fear called a pale man; rather, he is said to have been affected in some way. So such things are called affections, not qualities. Likewise there are affective qualities and affections in the soul too. Things that come about at birth from certain affections that are hard to move are called qualities: madness, for instance, and anger, and such things, since people are called such and such in virtue of them, irascible and mad. So too with any derangements that are not natural but have come about from some other circumstances and are hard to get rid of, or even altogether unchangeable: such things too are qualities, since people are called such and such in virtue of them. But those that come about from things that quickly go away are called affections: if, for instance, someone in distress is rather irritable. A man who is rather irritable in such an affection is not called irascible, but rather said to have been affected in some way. So such things are called affections, not qualities.
!note "Affective" (παθητικός) here means "producing an affection" in the senses, or "coming from an affection", as Aristotle explains; it is not "passive".
!flag Similarly to these also passive qualities and passions are denominated in the soul | Taylor's "passive qualities" and "passions" are literal; Aristotle explains that sweetness is called παθητική because it produces an affection in the senses, not because it is passive, and the modern version says "affective".

@p
@greek Τέταρτον δὲ γένος ποιότητος σχῆμά τε
A fourth kind of quality is figure and the shape that belongs to each thing, and besides these straightness and curvedness and anything like them. A thing is called such and such in virtue of each of these: by being triangular or square a thing is said to be of a certain quality, and by being straight or curved; and in virtue of its shape each thing is said to be of a certain quality. Rare and dense, rough and smooth, might be thought to signify a quality, but such things seem to fall outside the division of quality: each of them seems rather to reveal a certain position of the parts. A thing is dense because its parts are close to one another, rare because they are apart from one another; smooth because its parts lie somehow in a straight line, rough because some parts stick up and others are lower.

@p
@greek Ἴσως μὲν οὖν καὶ ἄλλος ἄν τις φανείη
There may perhaps be some other way of quality, but these are the ones most spoken of.

@p
@greek Ποιότητες μὲν οὖν εἰσὶν αἱ εἰρημέναι
The things we have named, then, are qualities; and things are qualified when they are called so paronymously from these, or in some other way from them. In most cases, indeed in nearly all, they are called so paronymously: the white man from whiteness, the grammarian from grammar, the just man from justice, and so with the others. But in some cases, because there are no names for the qualities, things cannot be called paronymously from them. The runner or the boxer, so called by a natural capacity, is not called so paronymously from any quality, since there are no names for the capacities by which these men are called such and such. It is different with the branches of knowledge by which men are called boxers or wrestlers by their condition: there is a knowledge called boxing, and one called wrestling, and those in that condition are called such and such paronymously from them. Sometimes, though there is a name, what is called such and such by the quality is not called so paronymously: the good man, for instance, from virtue. He is called good because he has virtue, but not paronymously from virtue. This is not often the case, however. So things are called qualified when they are called so paronymously from the qualities we have named, or in some other way from them.
!note "Good" here is σπουδαῖος, the serious, upright man of the Ethics; its root has nothing to do with ἀρετή, "virtue", which is Aristotle's point.

@p
@greek Ὑπάρχει δὲ καὶ ἐναντιότης κατὰ τὸ ποιόν
There is contrariety in quality too: justice, for instance, is contrary to injustice, and whiteness to blackness, and so on; and so are the things called such and such in virtue of them, the unjust to the just and the white to the black. But this is not so in every case: nothing is contrary to red or yellow or such colours, though they are qualities. Further, if one of two contraries is a quality, the other too will be a quality. This is clear if one goes through the other categories. If justice is contrary to injustice, for instance, and justice is a quality, then injustice is a quality too: none of the other categories will fit injustice, neither quantity nor relative nor where, nor anything else of that kind at all, except quality. So too with the other contraries in quality.

@p
@greek Ἐπιδέχεται δὲ τὸ μᾶλλον καὶ τὸ ἦττον τὰ ποιά
Qualified things admit of more and less. One thing is called more or less white than another, and one more just than another. And the same thing can increase: what is white can still become whiter. Not all, but most. It might be questioned whether one justice is called more or less a justice than another, and likewise with the other conditions. Some people dispute about such cases. They say that one justice ought not to be called more or less a justice than another, nor one health more or less a health than another, but that one man has less health than another, and less justice than another, and so with grammar and the other conditions. But the things called such and such in virtue of these undisputedly admit of more and less: one man is called more grammatical than another, healthier and more just, and so with the others. Triangle and square, however, do not seem to admit of more, nor does any other figure. Things that take the account of a triangle or of a circle are all equally triangles or circles; and of things that do not take it, none will be said to be more so than another: the square is no more a circle than the oblong is, since neither takes the account of a circle. In short, unless both take the account of the thing in question, one will not be called more so than the other. So not all qualified things admit of more and less.

@p
@greek Τῶν μὲν οὖν εἰρημένων οὐδὲν ἴδιον ποιότητος
None of the features we have named is distinctive of quality; but things are called like and unlike in virtue of qualities alone. One thing is not like another in virtue of anything but that in virtue of which it is qualified. So it would be distinctive of quality that things are called like and unlike in virtue of it.

@p
@greek Οὐ δεῖ δὲ ταράττεσθαι
We should not be troubled in case someone says that, though we set out to discuss quality, we counted in many relatives, since we said that states and conditions are relatives. In nearly all such cases the genera are called relatives, but none of the particular things is. Knowledge, a genus, is said to be just what it is of something else (it is called knowledge of something), but none of the particular branches of knowledge is said to be just what it is of something else: grammar is not called the grammar of something, nor music, the musician's art, the music of something. If at all, these too are called relatives by way of their genus: grammar is called the knowledge of something, not the grammar of something, and music the knowledge of something, not the music of something. So the particular branches are not relatives. And it is by the particular ones that we are called such and such, since it is these that we have: we are called knowledgeable because we have one of the particular branches of knowledge. So these, the particular ones, by which we are on occasion called such and such, would be qualities; and they are not relatives. Further, if the same thing should happen to be both a relative and a quality, there is nothing absurd in counting it in both genera.
!note Music (μουσική) to a Greek covered poetry and the arts of the Muses as well as music; it is the musician's skill that is meant.

@ch Cat.9 | Doing and being affected; the other categories
Doing and being affected admit of contraries and of more and less. Of being in a position it has been said that it is named from positions; when, where and having are plain enough to need nothing beyond the examples given at the start.

@p
Doing and being affected also admit of contrariety, and of more and less. Heating is contrary to cooling, and being heated to being cooled, and being pleased to being pained; so they admit of contrariety. And of more and less too: it is possible to heat more and less, and to be heated more and less. So doing and being affected admit of more and less.

About the rest, when, where and having, because they are obvious nothing more is said about them than what was said at the start: that having signifies having shoes on, having armour on; where, for instance, in the Lyceum; and the other things said about them.

@ch Cat.10 | The four kinds of opposite
Things are opposed in four ways: as relatives (double, half), as contraries (good, bad), as privation and possession (blindness, sight), and as affirmation and denial ("he sits", "he does not sit"). Each kind differs from the others; only of affirmation and denial is one always true and the other false.

@p
Enough has been said about the genera we proposed. We must now speak of opposites, and of the number of ways in which things are usually said to be opposed. One thing is said to be opposed to another in four ways: as relatives, as contraries, as privation and possession, or as affirmation and denial. To give a rough idea, each of these is opposed as follows: as relatives, the double to the half; as contraries, the bad to the good; as privation and possession, blindness and sight; as affirmation and denial, "he is sitting" and "he is not sitting".
!note "Possession" is ἕξις, the word rendered "state" in chapter 8: here it is the having of a capacity, such as sight, whose loss is a privation.

@p
@greek Ὅσα μὲν οὖν ὡς τὰ πρός τι ἀντίκειται
Things opposed as relatives are said to be just what they are of their opposites, or in some other way in relation to them. The double, for instance, is said to be just what it is, double, of something else, since it is double of something. Knowledge, too, is opposed as a relative to the knowable, and knowledge is said to be just what it is of the knowable; and the knowable too is said to be just what it is in relation to its opposite, knowledge, since the knowable is called knowable by something, by knowledge.

@p
@greek Ὅσα οὖν ἀντίκειται ὡς τὰ πρός τι
Things opposed as relatives, then, are said to be just what they are of others, or are in some way said in relation to one another. But things opposed as contraries are never said to be just what they are in relation to one another, though they are said to be contrary to one another. The good is not called the good of the bad, but its contrary; nor is the white called the white of the black, but its contrary. So these two kinds of opposition differ from one another. Where contraries are such that one or other of them must belong to the things in which they naturally occur, or of which they are predicated, there is nothing between them. But where it is not necessary for one or the other to belong, there is always something between them. Sickness and health, for instance, naturally occur in the body of an animal, and one or other of them, sickness or health, must belong to an animal's body. And odd and even are predicated of number, and one or other, odd or even, must belong to a number. And there is nothing between these, neither between sickness and health nor between odd and even. But where it is not necessary for one or the other to belong, there is something between them. Black and white, for instance, naturally occur in body, but it is not necessary for one or the other to belong to a body, since not every body is either white or black. And bad and good are predicated both of man and of many other things, but it is not necessary for one or the other to belong to the things they are predicated of, since not everything is either bad or good. And there is something between these: between white and black there are grey and yellow and the other colours, and between bad and good there is what is neither bad nor good. In some cases there are names for what lies between, as grey and yellow and the other colours between white and black. In other cases it is not easy to give a name to what lies between, but it is defined by the denial of each of the extremes, as with what is neither good nor bad, and neither just nor unjust.

@p
@greek Στέρησις δὲ καὶ ἕξις λέγεται μὲν περὶ ταὐτόν τι
Privation and possession are spoken of in connection with the same thing, as sight and blindness are with the eye. To put it generally, each of them is spoken of in connection with whatever the possession naturally occurs in. We say that something able to receive a possession is deprived of it when it is in no way present in that in which it naturally belongs, at the time when it is natural for it to have it. We call toothless not what has no teeth, and blind not what has no sight, but what does not have them at the time when it is natural for it to have them; for some creatures have neither sight nor teeth from birth, but they are not called toothless or blind. Being deprived and having the possession are not privation and possession. Sight is a possession, and blindness a privation; but having sight is not sight, nor is being blind blindness. Blindness is a privation, but being blind is being deprived, not a privation. Further, if blindness were the same as being blind, both would be predicated of the same thing; but a man is called blind, and a man is never called blindness. Yet these too, being deprived and having the possession, seem to be opposed as privation and possession, since the way of opposition is the same: as blindness is opposed to sight, so being blind is opposed to having sight.
!flag For the sight is habit, but blindness privation | Taylor's "habit" (as Edghill's "positive") renders ἕξις, which here is the having of a natural capacity, such as sight; the modern version says "possession". In chapter 8 the same word is a lasting "state", such as a virtue.

@p
@greek Οὐκ ἔστι δὲ οὐδὲ τὸ ὑπὸ τὴν ἀπόφασιν
Nor is what underlies an affirmation and a denial itself an affirmation and a denial. An affirmation is an affirmative statement, and a denial a negative statement, but none of the things that underlie an affirmation and a denial is a statement. But these too are said to be opposed to one another as affirmation and denial, since in their case too the way of opposition is the same. As an affirmation is opposed to a denial, "he is sitting" to "he is not sitting", so too the actual thing underlying each is opposed, his sitting to his not sitting.

@p
@greek Ὅτι δὲ ἡ στέρησις καὶ ἡ ἕξις οὐκ ἀντίκειται ὡς τὰ πρός τι
It is plain that privation and possession are not opposed as relatives, since neither is said to be just what it is of its opposite. Sight is not the sight of blindness, nor is it said in any other way in relation to it; nor, likewise, would blindness be called the blindness of sight. Blindness is called the privation of sight, but it is not called the blindness of sight. Further, all relatives are spoken of in relation to correlatives that reciprocate, so if blindness were a relative, what it is spoken of in relation to would reciprocate with it. But it does not: sight is not called the sight of blindness.

@p
@greek Ὅτι δὲ οὐδ᾿ ὡς τὰ ἐναντία ἀντίκειται
That things spoken of as privation and possession are not opposed as contraries either is clear from the following. Of contraries with nothing between them, one or the other must always belong to that in which they naturally occur, or of which they are predicated: for there was nothing between those contraries of which one or the other had to belong to what can receive them, as with sickness and health, and odd and even. But where there is something between, it is never necessary for one or the other to belong to everything: it is not necessary for everything that can receive them to be white or black, or hot or cold, since nothing prevents something between these from belonging to it. Further, there was something between those contraries of which it was not necessary for one or the other to belong to what can receive them, unless one of them belongs to it by nature, as being hot belongs to fire and being white to snow. In these cases it is necessary that one of them, and a definite one, belong, and not whichever it may chance: fire cannot be cold, nor snow black. So it is not necessary for one of them to belong to everything that can receive them, but only to those to which one belongs by nature, and to these the one, definitely, and not whichever it may chance. But with privation and possession neither of the things we have said is true. It is not necessary for one or the other always to belong to what can receive them, since what is not yet of an age to have sight is called neither blind nor sighted. So these would not be contraries of the kind with nothing between them. But neither are they of the kind with something between them, since at some time one or the other must belong to everything that can receive them: once a thing is of an age to have sight, it will be called either blind or sighted, and not one of these definitely, but whichever it may chance, since it need not be blind, nor need it be sighted, but either may chance. But with contraries that have something between them, it was never necessary for one or the other to belong to everything, but only to some, and to these one, definitely. So it is clear that things opposed as privation and possession are not opposed as contraries in either of these ways.

@p
@greek Ἔτι ἐπὶ μὲν τῶν ἐναντίων, ὑπάρχοντος τοῦ δεκτικοῦ
Further, with contraries, while the thing that can receive them is still there, a change from one to the other is possible, unless one of them belongs to something by nature, as being hot belongs to fire. What is healthy can fall sick, what is white can become black, and what is cold can become hot; and it is possible to become bad from good and good from bad. A bad man who is led into better ways of life and better talk will make some progress, however small, towards being better. And once he has made any progress, however small, it is plain that he might change completely, or at least make very great progress; for he grows ever more easily moved towards virtue, even if he has made only a little progress from the start, so that he is likely to make still more. And as this keeps happening, it brings him at last entirely to the contrary state, unless he is prevented by lack of time. But with possession and privation it is impossible for a change to occur both ways. There is change from possession to privation, but from privation to possession it is impossible: no one who has gone blind has seen again, no one who is bald has grown his hair again, and no one who has lost his teeth has grown new ones.

@p
@greek Ὅσα δὲ ὡς κατάφασις καὶ ἀπόφασις ἀντίκειται
Things opposed as affirmation and denial are plainly not opposed in any of the ways we have named, since in their case alone it is always necessary for one to be true and the other false. With contraries it is not always necessary for one to be true and the other false, nor with relatives, nor with possession and privation. Health and sickness, for instance, are contraries, and neither of them is either true or false. Likewise the double and the half are opposed as relatives, and neither of them is either true or false. Nor are things opposed as privation and possession, such as sight and blindness. In general, nothing said without any combination is either true or false, and all the things we have named are said without combination. Yet this might seem most of all to happen with contraries said in combination: "Socrates is well" is contrary to "Socrates is sick". But not even with these is it always necessary for one to be true and the other false. If Socrates exists, one will be true and the other false; but if he does not, both will be false, since neither "Socrates is sick" nor "Socrates is well" is true if Socrates himself does not exist at all. With privation and possession, if he does not exist at all, neither is true, and if he does, it is not always the case that one is true and the other false. "Socrates has sight" is opposed to "Socrates is blind" as privation and possession; and if he exists, it is not necessary for one to be true or false (when he is not yet of an age to have sight, both are false), and if Socrates does not exist at all, again both are false, both that he has sight and that he is blind. But with affirmation and denial, always, whether he exists or not, one will be false and the other true. Take "Socrates is sick" and "Socrates is not sick": if he exists, it is plain that one of them is true or false; and likewise if he does not, since if he does not exist "he is sick" is false, and "he is not sick" true. So it would be distinctive of these alone, the things opposed as affirmation and denial, that one of them is always true or false.

@ch Cat.11 | More on contraries
The contrary of a good is always a bad; the contrary of a bad is sometimes a good, sometimes a bad (excess and deficiency, with the mean contrary to both). If one contrary exists the other need not. Contraries occur in the same thing, in species or genus, and are in the same genus, in contrary genera, or are genera themselves.

@p
What is contrary to a good is necessarily a bad. This is clear by induction from each case: sickness is contrary to health, and cowardice to courage, and likewise with the others. But what is contrary to a bad is sometimes a good, sometimes a bad. To deficiency, which is a bad, excess is contrary, and it too is a bad; and likewise the mean, which is a good, is contrary to each of them. One would see this in few cases, however; in most, what is contrary to a bad is always a good.
!note Excess, deficiency and the mean between them are the frame of Aristotle's account of the virtues in the Nicomachean Ethics (II 6-8).

@p
@greek Ἔτι ἐπὶ τῶν ἐναντίων οὐκ ἀναγκαῖον
Further, with contraries, it is not necessary that if one exists the other does too. If everyone were well, there would be health but not sickness; likewise, if everything were white, there would be whiteness but not blackness. Further, if Socrates' being well is contrary to Socrates' being sick, and both cannot belong to the same thing at the same time, then it would not be possible, when one of the contraries exists, for the other to exist too: when Socrates' being well exists, Socrates' being sick would not.

@p
@greek Δῆλον δὲ ὅτι καὶ περὶ ταὐτὸν
It is clear too that contraries naturally occur in the same thing, the same either in species or in genus. Sickness and health naturally occur in the body of an animal, whiteness and blackness in body without qualification, and justice and injustice in the soul of a man.

@p
@greek Ἀνάγκη δὲ πάντα τὰ ἐναντία
All contraries must either be in the same genus, or in contrary genera, or be genera themselves. White and black are in the same genus, since colour is their genus; justice and injustice are in contrary genera, since the genus of the one is virtue and of the other vice; and good and bad are not in a genus, but are themselves the genera of certain things.

@ch Cat.12 | Prior
One thing is called prior to another in four ways: in time; in not reciprocating as to existence (one is prior to two); in order (the elements of a science, the preface of a speech); and in being better or more honoured. A fifth way: of things that reciprocate as to existence, the one that is the cause of the other's being is prior by nature.

@p
One thing is called prior to another in four ways. First and most properly, in time, the way in which one thing is called older and more ancient than another, since it is called older and more ancient because its time is longer. Second, what does not reciprocate as to the implication of existence: one, for instance, is prior to two. If there are two, it follows at once that there is one; but if there is one, there need not be two, so the implication of the other's existence does not reciprocate from the one. And what seems to be prior is that from which the implication of existence does not reciprocate.
!note "Does not reciprocate as to the implication of existence": two cannot exist without one, but one can exist without two.

@p
@greek Τρίτον δὲ κατά τινα τάξιν τὸ πρότερον λέγεται
Third, the prior is spoken of in respect of some order, as with sciences and speeches. In the demonstrative sciences there is prior and posterior in order (the elements are prior in order to the propositions, and in grammar the letters are prior to the syllables), and likewise with speeches, since the introduction is prior in order to the narrative.
!note "The elements" (στοιχεῖα) of geometry are its first propositions, as in Euclid's Elements; the "propositions" are διαγράμματα, literally the drawn figures that proved them. In grammar the same word στοιχεῖα means the letters.

@p
@greek Ἔτι παρὰ τὰ εἰρημένα τὸ βέλτιον
Further, besides the ways we have named, what is better and more honoured is thought to be prior by nature. People commonly say that those they honour more and love more are "first" with them. This way is perhaps the most out of place of all.

@p
@greek Οἱ μὲν οὖν λεγόμενοι τρόποι τοῦ προτέρου
These, then, are about all the ways in which prior is spoken of. But there would seem to be another way of being prior besides those we have named. Of things that reciprocate as to the implication of existence, the one that is in any way the cause of the other's existence might reasonably be called prior by nature. That there are such cases is clear. That there is a man reciprocates as to the implication of existence with the true statement about it: if there is a man, the statement by which we say that there is a man is true. And it reciprocates: if the statement by which we say that there is a man is true, there is a man. But the true statement is in no way the cause of the thing's existence, whereas the thing does seem in some way to be the cause of the statement's being true, since it is because the thing is or is not that the statement is called true or false. So one thing may be called prior to another in five ways.

@ch Cat.13 | Simultaneous
Things are simultaneous without qualification when they come to be at the same time; simultaneous by nature when they reciprocate as to existence and neither causes the other (double and half), and when they are species dividing the same genus (winged, footed, water-dwelling). The genus is always prior to its species.

@p
Things are called simultaneous without qualification, and most properly, when they come to be at the same time, since neither is prior or posterior. These are called simultaneous in time. Things are simultaneous by nature when they reciprocate as to the implication of existence, but neither is in any way the cause of the other's existence, as with the double and the half. These reciprocate, since if there is a double there is a half, and if there is a half there is a double, but neither is the cause of the other's existence.

@p
@greek Καὶ τὰ ἐκ τοῦ αὐτοῦ δὲ γένους
Things that are divided against one another from the same genus are also called simultaneous by nature. Things are said to be divided against one another when they come under the same division: the winged, for instance, against the footed and the water-dwelling. These are divided against one another from the same genus, since animal is divided into them, into the winged, the footed and the water-dwelling, and none of them is prior or posterior; such things seem to be simultaneous by nature. And each of these, the footed, the winged and the water-dwelling, might be divided again into species. Those too, then, will be simultaneous by nature, which come from the same genus under the same division. But genera are always prior to species, since they do not reciprocate as to the implication of existence: if there is something water-dwelling there is an animal, but if there is an animal there need not be something water-dwelling.

@p
@greek Ἄμα οὖν τῇ φύσει λέγεται
Things are called simultaneous by nature, then, when they reciprocate as to the implication of existence but neither is in any way the cause of the other's existence, and also when they are divided against one another from the same genus; and simultaneous without qualification when they come to be at the same time.

@ch Cat.14 | Change
There are six kinds of change: coming to be, passing away, increase, decrease, alteration and change of place. Alteration is distinct from the others: a thing can be altered without growing, and grow without being altered. Rest is contrary to change in general; to each kind, its own contrary; to alteration, rest in quality or change to the contrary quality.

@p
There are six kinds of change: coming to be, passing away, increase, decrease, alteration and change of place.
!note "Change" renders κίνησις, often rendered "motion" (as Taylor and Edghill do). Aristotle means any change, not only movement from place to place, which is the sixth kind.
!flag Of motion there are six species | Taylor's "motion" for κίνησις; Aristotle means change of every kind, of which motion from place to place is only one.

@p
@greek Αἱ μὲν οὖν ἄλλαι κινήσεις φανερὸν
That the other changes are different from one another is plain: coming to be is not passing away, nor is increase decrease, nor is change of place, and so with the others. But alteration raises a puzzle: perhaps what is altered must be altered by one of the other changes. This is not true. In nearly all our affections, or most of them, we happen to be altered without having any share in the other changes: what is changed by an affection need not increase or decrease, and so with the others. So alteration would be distinct from the other changes. If it were the same, what is altered would at once have to increase or decrease, or undergo one of the other changes; but it need not. Likewise, what increases, or undergoes some other change, would have to be altered. But there are things that increase and are not altered: a square, for instance, increases when a gnomon is put round it, but it has not become any different in kind; and so with other such things. So the changes would be different from one another.
!note A gnomon is the L-shaped figure that, fitted round a square, makes a larger square: the square grows, but stays a square.

@p
@greek Ἔστι δὲ ἁπλῶς μὲν κινήσει ἠρεμία ἐναντία
Rest is contrary to change in general; to each particular change, its particular contrary: to coming to be, passing away; to increase, decrease; to change of place, rest in place. But change towards the contrary place seems most of all to be opposed: upward change to downward, and downward to upward. As to the remaining one of the changes we have listed, it is not easy to say what is contrary to it. It seems that nothing is contrary to it, unless one were to set against it rest in quality, or change to the contrary quality, as with change of place we set rest in place or change to the contrary place. For alteration is change in quality. So change in quality will be opposed by rest in quality or by change to the contrary quality: becoming white, for instance, by becoming black. For a thing is altered when a change to the contrary quality takes place.

@ch Cat.15 | Having
"Having" is said in many ways: a state or quality, a size, clothing, a ring on a finger, a part of the body, what a vessel holds, a possession, and a wife or husband (the most out of place of all).

@p
Having is spoken of in several ways. As a state and condition, or some other quality: we are said to have a piece of knowledge, or a virtue. As a quantity, such as the size someone happens to have: he is said to have a height of three or four feet. As things on the body, such as a cloak or a tunic. As in a part, such as a ring on a hand. As a part, such as a hand or a foot. As in a vessel, as the bushel measure has the wheat, or the jar the wine: the jar is said to have wine, and the bushel wheat. So all these are said to have as in a vessel. As a possession: we are said to have a house or a field.
!note A medimnus, the Greek bushel, held about fifty litres of grain.

@p
@greek Λεγόμεθα δὲ καὶ γυναῖκα ἔχειν
We are also said to have a wife, and the wife a husband. But the way just mentioned seems the most out of place of all, since by "he has a wife" we signify nothing other than that he lives with her.

@p
@greek Ἴσως δ᾿ ἂν καὶ ἄλλοι τινὲς φανείησαν
Perhaps some other ways of having might come to light; but the ones usually spoken of have pretty well all been listed.
