@book I | The gods in Plato: the method, the sources, and the axioms of theology
Proclus opens with a preface to his friend Pericles: Plato's philosophy came down from the gods, was handed on by Plotinus and his heirs, and was taught to Proclus by Syrianus. He sets out how the work will go, what its reader needs, who a theologian is in Plato's sense, and the four ways Plato speaks of the gods. He names the dialogues that hold Plato's theology, and answers the objection that a theology gathered from many dialogues is patchwork: the whole of it is in one dialogue, the Parmenides.

@ch I.1 | Preface: Plato's philosophy, a gift of the gods
Proclus tells Pericles that Plato's philosophy first came to light by the gods' will, then hid itself, then shone again through a line of true interpreters: Plotinus, Amelius and Porphyry, Iamblichus and Theodorus, and at last his own teacher, Syrianus. He cannot thank Syrianus enough, but he can leave a record of what he was shown. So, as the Timaeus bids, he begins by calling on the gods.

@p
Pericles, dearest of my friends: I believe that the whole of Plato's philosophy first came to light by the kind will of higher beings. It showed the intellect hidden in them, and the truth that is together with the things that are, to souls who live in the world of becoming, so far as they are allowed to share in so great a good beyond nature. Afterwards, once it had reached its perfection, it turned back into itself, as it were, and was hidden from many who claimed to be philosophers and who were eager to search for true being. Then it came to light again. Above all, I think that the sacred teaching about the gods, which rests purely on a holy foundation and is always present with the gods themselves, was then revealed to those in time who were able to receive it, through one man. I would not be wrong to call him the first leader and the chief priest of those true mysteries into which souls are initiated once they are set free from the places of earth, and of those whole and steady visions which they share who truly embrace a happy and blessed life. From him this philosophy first shone out, holy and secret, as if set up in sacred temples and in their inner shrines. It was unknown to many of those who entered these holy places. Then, at set periods of time, it came out into the light as far as it could, through certain true priests, men who lived a life in keeping with the tradition of such mysteries. And it seems to me that the whole place became bright, and that visions of divine sights showed themselves everywhere.
!flag capable of enjoying it for a time | Taylor's "for a time" is unclear: it can be read as "for a while", or as "in time", that is, to souls who live in time. The modern version takes the second reading, which fits the contrast with the gods; the Greek is not yet on the site to settle it.
!note "One man" is Plato, as Taylor's footnote says. The "true mysteries" and their "visions" are the language of the Phaedrus (250b-c), where the soul, before birth, was initiated into the most blessed of mysteries and saw whole and steady visions.

@p
These are the interpreters of Plato's vision of the mysteries (the epopteia, the highest stage of initiation), who have unfolded for us the most sacred accounts of divine things and were given a nature like their leader's. I would name them as the Egyptian Plotinus; those who received the teaching from him, I mean Amelius and Porphyry; and in the third place those who grew from these like statues of heroes, Iamblichus and Theodorus; and any others who came after them, followed this divine choir, and worked at Plato's teachings with a mind inspired by the gods. From these came the man who, after the gods, has been our guide to everything beautiful and good. He received in his soul, undefiled, the most genuine and pure light of truth. He made us share in all the rest of Plato's philosophy. He passed on to us the secret teaching he had received from those older than himself. And he made us, together with him, stirred by the gods over the sacred truth about divine things.
!note The man who "after the gods" was Proclus' guide is his teacher Syrianus, head of the school at Athens before him, as Taylor's footnote says. Theodorus is Theodorus of Asine, a pupil of Porphyry and Iamblichus.

@p
If we tried to give this man thanks equal to the good we have received from him, the whole of time would not be enough. But it is needful not only that we have received from others the surpassing good of Plato's philosophy. We must also leave to those who come after us a record of the blessed sights that we have seen and, as far as we could, followed, under the most perfect leader of our time, a man who reached the summit of philosophy. So perhaps we will do well to call on the gods to kindle the light of truth in our soul, and to pray to the attendants and servants of the higher beings to guide our intellect and lead it to the complete, divine and lofty end of Plato's teaching. For I think that anyone who has even the least share of intelligence will always begin what he does from the gods, and most of all when he explains the gods. We cannot understand the divine nature in any other way than by being perfected in the gods' light. Nor can we make it known to others unless the gods guide us, keeping us free of the many shapes of opinion and of the shifting variety of words, and keeping the meaning of the divine names whole. Knowing this, and following the advice of Plato's Timaeus, we first set the gods as the leaders of the teaching about themselves. May they hear our prayers and be kind to us. May they come to us gently, guide the intellect of our soul, and lead it around Plato's Hestia, the hearth at the centre of his teaching, and up to the steep heights of this inquiry. When we arrive there, we will receive the whole truth about them, and our labour to bring forth our thoughts about divine things will reach its best end: wanting to know something about them, asking others about them, and at the same time, as far as we can, searching them out ourselves.
!flag lead it about the Vesta of Plato | Taylor gives Hestia her Latin name, Vesta. She is the goddess of the hearth, who in the Phaedrus (247a) stays at home while the other gods go round; Proclus means the fixed centre of Plato's teaching, round which the soul is led.
!note The Timaeus (27c) says that everyone with the least good sense calls on a god before any undertaking, small or great; Timaeus then prays before his account of the universe. Taylor's footnote: the Greek lacks the word "only" in "not only that we have received".

@ch I.2 | How the work will proceed, and what its hearer needs
The work has three parts: the common teachings about the gods; the orders of gods, in their procession; and the gods named here and there in Plato. It will prefer clarity, read symbols and images for what they stand for, and test everything by Plato's own writings. Its hearer must be good in character, trained in logic, at home in the study of nature and in mathematics, and must come to it with dialectic and with love.

@p
So much for the preface. Next I must explain the method of the teaching I propose, what one should expect it to be, and what preparation its hearer should bring, so that he is ready to approach, not my words, but Plato's philosophy, which lifts up the intellect and makes us like the gods. For the fitness of the hearers should match the kind of teaching. It is as in the mysteries. Those skilled in such things first prepare vessels to receive the gods. To bring the gods to be present, they do not use just any lifeless things, or any animals, or any men. Out of each kind they bring into the sacred rites only what is naturally able to share in divine light.
!note The "vessels" are the statues and other objects that the theurgists prepared to receive the presence of a god.

@p
First, then, I will divide this discourse into three parts. At the beginning I will look at all the common teachings about the gods that Plato gives in summary, and at the power and the high standing of the axioms of theology wherever they appear. In the middle of the work I will study the whole orders of the gods, count their distinctive characters, mark out their processions as Plato does, and refer everything to the principles laid down by the theologians. At the end I will speak about the gods praised here and there in Plato's writings, whether they are above the world or in the world, and I will refer what is taught about them to the whole classes of the divine orders.
!note The three parts are Book I (the common teachings), Books II-VI (the orders of the gods, from the One down to the gods in the world), and a third part on particular gods, which is lost or was never written; Taylor's own seventh book stands in for it.

@p
In every part of this work, too, we will prefer what is clear, distinct and simple to their opposites. What is handed down through symbols we will turn into clear teaching. What is handed down through images we will carry back to the patterns they copy. What is written more as plain statement we will test by reasoning from causes. What is set out by demonstration we will examine, and we will also explain the kind of truth it holds and make it known to the hearers. What is put in riddles we will make clear from elsewhere, not from other people's assumptions, but from Plato's most genuine writings. And of the things the hearers meet at once, we will consider how they agree with the things themselves. From all this, one complete form of Plato's theology will appear to us: its truth, which runs through all of divine thought; the one intellect that gave birth to all the beauty of this theology; and the sacred unfolding of this vision. Such, as I have said, will be the present work.

@p
The hearer of these teachings is supposed to be adorned with the moral virtues. He should have bound, by the rule of virtue, every mean and unharmonious motion of the soul, and tuned them all to the single form of the practical wisdom that belongs to intellect. For, as Socrates says, it is not lawful for the impure to touch the pure. Every bad person is wholly impure, and the opposite kind of person is pure. He should also have been trained in all the methods of logic, and have studied many sound ways of analysis and many of division, and their opposites, in keeping, I think, with Parmenides' advice to Socrates. For until one has gone through such a contest of arguments, the knowledge of the divine kinds, and of the truth set in them, is hard to reach and hard to enter. Third, he must not be untrained in the study of nature. Whoever has become familiar with the many opinions of the natural philosophers, and has explored in a way, in images, the causes of the things that are, will more easily go on to the nature of the separate and first beings. So, as I have said, the hearer of this work must not be ignorant of the truth found in appearances, nor a stranger to the paths of learning and the mathematical studies they contain, for through these we gain a less bodily knowledge of divine being. But all of these must be bound together under intellect, which leads. He must also share in Plato's dialectic, and practise the immaterial activities that are separate from bodily powers. He must long to contemplate true beings by intellectual insight joined with reason. Then let him give himself wholly to explaining the divine and blessed teachings, and fill his soul, as the Oracle says, with deep love. For, as Plato says somewhere, to grasp this vision no better helper than love can be found.
!flag intelligence in conjunction with reason [true] beings | Taylor adds "true" in brackets, and his footnote corrects the Greek he translated (from "intelligence with reason" as a subject to "by intelligence with reason"). The phrase is the Timaeus' (28a): what always is, is grasped "by intellectual insight together with a rational account".
!note "It is not lawful for the impure to touch the pure" is Socrates in the Phaedo (67b). Parmenides advises the young Socrates to train himself in the method of hypothesis before he speaks of the Forms (Parmenides 135c-136c). The Oracle is one of the Chaldean Oracles, which bid the soul fill itself with "deep love". Plato says that for this possession human nature could find no better helper than love in the Symposium (212b).

@p
He must also be trained in the truth that runs through all things, and wake his intelligible eye, the eye of intellect, to what is real and complete truth. He must settle himself in a firm, unshaken and safe kind of divine knowledge. He must be persuaded to admire nothing else, and not even to turn his attention to other things, but to hurry towards divine light with a fearless activity of reasoning and with the power of a tireless life. In short, he must set before himself the kind of activity and rest proper to one who means to be such a leader of the choir as Socrates describes in the Theaetetus. Such, then, is the size of our task, and such the method of our discourses on it. But before I begin to set out what I have proposed, I want to speak about theology itself, its different kinds, and which forms of theology Plato approves and which he rejects. Once these are known, we will more easily learn, in what follows, what supports the demonstrations themselves.
!note The "leader of the choir" (coryphaeus) is the true philosopher of the Theaetetus' digression (173c-174a), who does not know the way to the market place, but whose mind ranges over the whole of nature.

@ch I.3 | Who a theologian is, and how the gods are known
Everyone who has spoken of theology calls the first principles "gods", but they disagree on what is first: bodies, soul or intellect. Plato alone goes beyond intellect to a principle above it, the One, from which the henads proceed. The theologian knows the gods not by sense, opinion, reasoning or even intellect, but by the "one" of the soul, its highest point, which is joined to the henads. Turning inward, the soul finds first itself, then intellect, then the gods.

@p
Everyone who has ever touched on theology has called the things that are first by nature "gods", and has said that the science of theology is about them. Some have held that bodily being is the only thing that exists at all. They have put every kind of bodiless nature second to it in being, taken the principles of things to be bodily in form, and shown that the state in us by which we know these principles is bodily too. Others make all bodies depend on bodiless natures, and place the first hyparxis in soul and its powers. These, I think, call the best of souls "gods", and they call the science that reaches as far as these souls, and knows them, "theology". Still others bring the many souls out of another, older principle, and set intellect as the leader of all things. These say that the best end is the union of the soul with intellect, and they think the life of intellect the most honourable of all things. No doubt they also take theology and the study of intellectual being to be one and the same. All these, then, as I have said, call the first and most self-sufficient principles of things "gods", and the science of them "theology".
!note Taylor's footnote: hyparxis is "the summit of any nature, or blossom, as it were, of its being", its first and highest mode of existence. The Workshop keeps the word.

@p
But Plato's account of the divine, and his alone, rejects every bodily nature as a principle. Everything that can be divided and has extension cannot by nature make or keep itself in being. It has its being, its activity and its being acted on through soul and the motions soul contains. Plato shows that the being of soul is older than bodies, but depends in turn on an intellectual reality. For whatever is moved in time, even if it moves itself, is of a more ruling nature than what is moved by another, but comes after an eternal motion. So he shows, as we have said, that intellect is the father and cause of bodies and souls, and that everything whose life is spent in changes and unfoldings both exists and acts around intellect.
!note Plato shows that soul, the self-moving motion, is older than bodies in the Laws (X 896a-c) and the Phaedrus (245c-e), and that soul depends on intellect in the Timaeus (30b) and the Philebus (30c).

@p
Plato, however, goes on to another principle, wholly set apart from intellect, more bodiless and more inexpressible, from which all things, even the very last, must have their existence. For not all things are by nature fit to share in soul, but only those that have some life in them, clearer or dimmer. Nor can all things enjoy intellect and being, but only those that exist according to Form. But the principle of all things must be shared by all things, if it is to leave nothing out, since it is the cause of everything that in any way is said to be. Plato found by divine insight this first principle of all things, which is higher than intellect and hidden in places no one can reach. He showed three causes, or monads, and showed that they are above bodies: soul, the first intellect, and a union above intellect. From these, as from monads, he brings out their own numbers: one multitude made of unities, a second made of intellects, and a third made of souls. For every monad leads a multitude of its own rank. And as Plato joins bodies to souls, so he joins souls to intellectual Forms, and these again to the henads of beings. But he turns all things back to one unshared unity. Once he has run back as far as this unity, he holds that he has reached the highest end of the study of all things, and that this is the truth about the gods: the truth that is about the henads of beings, and teaches their processions and their distinctive characters, how beings are in contact with them, and the orders of Forms that depend on these unitary realities.
!flag one multitude indeed being uniform | Taylor's "uniform" (Greek henoeides, "one-like") does not mean "all the same": it means having the form of the One, made of unities. The first multitude is that of the henads, the gods.
!note Taylor's two footnotes on this paragraph: "unical" means "characterized by unity", and "unity" here (the henads) means "of the nature of the One". The Workshop renders Taylor's "unities" as "henads", and his "unical" as "unitary".

@p
Plato teaches us that the study of intellect, and of the Forms and kinds that turn about intellect, comes after the science of the gods themselves. The study of intellect grasps the intelligibles, and the Forms that the soul can know through the outward-reaching activity of intellect. But the science of theology goes beyond this. It is about the secret and inexpressible hyparxeis of the gods, and it traces how they are set apart from each other and how they come to light from the one cause of all. So I believe that the intellectual part of the soul can grasp the intellectual Forms and the difference among them. But the summit of intellect, its "flower", as they say, and its hyparxis, is joined to the henads of beings, and through these to the hidden union of all the divine henads. For though we have many powers of knowing, it is through this alone that we are by nature able to be joined to that hidden union and to share in it. The race of the gods cannot be grasped by sense, since it is set above all bodies. Nor by opinion and discursive reason, for these are divided and deal with things of many forms. Nor by intellectual insight joined with reason, for that kind of knowledge belongs to true beings. But the hyparxis of the gods rides upon beings, and is marked out by the very union of all things. So it follows, if the divine nature can be known in any way at all, that it must be grasped by the hyparxis of the soul, and known through it as far as possible. For we say that everywhere like is known by like: the perceptible by sense, what is opined by opinion, what is reasoned by discursive reason, and the intelligible by intellect. So the most unitary nature must be known by the one in us, and the inexpressible by what is inexpressible in us.
!note Taylor's footnotes: dianoia, here "discursive reason", is "the discursive activity of reason, or the power of the soul that reasons scientifically"; "the doxastic" is the object of opinion. "The flower of intellect" is the Chaldean Oracles' phrase. The hyparxis of the gods "rides upon" beings as the gods in the Phaedrus (247b-c) ride in their chariots on the back of the heaven.

@p
Socrates rightly says in the First Alcibiades that the soul, when it enters into itself, will see all other things, and god himself. For as it bends towards its own unity, the centre of all life, and lays aside multitude and the variety of the many powers it holds, it climbs to the highest look-out over all beings. In the holiest of the mysteries, they say, the initiates first meet the many kinds of many shapes that are hurled out in front of the gods. But when they enter the inside of the temple, unmoved and guarded by the rites, they truly receive divine light into themselves, and, stripped of their clothes, as they would say, they share in the divine nature. The same thing, I think, happens in the vision of all things. When the soul looks at what comes after it, it sees the shadows and images of beings. But when it turns back to itself, it unfolds its own being and the reason-forms it holds. At first it sees, as it were, only itself. But when it goes deeper into the knowledge of itself, it finds in itself both intellect and the orders of beings. And when it goes into its inmost part, the inner shrine, as it were, of the soul, it sees, with its eyes closed, the race of the gods and the henads of beings. For all things are in us in the manner of soul, and so we are by nature able to know all things, by waking the powers and images of the whole that we hold.
!flag the multiform, and many-shaped genera, which are hurled forth before the Gods | Taylor's footnote says these are "evil daemons". They are the frightening apparitions that, in the rites, appear before the god appears; the modern version keeps Proclus' image.
!note The First Alcibiades (132c-133c): the soul that looks into the best part of itself, where wisdom is, will know god and itself. "With its eyes closed" recalls the initiate (mystes) of the mysteries, whose name Proclus' readers took from "closing" (myein) the eyes.

@p
And this is the best use of our activity: to reach out to the divine nature itself, with our powers at rest; to circle around it in harmony; to wake all the multitude of the soul to this union; and, laying aside everything that comes after the One, to settle ourselves in, and be joined to, what is inexpressible and beyond all things. For the soul may climb until it ends its flight in the principle of things. When it has arrived there and seen the place there, it may come down from it and make its way through beings, unfolding the many Forms, searching out their monads and their numbers, and grasping by intellect how each depends on its own henad. Then we may hold that the soul has the most perfect science of divine natures, seeing as one the processions of the gods into beings, and the distinctions of beings around the gods. Such, then, by Plato's judgement, is our theologian. And theology is a state of this kind: it unfolds the very hyparxis of the gods; it sets apart and contemplates their unknowable and unitary light, distinct from the character of the things that share in them; and it makes it known to those worthy of this activity, which is blessed and holds all things together at once.
!note "The place there" is the place beyond the heaven of the Phaedrus (247c), which the gods and the best souls see.

@ch I.4 | The four ways Plato teaches about the gods
Plato speaks of the gods in four ways: inspired (as in the Phaedrus), dialectically (the Sophist and the Parmenides), in symbols and myths (the Gorgias, Symposium and Protagoras), and through images, mathematical or ethical (the Timaeus, the Statesman, the Republic). These answer to four traditions: the inspired initiators, the Orphic myths, Pythagorean mathematics, and Plato's own science. Plato keeps only myths that are worthy of the gods, and he does not reduce them to physics.

@p
After this complete account of the first part of our inquiry, we must set out the ways in which Plato teaches us the sacred ideas about divine natures. For he does not seem to have taught about them in the same way everywhere. Sometimes he unfolds the truth about them by an activity inspired by the gods, and at other times dialectically. Sometimes he announces their inexpressible characters in symbols, and at other times he goes back up to them from images and finds in them the first causes of all things. In the Phaedrus, inspired by the Nymphs, and having exchanged human understanding for something better, divine madness, he reveals with an inspired voice many secret teachings about the intellectual gods, and many about the liberated rulers of the universe, who lead the many gods of the world upward to the monads that are intelligible and separate from the wholes of the world. And speaking still more of the gods to whom the world is allotted, he praises their acts of thinking, their making of the world, their pure providence and their governing of souls, and everything else that Socrates says in that dialogue in an inspired way, as he plainly says himself, laying this madness to the gods of the place.
!flag the liberated rulers of the universe | "Liberated" (Greek apolytoi, "unbound") is Proclus' name for an order of gods between the gods above the world and those in it: they rule the world but are not bound to it. The Workshop keeps Taylor's word.
!note In the Phaedrus Socrates says he is "possessed by the Nymphs" (238c-d, 241e) and blames "the gods of the place" (262d); his second speech tells of the gods' procession round the heaven (246e-247c).

@p
In the Sophist, arguing dialectically about being and about how the One is set apart from beings, and raising difficulties against those older than himself, he shows how all beings depend on their cause and on the first being. Being itself shares in the unity that is set above all things. It is a one that has unity as something it undergoes, but not the One itself: it is subject to the One and united to it, but it is not what is first of all one. In the same way, in the Parmenides, he unfolds dialectically, through the first hypotheses, the processions of being from the One and the One's transcendence, and does so, as he says in that dialogue, by the most perfect division of this method. Again, in the Gorgias he tells the myth of the three Craftsmen and of their allotment as makers of the world. This is not only a myth but a true account. In the Symposium he speaks of the union brought by Love. And in the Protagoras he speaks of how the mortal animals were given their shares by the gods. In these he hides the truth about divine natures in symbols, and unfolds his mind only so far as to hint at it to the most genuine of his hearers.
!flag that it is a passive one | Taylor's "passive one" renders the Sophist's phrase (245a): being "has unity as an affection", has the one as something that happens to it. It does not mean that being is inactive; the modern version says what is meant.
!note The Sophist (244b-245e) argues against those before Plato who said that all is one. The "three Craftsmen" of the Gorgias (523a) are Zeus, Poseidon and Pluto (Hades), who divide the realm of Kronos, as in the Iliad (15.187-193). Aristophanes' speech in the Symposium (189c-193d) and Diotima's (201d-212a) speak of love that unites; the Protagoras' myth (320c-322a) tells how Epimetheus and Prometheus gave the animals their powers.

@p
Then there is the teaching given through the mathematical studies, and the treatment of divine things drawn from ethical or physical discussions, of which much can be found in the Timaeus, much in the dialogue called the Statesman, and much scattered in other dialogues. If you like, I will mention these too. Here also, for those of you who want to know divine things through images, the method will be plain. For all these give a shadowy picture of the powers of the divine. The Statesman, for example, pictures the making of the world in the heavens. The shapes of the five elements, given in geometrical proportions in the Timaeus, show in images the distinctive characters of the gods who ride on the parts of the universe. And the divisions of the being of soul in that dialogue give a shadowy picture of the whole orders of the gods.
!note The Statesman's myth of the world's two motions (269c-274e); the five regular solids of the Timaeus (53c-56c); the division of the world-soul by numbers (35a-36d). Taylor's footnote: the words "in the Timaeus" are missing from the Greek he translated.

@p
I pass over the fact that Plato composes constitutions, likening them to divine natures and to the whole world, and orders them from the powers the world holds. Through the likeness of mortal things to divine ones, all of these show us in images the processions, orders and works of making of the divine natures. These, then, are the ways of teaching about the gods that Plato uses.
!note The constitutions are those of the Republic and the Laws; the Republic's city is "a pattern laid up in heaven" (592b).

@p
It is clear from what has been said that there must be just this many of them. Those who treat divine things by hints either speak in symbols and myths, or through images. Of those who state their thoughts openly, some build their accounts by science, and others by inspiration from the gods. Whoever wants to point to divine things through symbols is Orphic, and in short agrees with those who write myths about the gods. Whoever does it through images is Pythagorean. For the Pythagoreans invented the mathematical studies to help recollect divine things, which they try to reach through these, as through images. According to the witness of their historians, they refer both numbers and shapes to the gods. The inspired person, who unfolds the truth about the gods by itself under divine inspiration, stands most clearly among the highest of the initiators. These do not think it right to show the divine orders or their characters to their companions through veils, but announce the gods' powers and numbers because the gods themselves move them. But to hand down divine things by science is the glorious distinction of Plato's philosophy. For Plato alone, I think, of all those known to us, has tried to divide by method, and set in order, the regular procession of the divine kinds, how they differ from each other, the characters common to the whole orders, and the characters given out to each one. The truth of this will be clear when we give the proper demonstrations about the Parmenides and all the divisions it contains.
!flag when we frame precedaneous demonstrations | Taylor's "precedaneous" (an old word for "going first, principal") means the demonstrations that belong first and properly to the subject: the account of the Parmenides, which is the core of the work.

@p
For now we will note that Plato does not accept all the inventions of poetry and drama, but only those that point to the beautiful and the good and do not clash with divine being. The way of myth, which points to divine things by guesswork, is ancient. It hides the truth under many veils, and works much as nature does, which holds out perceptible inventions in place of intelligible things, material in place of immaterial, divisible in place of indivisible, and images, things that have a false being, in place of what is wholly true. But Plato rejects the more tragic way of making myths used by the old poets. They wanted to set up a secret theology about the gods, and for this they invented wanderings, cuttings, battles, tearings apart, rapes and adulteries of the gods, and many other symbols of the truth about the divine that this theology hides. This way Plato rejects, and he says that it is in every way most foreign to education. But he holds that those myths about the gods are more convincing, and closer to the truth and to the philosophic temper, which say that the divine is the cause of all good and of no evil, that it does not change at all, always keeping its own order unchanged, and that it holds in itself the source of truth but is never the cause of any deceit for others. Such are the patterns of theology that Socrates gives in the Republic.
!note Republic II-III (377b-392c): Socrates rejects Hesiod's and Homer's stories of Kronos' deeds and the gods' battles, and lays down the "patterns of theology" (379a): god is the cause only of good things (379b-c), does not change (380d-381e), and does not deceive (382a-383a).

@p
So all of Plato's myths, while keeping the truth hidden, do not have even an outward dress that clashes with our untaught and unspoiled sense of the gods. They carry an image of the way the world is put together, in which the visible beauty is worthy of the divine, and a beauty more divine than this is set in the unseen lives and powers of the gods. This, then, is one of the ways of myth about divine things: it passes from what seems lawless, irrational and disordered into order and limit, and aims at putting together the beautiful and the good.

@p
But there is another way, which he teaches in the Phaedrus. It consists in always keeping the myths of theology unmixed with accounts of nature, and in taking care never to confuse theology and the study of nature with each other, or to swap one for the other. For as divine being is separate from the whole of nature, it is right that accounts of the gods should also be clean of physical explanations. Such a mixing, he says, is laborious, and to make the things that happen in nature the meaning of a myth is the work of no very good man: for example, someone who, by his supposed wisdom, takes the Chimaera, the Gorgon and the like to be natural events. Socrates in the Phaedrus disapproves of this way of handling myths, and makes its followers say, in a story, that Orithyia playing with the wind Boreas and thrown down the rocks means nothing more than that Orithyia, a mortal girl, was carried off by Boreas out of love. For it seems to me that the hidden meaning of myths about the gods should always be holier than the apparent one. So if anyone gives us physical interpretations of Plato's myths, about the things below the moon, we must say that they wholly miss the philosopher's aim. Only those interpretations truly explain the truth these myths hold which aim at a divine, immaterial and separate reality, and which, looking to it, build and take apart the myths in keeping with our inborn sense of divine things.
!note Phaedrus 229c-230a: Socrates will not explain away the myth of Boreas and Orithyia as a girl blown off the rocks by the north wind, since then one would have to explain Centaurs, the Chimaera, Gorgons and Pegasus too, "a laborious task, and not for a very lucky man". Taylor's footnote corrects a word of the Greek.

@ch I.5 | The dialogues that hold Plato's theology, and their orders of gods
Plato's teaching about the gods runs through every dialogue, but most fully through the Phaedo, Phaedrus, Symposium, Philebus, Sophist, Statesman, Cratylus and Timaeus; then the myths of the Gorgias and Protagoras, the Laws on providence, Republic X; then the Letters. Proclus names the orders of gods each one teaches. All Greek theology comes from Orpheus, through Pythagoras to Plato, and Plato agrees with the theologians wherever he speaks of the gods.

@p
Now that we have counted all these ways of Plato's theology, and shown what building and taking apart of myths fits the truth about the gods, let us next consider where, and from which dialogues above all, we think Plato's teachings about the gods can be gathered, and by looking for which patterns or forms we can tell his genuine writings from the false works that are put down to him.

@p
The truth about the gods runs, one may say, through all of Plato's dialogues. In all of them thoughts of the first philosophy are sown, holy, clear and above nature: in some more dimly, in others more plainly. These thoughts stir those who can in any way share in them towards the immaterial and separate being of the gods. In each part of the universe, and in nature herself, the Craftsman of all that the world holds set up likenesses of the unknowable hyparxis of the gods, so that all things might turn back to the divine nature through their kinship with it. In the same way, I believe, Plato's divine intellect weaves thoughts about the gods into all his writings, and leaves nothing without mention of the divine, so that from the whole of them a recollection of all things may be gained and given to the genuine lovers of divine things.

@p
But if I must set before the reader, out of many, the dialogues that above all unfold for us the sacred teaching about the gods, I would not be wrong to count among them the Phaedo and the Phaedrus, the Symposium and the Philebus, and with these the Sophist and the Statesman, the Cratylus and the Timaeus. All these are full, through and through, one may say, of Plato's divine science. In the second rank after these I would put the myth in the Gorgias and the one in the Protagoras; also what is said in the Laws about the gods' providence, and what is taught about the Fates, or the mother of the Fates, or the turnings of the universe, in the tenth book of the Republic. In the third rank you may, if you like, put those Letters through which we can reach the science of divine natures. For in them the three kings are mentioned, and very many other divine teachings worthy of Plato's vision are given. So we must look to these, and search out in them each order of the gods.
!note The Laws on providence: Book X (899d-905d). The Fates and their mother, Necessity, and the turning spindle of the universe: the myth of Er, Republic X (616b-617d). The "three kings" are in the Second Letter (312e): "all things are about the king of all ... the second about the second, and the third about the third".

@p
So from the Philebus we can receive the science of the one Good and of the two first principles of things, together with the triad that comes to light from them. You will find all of these set out clearly by Plato in that dialogue. From the Timaeus you can gain the study of the intelligibles, a divine account of the monad of the Craftsman, and the fullest truth about the gods of the world. From the Phaedrus, a scientific knowledge of all the intelligible and intellectual kinds, and of the liberated orders of gods, which are set just above the circuits of the heavens. From the Statesman, the study of the making of the world in the heavens, of the uneven periods of the universe, and of the intellectual causes of those periods. From the Sophist, the whole of becoming below the moon, and the distinctive character of the gods to whom the region below the moon is allotted, who preside over its comings to be and passings away. And about each of the gods we can find many ideas fit for sacred things in the Symposium, many in the Cratylus, and many in the Phaedo. For in each of these dialogues the divine names are mentioned, more or less, and from these it is easy for those trained in divine things to find out, by reasoning, the distinctive character of each.

!note The Phaedrus' place "above the heaven" and the gods' procession (246e-247e); the Statesman's two periods of the universe (269c-270a).

@p
But we must show that each teaching agrees with Plato's principles and with the sacred traditions of the theologians. For all Greek theology is the offspring of the sacred tradition of Orpheus. Pythagoras first learnt the rites of the gods from Aglaophemus, and Plato, second, received a complete science of the gods from the writings of the Pythagoreans and the Orphics. For in the Philebus, referring the study of the two kinds of principles, limit and the unlimited, to the Pythagoreans, he calls them men who live with the gods and are truly blessed. Philolaus the Pythagorean left us in writing many wonderful thoughts about these principles, praising their common procession into beings and the separate work of making that each performs. In the Timaeus, when Plato means to teach us about the gods below the moon and their order, he turns to the theologians, calls them the sons of the gods, and makes them the fathers of the truth about these gods. And at last he gives the orders of the gods below the moon as coming from the wholes, following the procession of the intellectual kings that they teach. Again, in the Cratylus he follows the theologians' traditions about the order of the divine processions. In the Gorgias he takes up Homer's teaching about the three Craftsmen. In short, wherever he speaks of the gods, he speaks in keeping with the theologians' principles. He rejects the tragic side of mythical invention, but he lays down the first principles in common with the makers of myths.

@ch I.6 | An objection: theology gathered from many dialogues is patchwork
Someone may object that a theology pieced together from many dialogues is not Plato's, has no whole, and cannot show how the orders of gods proceed from the one principle. Besides, the myths and divine passages in Plato are there for the sake of ethics or physics, not for their own sake. So such a theology is borrowed and forced, not genuine.

@p
But perhaps someone will object here that we are not presenting Plato's theology properly, scattered as it is everywhere, and that we are trying to heap together different pieces from different dialogues, as if we were eager to collect many things into one mixture, instead of drawing them all from one and the same source. If that were so, we could refer different teachings to different works of Plato, but we would by no means have a proper, primary teaching about the gods. Nor would there be any dialogue that gives us the complete and whole procession of the divine kinds and how they are ranked with each other. We would be like those who try to get a whole from parts, for want of a whole that is prior to the parts, and to weave the perfect together out of imperfect things, when the imperfect ought, on the contrary, to have the first cause of its coming to be in the perfect. The Timaeus, for example, will teach us the study of the intelligible kinds, and the Phaedrus seems to give us an orderly account of the first intellectual orders. But where will we find how the intellectual is ranked with the intelligible? And how the second comes to be from the first? In short, we will not be able to show how the procession of the divine orders comes about from the one principle of all things, and how, in the generations of the gods, the orders between the One and the complete number are filled in.

@p
Further, the objector may say, where will be the holiness of your much-praised science of divine natures? It is absurd to call these teachings Plato's when they are gathered from many places and, as you admit, brought into Plato's philosophy under names foreign to it; nor can you show one whole and complete truth about divine natures. Perhaps, they will say, certain people younger than Plato have written and left to their pupils one complete form of theology. You, then, can bring out one whole account of nature from the Timaeus, and from the Republic or the Laws the finest teachings about character, which tend to one form of philosophy. Only the work of Plato that holds all the good of the first philosophy, and may be called the summit of the whole study, you leave aside, and so you will lack the most perfect knowledge of beings, unless you are so foolish as to boast of mythical inventions, though the explaining of such things is full of the probable and empty of the demonstrative. Besides, such things are only brought into Plato's dialogues by the way. The myth in the Protagoras, for instance, is put in for the sake of the art of politics and the demonstrations about it. In the same way the myth in the Republic is put in for the sake of justice, and the one in the Gorgias for the sake of self-control. For Plato joins mythical stories to inquiries into ethical teachings not for the sake of the myths, but for the sake of the main aim: so that we may not only train the intellectual part of the soul through contending arguments, but so that the divine part of the soul may receive the knowledge of beings more perfectly, through its sympathy with more sacred things. For from other kinds of discourse we seem like people forced to accept the truth; but from myths we are moved in an inexpressible way, and we call up our unspoiled ideas, revering the sacred teaching they hold.

@p
So, I think, Timaeus rightly thinks it fitting that we should bring forth the divine kinds by following the makers of myths, as sons of the gods, and accept that they always generate the second natures from the first, even if they speak without demonstration. For this kind of discourse is not demonstrative but inspired, and the ancients invented it not from necessity but for the sake of persuasion, aiming not at mere teaching but at sympathy with the things themselves. And if you are willing to look at the causes not only of the myths but of the other teachings of theology, you will find that some are scattered in Plato's dialogues for the sake of ethical questions, and others for the sake of physical ones. In the Philebus, Plato speaks of limit and the unlimited for the sake of pleasure and of the life lived by intellect. For I think the second are kinds of the first. In the Timaeus the account of the intelligible gods is taken up for the sake of the study of nature that is proposed. So everywhere images must be known from their patterns, and the patterns of material things must be immaterial, of perceptible things intelligible, and the patterns of natural forms must be separate.

@p
Again, in the Phaedrus, Plato praises the place above the heaven, the depth below the heaven, and every kind beneath this, for the sake of the madness of love, and of how the souls' recollection comes about and their passage there from here. Everywhere, one may say, the main aim is either physical or political, and the thoughts about divine natures come in either for the sake of discovery or of perfection. How, then, can a study like yours still be holy and above nature, and worth studying above everything, when it can show neither the whole in itself, nor the perfect, nor what comes first in Plato's writings, but lacks all these, and is forced, not natural, and has an order that is not its own but brought in from outside, as in a play? Such are the objections that may be raised against our plan.

@ch I.7 | The answer: the whole of Plato's theology is in the Parmenides
Plato speaks of the gods in keeping with tradition and with the nature of things, sometimes as a means, sometimes as his main aim. But there is one dialogue that holds the whole of theology: the Parmenides. In it all the divine kinds proceed in order from the first cause, the highest simplest and most hidden, the last most many. The other dialogues give parts of the teaching, and must be referred to it, as the Timaeus holds the whole science of nature.

@p
To an objection of this kind I will give a fair and clear answer. I say that Plato everywhere speaks of the gods in keeping with ancient report and with the nature of things. Sometimes, for the sake of the cause of the things in hand, he carries them back to the principles of the teachings, and from there, as from a look-out, studies the nature of what is in hand. But sometimes he sets the science of theology as his main aim. In the Phaedrus his subject is intelligible beauty, and how beauty, shared from there, runs through all things; and in the Symposium it is the order of love.
!note Phaedrus 249d-250e: the beauty seen above the heaven, and its image here; Symposium 210a-212a: the ascent to the beautiful itself.

@p
But if we must survey, in one dialogue of Plato's, the whole of theology, complete and connected, reaching to its full number, I may perhaps say something surprising, which only those close to us will find plain. Still, we must be bold, since we have begun on such arguments, and say against our opponents that the Parmenides, and the sacred ideas it holds, will give you all you want. For in this dialogue all the divine kinds proceed in order from the first cause and show how they are linked to and depend on each other. The highest, those akin to the One and of a first nature, are given a unitary, hidden and simple form of hyparxis. The last are multiplied, divided into many parts, and rich in number, but weaker in power than those of a higher order. The middle ones, in fitting proportion, are more composite than their causes but simpler than their own offspring. In short, all the axioms of the science of theology appear complete in this dialogue, and all the divine orders are shown linked together. So this is nothing other than the famous generation of the gods, and the procession of every kind of being from the inexpressible and unknowable cause of all things. The Parmenides, then, kindles in the lovers of Plato the whole and perfect light of the science of theology. After it, the dialogues mentioned before give out parts of the sacred teaching about the gods. All of them, one may say, share in divine wisdom and wake our own ideas about the divine nature. All the parts of this sacred teaching must be referred to these dialogues, and these again to the one complete study of the Parmenides. In this way, I think, we will make the more imperfect depend on the perfect, and the parts on the wholes, and we will show accounts made like the things they explain, as Plato's Timaeus says. Such, then, is our answer to the objection that may be raised against us. And so we refer Plato's teaching on the gods to the Parmenides, just as everyone with the least understanding agrees that the Timaeus holds the whole science of nature.

!note The Timaeus (29b): accounts are akin to the things they explain. The school read the hypotheses of the Parmenides' second part (137c-166c) as an account of all the orders of reality from the One down; the next chapters set out this reading.

@ch I.8 | Two kinds of opponents on the Parmenides
Proclus faces two groups. One holds that the Parmenides is only an exercise in arguing both sides. The other, more serious, reads its first hypotheses as about the first God, intellect, and what comes after, but will not divide them further into the many orders of gods. Against both he will set out the reading of his teacher, Syrianus.

@p
By this, however, I seem to have stirred up for myself a contest on two fronts against those who try to study Plato's writings. I see two kinds of people who will oppose what has been said. The first thinks it right to look for no other aim in the Parmenides than practice in arguing both sides, and not to bring into this dialogue a crowd of secret and intellectual teachings foreign to its purpose. The second kind are more serious than these, and lovers of the Forms. They say that one of the hypotheses is about the first God, another about the second God and the whole of intellectual nature, and a third about the natures after this, whether they are the higher kinds, or souls, or some other kind of beings. For to inquire into these details does not belong to the present discussion.
!note The first group follows Parmenides' own words (135c-136c), that what follows is "an exercise". The second is the reading of Plotinus (V.1.8) and his school: the first hypothesis is about the One, the second about intellect, the third about soul. "The higher kinds" are the angels, daemons and heroes, the kinds above human souls.

@p
These, then, divide three of the hypotheses in this way. But they do not think it right to concern themselves with the many gods, the intelligible and intellectual kinds, the natures above the world and in the world, or to unfold all these by division and search them out with care. For according to them, though Plato speaks of intellectual beings in the second hypothesis, the nature of intellect is one, simple and undivided. Whoever holds the view of the Parmenides that we have stated must contend against both. But the contest is not equal against both. Those who make the Parmenides an exercise in logic are themselves attacked by those who hold the divine way of reading it. And those who do not unfold the many beings and the orders of divine natures are, as Homer says, in every way men to be revered and skilled. But for the sake of Plato's philosophy we must raise difficulties against them too, following in this our leader to the most holy and sacred truth. It is right also to set out, so far as it helps our aim, what we take to be the truth about the hypotheses of the Parmenides. For in this way, perhaps, by a process of reasoning, we may take in the whole of Plato's theology.
!note "To be revered and skilled" (or "awesome"): Helen's words to Priam in the Iliad (3.172), which Socrates uses of Parmenides in the Theaetetus (183e). "Our leader" is Syrianus.
