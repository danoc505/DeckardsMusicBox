@book I | The gods in Plato: the method, the sources, and the axioms of theology
Proclus opens with a preface to his friend Pericles: Plato's philosophy came down from the gods, was handed on by Plotinus and his heirs, and was taught to Proclus by Syrianus. He sets out how the work will go, what its reader needs, who a theologian is in Plato's sense, and the four ways Plato speaks of the gods. He names the dialogues that hold Plato's theology, and answers the objection that a theology gathered from many dialogues is patchwork: the whole of it is in one dialogue, the Parmenides.

@ch I.1 | Preface: Plato's philosophy, a gift of the gods
Proclus tells Pericles that Plato's philosophy first came to light by the gods' will, then hid itself, then shone again through a line of true interpreters: Plotinus, Amelius and Porphyry, Iamblichus and Theodorus, and at last his own teacher, Syrianus. He cannot thank Syrianus enough, but he can leave a record of what he was shown. So, as the Timaeus bids, he begins by calling on the gods.

@p
Pericles, dearest of my friends: I believe that the whole of Plato's philosophy first came to light by the kind will of higher beings. It showed the intellect hidden in them, and the truth that is together with the things that are, to souls who live in the world of becoming, so far as they are allowed to share in so great a good beyond nature. Afterwards, once it had reached its perfection, it turned back into itself, as it were, and was hidden from many who claimed to be philosophers and who were eager to search for true being. Then it came to light again. Above all, I think that the sacred teaching about the gods, which rests purely on a holy foundation and is always present with the gods themselves, was then revealed to those in time who were able to receive it, through one man. I would not be wrong to call him the first leader and the chief priest of those true mysteries into which souls are initiated once they are set free from the places of earth, and of those whole and steady visions which they share who truly embrace a happy and blessed life. From him this philosophy first shone out, holy and secret, as if set up in sacred temples and in their inner shrines. It was unknown to many of those who entered these holy places. Then, at set periods of time, it came out into the light as far as it could, through certain true priests, men who lived a life in keeping with the tradition of such mysteries. And it seems to me that the whole place became bright, and that visions of divine sights showed themselves everywhere.
!flag capable of enjoying it for a time | Taylor's "for a time" is unclear: it can be read as "for a while", or as "in time", that is, to souls who live in time. The modern version takes the second reading, which fits the contrast with the gods; the Greek is not yet on the site to settle it.
!note "One man" is Plato, as Taylor's footnote says. The "true mysteries" and their "visions" are the language of the Phaedrus (250b-c), where the soul, before birth, was initiated into the most blessed of mysteries and saw whole and steady visions.

@p
These are the interpreters of Plato's vision of the mysteries (the epopteia, the highest stage of initiation), who have unfolded for us the most sacred accounts of divine things and were given a nature like their leader's. I would name them as the Egyptian Plotinus; those who received the teaching from him, I mean Amelius and Porphyry; and in the third place those who grew from these like statues of heroes, Iamblichus and Theodorus; and any others who came after them, followed this divine choir, and worked at Plato's teachings with a mind inspired by the gods. From these came the man who, after the gods, has been our guide to everything beautiful and good. He received in his soul, undefiled, the most genuine and pure light of truth. He made us share in all the rest of Plato's philosophy. He passed on to us the secret teaching he had received from those older than himself. And he made us, together with him, stirred by the gods over the sacred truth about divine things.
!note The man who "after the gods" was Proclus' guide is his teacher Syrianus, head of the school at Athens before him, as Taylor's footnote says. Theodorus is Theodorus of Asine, a pupil of Porphyry and Iamblichus.

@p
If we tried to give this man thanks equal to the good we have received from him, the whole of time would not be enough. But it is needful not only that we have received from others the surpassing good of Plato's philosophy. We must also leave to those who come after us a record of the blessed sights that we have seen and, as far as we could, followed, under the most perfect leader of our time, a man who reached the summit of philosophy. So perhaps we will do well to call on the gods to kindle the light of truth in our soul, and to pray to the attendants and servants of the higher beings to guide our intellect and lead it to the complete, divine and lofty end of Plato's teaching. For I think that anyone who has even the least share of intelligence will always begin what he does from the gods, and most of all when he explains the gods. We cannot understand the divine nature in any other way than by being perfected in the gods' light. Nor can we make it known to others unless the gods guide us, keeping us free of the many shapes of opinion and of the shifting variety of words, and keeping the meaning of the divine names whole. Knowing this, and following the advice of Plato's Timaeus, we first set the gods as the leaders of the teaching about themselves. May they hear our prayers and be kind to us. May they come to us gently, guide the intellect of our soul, and lead it around Plato's Hestia, the hearth at the centre of his teaching, and up to the steep heights of this inquiry. When we arrive there, we will receive the whole truth about them, and our labour to bring forth our thoughts about divine things will reach its best end: wanting to know something about them, asking others about them, and at the same time, as far as we can, searching them out ourselves.
!flag lead it about the Vesta of Plato | Taylor gives Hestia her Latin name, Vesta. She is the goddess of the hearth, who in the Phaedrus (247a) stays at home while the other gods go round; Proclus means the fixed centre of Plato's teaching, round which the soul is led.
!note The Timaeus (27c) says that everyone with the least good sense calls on a god before any undertaking, small or great; Timaeus then prays before his account of the universe. Taylor's footnote: the Greek lacks the word "only" in "not only that we have received".

@ch I.2 | How the work will proceed, and what its hearer needs
The work has three parts: the common teachings about the gods; the orders of gods, in their procession; and the gods named here and there in Plato. It will prefer clarity, read symbols and images for what they stand for, and test everything by Plato's own writings. Its hearer must be good in character, trained in logic, at home in the study of nature and in mathematics, and must come to it with dialectic and with love.

@p
So much for the preface. Next I must explain the method of the teaching I propose, what one should expect it to be, and what preparation its hearer should bring, so that he is ready to approach, not my words, but Plato's philosophy, which lifts up the intellect and makes us like the gods. For the fitness of the hearers should match the kind of teaching. It is as in the mysteries. Those skilled in such things first prepare vessels to receive the gods. To bring the gods to be present, they do not use just any lifeless things, or any animals, or any men. Out of each kind they bring into the sacred rites only what is naturally able to share in divine light.
!note The "vessels" are the statues and other objects that the theurgists prepared to receive the presence of a god.

@p
First, then, I will divide this discourse into three parts. At the beginning I will look at all the common teachings about the gods that Plato gives in summary, and at the power and the high standing of the axioms of theology wherever they appear. In the middle of the work I will study the whole orders of the gods, count their distinctive characters, mark out their processions as Plato does, and refer everything to the principles laid down by the theologians. At the end I will speak about the gods praised here and there in Plato's writings, whether they are above the world or in the world, and I will refer what is taught about them to the whole classes of the divine orders.
!note The three parts are Book I (the common teachings), Books II-VI (the orders of the gods, from the One down to the gods in the world), and a third part on particular gods, which is lost or was never written; Taylor's own seventh book stands in for it.

@p
In every part of this work, too, we will prefer what is clear, distinct and simple to their opposites. What is handed down through symbols we will turn into clear teaching. What is handed down through images we will carry back to the patterns they copy. What is written more as plain statement we will test by reasoning from causes. What is set out by demonstration we will examine, and we will also explain the kind of truth it holds and make it known to the hearers. What is put in riddles we will make clear from elsewhere, not from other people's assumptions, but from Plato's most genuine writings. And of the things the hearers meet at once, we will consider how they agree with the things themselves. From all this, one complete form of Plato's theology will appear to us: its truth, which runs through all of divine thought; the one intellect that gave birth to all the beauty of this theology; and the sacred unfolding of this vision. Such, as I have said, will be the present work.

@p
The hearer of these teachings is supposed to be adorned with the moral virtues. He should have bound, by the rule of virtue, every mean and unharmonious motion of the soul, and tuned them all to the single form of the practical wisdom that belongs to intellect. For, as Socrates says, it is not lawful for the impure to touch the pure. Every bad person is wholly impure, and the opposite kind of person is pure. He should also have been trained in all the methods of logic, and have studied many sound ways of analysis and many of division, and their opposites, in keeping, I think, with Parmenides' advice to Socrates. For until one has gone through such a contest of arguments, the knowledge of the divine kinds, and of the truth set in them, is hard to reach and hard to enter. Third, he must not be untrained in the study of nature. Whoever has become familiar with the many opinions of the natural philosophers, and has explored in a way, in images, the causes of the things that are, will more easily go on to the nature of the separate and first beings. So, as I have said, the hearer of this work must not be ignorant of the truth found in appearances, nor a stranger to the paths of learning and the mathematical studies they contain, for through these we gain a less bodily knowledge of divine being. But all of these must be bound together under intellect, which leads. He must also share in Plato's dialectic, and practise the immaterial activities that are separate from bodily powers. He must long to contemplate true beings by intellectual insight joined with reason. Then let him give himself wholly to explaining the divine and blessed teachings, and fill his soul, as the Oracle says, with deep love. For, as Plato says somewhere, to grasp this vision no better helper than love can be found.
!flag intelligence in conjunction with reason [true] beings | Taylor adds "true" in brackets, and his footnote corrects the Greek he translated (from "intelligence with reason" as a subject to "by intelligence with reason"). The phrase is the Timaeus' (28a): what always is, is grasped "by intellectual insight together with a rational account".
!note "It is not lawful for the impure to touch the pure" is Socrates in the Phaedo (67b). Parmenides advises the young Socrates to train himself in the method of hypothesis before he speaks of the Forms (Parmenides 135c-136c). The Oracle is one of the Chaldean Oracles, which bid the soul fill itself with "deep love". Plato says that for this possession human nature could find no better helper than love in the Symposium (212b).

@p
He must also be trained in the truth that runs through all things, and wake his intelligible eye, the eye of intellect, to what is real and complete truth. He must settle himself in a firm, unshaken and safe kind of divine knowledge. He must be persuaded to admire nothing else, and not even to turn his attention to other things, but to hurry towards divine light with a fearless activity of reasoning and with the power of a tireless life. In short, he must set before himself the kind of activity and rest proper to one who means to be such a leader of the choir as Socrates describes in the Theaetetus. Such, then, is the size of our task, and such the method of our discourses on it. But before I begin to set out what I have proposed, I want to speak about theology itself, its different kinds, and which forms of theology Plato approves and which he rejects. Once these are known, we will more easily learn, in what follows, what supports the demonstrations themselves.
!note The "leader of the choir" (coryphaeus) is the true philosopher of the Theaetetus' digression (173c-174a), who does not know the way to the market place, but whose mind ranges over the whole of nature.

@ch I.3 | Who a theologian is, and how the gods are known
Everyone who has spoken of theology calls the first principles "gods", but they disagree on what is first: bodies, soul or intellect. Plato alone goes beyond intellect to a principle above it, the One, from which the henads proceed. The theologian knows the gods not by sense, opinion, reasoning or even intellect, but by the "one" of the soul, its highest point, which is joined to the henads. Turning inward, the soul finds first itself, then intellect, then the gods.

@p
Everyone who has ever touched on theology has called the things that are first by nature "gods", and has said that the science of theology is about them. Some have held that bodily being is the only thing that exists at all. They have put every kind of bodiless nature second to it in being, taken the principles of things to be bodily in form, and shown that the state in us by which we know these principles is bodily too. Others make all bodies depend on bodiless natures, and place the first hyparxis in soul and its powers. These, I think, call the best of souls "gods", and they call the science that reaches as far as these souls, and knows them, "theology". Still others bring the many souls out of another, older principle, and set intellect as the leader of all things. These say that the best end is the union of the soul with intellect, and they think the life of intellect the most honourable of all things. No doubt they also take theology and the study of intellectual being to be one and the same. All these, then, as I have said, call the first and most self-sufficient principles of things "gods", and the science of them "theology".
!note Taylor's footnote: hyparxis is "the summit of any nature, or blossom, as it were, of its being", its first and highest mode of existence. The Workshop keeps the word.

@p
But Plato's account of the divine, and his alone, rejects every bodily nature as a principle. Everything that can be divided and has extension cannot by nature make or keep itself in being. It has its being, its activity and its being acted on through soul and the motions soul contains. Plato shows that the being of soul is older than bodies, but depends in turn on an intellectual reality. For whatever is moved in time, even if it moves itself, is of a more ruling nature than what is moved by another, but comes after an eternal motion. So he shows, as we have said, that intellect is the father and cause of bodies and souls, and that everything whose life is spent in changes and unfoldings both exists and acts around intellect.
!note Plato shows that soul, the self-moving motion, is older than bodies in the Laws (X 896a-c) and the Phaedrus (245c-e), and that soul depends on intellect in the Timaeus (30b) and the Philebus (30c).

@p
Plato, however, goes on to another principle, wholly set apart from intellect, more bodiless and more inexpressible, from which all things, even the very last, must have their existence. For not all things are by nature fit to share in soul, but only those that have some life in them, clearer or dimmer. Nor can all things enjoy intellect and being, but only those that exist according to Form. But the principle of all things must be shared by all things, if it is to leave nothing out, since it is the cause of everything that in any way is said to be. Plato found by divine insight this first principle of all things, which is higher than intellect and hidden in places no one can reach. He showed three causes, or monads, and showed that they are above bodies: soul, the first intellect, and a union above intellect. From these, as from monads, he brings out their own numbers: one multitude made of unities, a second made of intellects, and a third made of souls. For every monad leads a multitude of its own rank. And as Plato joins bodies to souls, so he joins souls to intellectual Forms, and these again to the henads of beings. But he turns all things back to one unshared unity. Once he has run back as far as this unity, he holds that he has reached the highest end of the study of all things, and that this is the truth about the gods: the truth that is about the henads of beings, and teaches their processions and their distinctive characters, how beings are in contact with them, and the orders of Forms that depend on these unitary realities.
!flag one multitude indeed being uniform | Taylor's "uniform" (Greek henoeides, "one-like") does not mean "all the same": it means having the form of the One, made of unities. The first multitude is that of the henads, the gods.
!note Taylor's two footnotes on this paragraph: "unical" means "characterized by unity", and "unity" here (the henads) means "of the nature of the One". The Workshop renders Taylor's "unities" as "henads", and his "unical" as "unitary".

@p
Plato teaches us that the study of intellect, and of the Forms and kinds that turn about intellect, comes after the science of the gods themselves. The study of intellect grasps the intelligibles, and the Forms that the soul can know through the outward-reaching activity of intellect. But the science of theology goes beyond this. It is about the secret and inexpressible hyparxeis of the gods, and it traces how they are set apart from each other and how they come to light from the one cause of all. So I believe that the intellectual part of the soul can grasp the intellectual Forms and the difference among them. But the summit of intellect, its "flower", as they say, and its hyparxis, is joined to the henads of beings, and through these to the hidden union of all the divine henads. For though we have many powers of knowing, it is through this alone that we are by nature able to be joined to that hidden union and to share in it. The race of the gods cannot be grasped by sense, since it is set above all bodies. Nor by opinion and discursive reason, for these are divided and deal with things of many forms. Nor by intellectual insight joined with reason, for that kind of knowledge belongs to true beings. But the hyparxis of the gods rides upon beings, and is marked out by the very union of all things. So it follows, if the divine nature can be known in any way at all, that it must be grasped by the hyparxis of the soul, and known through it as far as possible. For we say that everywhere like is known by like: the perceptible by sense, what is opined by opinion, what is reasoned by discursive reason, and the intelligible by intellect. So the most unitary nature must be known by the one in us, and the inexpressible by what is inexpressible in us.
!note Taylor's footnotes: dianoia, here "discursive reason", is "the discursive activity of reason, or the power of the soul that reasons scientifically"; "the doxastic" is the object of opinion. "The flower of intellect" is the Chaldean Oracles' phrase. The hyparxis of the gods "rides upon" beings as the gods in the Phaedrus (247b-c) ride in their chariots on the back of the heaven.

@p
Socrates rightly says in the First Alcibiades that the soul, when it enters into itself, will see all other things, and god himself. For as it bends towards its own unity, the centre of all life, and lays aside multitude and the variety of the many powers it holds, it climbs to the highest look-out over all beings. In the holiest of the mysteries, they say, the initiates first meet the many kinds of many shapes that are hurled out in front of the gods. But when they enter the inside of the temple, unmoved and guarded by the rites, they truly receive divine light into themselves, and, stripped of their clothes, as they would say, they share in the divine nature. The same thing, I think, happens in the vision of all things. When the soul looks at what comes after it, it sees the shadows and images of beings. But when it turns back to itself, it unfolds its own being and the reason-forms it holds. At first it sees, as it were, only itself. But when it goes deeper into the knowledge of itself, it finds in itself both intellect and the orders of beings. And when it goes into its inmost part, the inner shrine, as it were, of the soul, it sees, with its eyes closed, the race of the gods and the henads of beings. For all things are in us in the manner of soul, and so we are by nature able to know all things, by waking the powers and images of the whole that we hold.
!flag the multiform, and many-shaped genera, which are hurled forth before the Gods | Taylor's footnote says these are "evil daemons". They are the frightening apparitions that, in the rites, appear before the god appears; the modern version keeps Proclus' image.
!note The First Alcibiades (132c-133c): the soul that looks into the best part of itself, where wisdom is, will know god and itself. "With its eyes closed" recalls the initiate (mystes) of the mysteries, whose name Proclus' readers took from "closing" (myein) the eyes.

@p
And this is the best use of our activity: to reach out to the divine nature itself, with our powers at rest; to circle around it in harmony; to wake all the multitude of the soul to this union; and, laying aside everything that comes after the One, to settle ourselves in, and be joined to, what is inexpressible and beyond all things. For the soul may climb until it ends its flight in the principle of things. When it has arrived there and seen the place there, it may come down from it and make its way through beings, unfolding the many Forms, searching out their monads and their numbers, and grasping by intellect how each depends on its own henad. Then we may hold that the soul has the most perfect science of divine natures, seeing as one the processions of the gods into beings, and the distinctions of beings around the gods. Such, then, by Plato's judgement, is our theologian. And theology is a state of this kind: it unfolds the very hyparxis of the gods; it sets apart and contemplates their unknowable and unitary light, distinct from the character of the things that share in them; and it makes it known to those worthy of this activity, which is blessed and holds all things together at once.
!note "The place there" is the place beyond the heaven of the Phaedrus (247c), which the gods and the best souls see.

@ch I.4 | The four ways Plato teaches about the gods
Plato speaks of the gods in four ways: inspired (as in the Phaedrus), dialectically (the Sophist and the Parmenides), in symbols and myths (the Gorgias, Symposium and Protagoras), and through images, mathematical or ethical (the Timaeus, the Statesman, the Republic). These answer to four traditions: the inspired initiators, the Orphic myths, Pythagorean mathematics, and Plato's own science. Plato keeps only myths that are worthy of the gods, and he does not reduce them to physics.

@p
After this complete account of the first part of our inquiry, we must set out the ways in which Plato teaches us the sacred ideas about divine natures. For he does not seem to have taught about them in the same way everywhere. Sometimes he unfolds the truth about them by an activity inspired by the gods, and at other times dialectically. Sometimes he announces their inexpressible characters in symbols, and at other times he goes back up to them from images and finds in them the first causes of all things. In the Phaedrus, inspired by the Nymphs, and having exchanged human understanding for something better, divine madness, he reveals with an inspired voice many secret teachings about the intellectual gods, and many about the liberated rulers of the universe, who lead the many gods of the world upward to the monads that are intelligible and separate from the wholes of the world. And speaking still more of the gods to whom the world is allotted, he praises their acts of thinking, their making of the world, their pure providence and their governing of souls, and everything else that Socrates says in that dialogue in an inspired way, as he plainly says himself, laying this madness to the gods of the place.
!flag the liberated rulers of the universe | "Liberated" (Greek apolytoi, "unbound") is Proclus' name for an order of gods between the gods above the world and those in it: they rule the world but are not bound to it. The Workshop keeps Taylor's word.
!note In the Phaedrus Socrates says he is "possessed by the Nymphs" (238c-d, 241e) and blames "the gods of the place" (262d); his second speech tells of the gods' procession round the heaven (246e-247c).

@p
In the Sophist, arguing dialectically about being and about how the One is set apart from beings, and raising difficulties against those older than himself, he shows how all beings depend on their cause and on the first being. Being itself shares in the unity that is set above all things. It is a one that has unity as something it undergoes, but not the One itself: it is subject to the One and united to it, but it is not what is first of all one. In the same way, in the Parmenides, he unfolds dialectically, through the first hypotheses, the processions of being from the One and the One's transcendence, and does so, as he says in that dialogue, by the most perfect division of this method. Again, in the Gorgias he tells the myth of the three Craftsmen and of their allotment as makers of the world. This is not only a myth but a true account. In the Symposium he speaks of the union brought by Love. And in the Protagoras he speaks of how the mortal animals were given their shares by the gods. In these he hides the truth about divine natures in symbols, and unfolds his mind only so far as to hint at it to the most genuine of his hearers.
!flag that it is a passive one | Taylor's "passive one" renders the Sophist's phrase (245a): being "has unity as an affection", has the one as something that happens to it. It does not mean that being is inactive; the modern version says what is meant.
!note The Sophist (244b-245e) argues against those before Plato who said that all is one. The "three Craftsmen" of the Gorgias (523a) are Zeus, Poseidon and Pluto (Hades), who divide the realm of Kronos, as in the Iliad (15.187-193). Aristophanes' speech in the Symposium (189c-193d) and Diotima's (201d-212a) speak of love that unites; the Protagoras' myth (320c-322a) tells how Epimetheus and Prometheus gave the animals their powers.

@p
Then there is the teaching given through the mathematical studies, and the treatment of divine things drawn from ethical or physical discussions, of which much can be found in the Timaeus, much in the dialogue called the Statesman, and much scattered in other dialogues. If you like, I will mention these too. Here also, for those of you who want to know divine things through images, the method will be plain. For all these give a shadowy picture of the powers of the divine. The Statesman, for example, pictures the making of the world in the heavens. The shapes of the five elements, given in geometrical proportions in the Timaeus, show in images the distinctive characters of the gods who ride on the parts of the universe. And the divisions of the being of soul in that dialogue give a shadowy picture of the whole orders of the gods.
!note The Statesman's myth of the world's two motions (269c-274e); the five regular solids of the Timaeus (53c-56c); the division of the world-soul by numbers (35a-36d). Taylor's footnote: the words "in the Timaeus" are missing from the Greek he translated.

@p
I pass over the fact that Plato composes constitutions, likening them to divine natures and to the whole world, and orders them from the powers the world holds. Through the likeness of mortal things to divine ones, all of these show us in images the processions, orders and works of making of the divine natures. These, then, are the ways of teaching about the gods that Plato uses.
!note The constitutions are those of the Republic and the Laws; the Republic's city is "a pattern laid up in heaven" (592b).

@p
It is clear from what has been said that there must be just this many of them. Those who treat divine things by hints either speak in symbols and myths, or through images. Of those who state their thoughts openly, some build their accounts by science, and others by inspiration from the gods. Whoever wants to point to divine things through symbols is Orphic, and in short agrees with those who write myths about the gods. Whoever does it through images is Pythagorean. For the Pythagoreans invented the mathematical studies to help recollect divine things, which they try to reach through these, as through images. According to the witness of their historians, they refer both numbers and shapes to the gods. The inspired person, who unfolds the truth about the gods by itself under divine inspiration, stands most clearly among the highest of the initiators. These do not think it right to show the divine orders or their characters to their companions through veils, but announce the gods' powers and numbers because the gods themselves move them. But to hand down divine things by science is the glorious distinction of Plato's philosophy. For Plato alone, I think, of all those known to us, has tried to divide by method, and set in order, the regular procession of the divine kinds, how they differ from each other, the characters common to the whole orders, and the characters given out to each one. The truth of this will be clear when we give the proper demonstrations about the Parmenides and all the divisions it contains.
!flag when we frame precedaneous demonstrations | Taylor's "precedaneous" (an old word for "going first, principal") means the demonstrations that belong first and properly to the subject: the account of the Parmenides, which is the core of the work.

@p
For now we will note that Plato does not accept all the inventions of poetry and drama, but only those that point to the beautiful and the good and do not clash with divine being. The way of myth, which points to divine things by guesswork, is ancient. It hides the truth under many veils, and works much as nature does, which holds out perceptible inventions in place of intelligible things, material in place of immaterial, divisible in place of indivisible, and images, things that have a false being, in place of what is wholly true. But Plato rejects the more tragic way of making myths used by the old poets. They wanted to set up a secret theology about the gods, and for this they invented wanderings, cuttings, battles, tearings apart, rapes and adulteries of the gods, and many other symbols of the truth about the divine that this theology hides. This way Plato rejects, and he says that it is in every way most foreign to education. But he holds that those myths about the gods are more convincing, and closer to the truth and to the philosophic temper, which say that the divine is the cause of all good and of no evil, that it does not change at all, always keeping its own order unchanged, and that it holds in itself the source of truth but is never the cause of any deceit for others. Such are the patterns of theology that Socrates gives in the Republic.
!note Republic II-III (377b-392c): Socrates rejects Hesiod's and Homer's stories of Kronos' deeds and the gods' battles, and lays down the "patterns of theology" (379a): god is the cause only of good things (379b-c), does not change (380d-381e), and does not deceive (382a-383a).

@p
So all of Plato's myths, while keeping the truth hidden, do not have even an outward dress that clashes with our untaught and unspoiled sense of the gods. They carry an image of the way the world is put together, in which the visible beauty is worthy of the divine, and a beauty more divine than this is set in the unseen lives and powers of the gods. This, then, is one of the ways of myth about divine things: it passes from what seems lawless, irrational and disordered into order and limit, and aims at putting together the beautiful and the good.

@p
But there is another way, which he teaches in the Phaedrus. It consists in always keeping the myths of theology unmixed with accounts of nature, and in taking care never to confuse theology and the study of nature with each other, or to swap one for the other. For as divine being is separate from the whole of nature, it is right that accounts of the gods should also be clean of physical explanations. Such a mixing, he says, is laborious, and to make the things that happen in nature the meaning of a myth is the work of no very good man: for example, someone who, by his supposed wisdom, takes the Chimaera, the Gorgon and the like to be natural events. Socrates in the Phaedrus disapproves of this way of handling myths, and makes its followers say, in a story, that Orithyia playing with the wind Boreas and thrown down the rocks means nothing more than that Orithyia, a mortal girl, was carried off by Boreas out of love. For it seems to me that the hidden meaning of myths about the gods should always be holier than the apparent one. So if anyone gives us physical interpretations of Plato's myths, about the things below the moon, we must say that they wholly miss the philosopher's aim. Only those interpretations truly explain the truth these myths hold which aim at a divine, immaterial and separate reality, and which, looking to it, build and take apart the myths in keeping with our inborn sense of divine things.
!note Phaedrus 229c-230a: Socrates will not explain away the myth of Boreas and Orithyia as a girl blown off the rocks by the north wind, since then one would have to explain Centaurs, the Chimaera, Gorgons and Pegasus too, "a laborious task, and not for a very lucky man". Taylor's footnote corrects a word of the Greek.

@ch I.5 | The dialogues that hold Plato's theology, and their orders of gods
Plato's teaching about the gods runs through every dialogue, but most fully through the Phaedo, Phaedrus, Symposium, Philebus, Sophist, Statesman, Cratylus and Timaeus; then the myths of the Gorgias and Protagoras, the Laws on providence, Republic X; then the Letters. Proclus names the orders of gods each one teaches. All Greek theology comes from Orpheus, through Pythagoras to Plato, and Plato agrees with the theologians wherever he speaks of the gods.

@p
Now that we have counted all these ways of Plato's theology, and shown what building and taking apart of myths fits the truth about the gods, let us next consider where, and from which dialogues above all, we think Plato's teachings about the gods can be gathered, and by looking for which patterns or forms we can tell his genuine writings from the false works that are put down to him.

@p
The truth about the gods runs, one may say, through all of Plato's dialogues. In all of them thoughts of the first philosophy are sown, holy, clear and above nature: in some more dimly, in others more plainly. These thoughts stir those who can in any way share in them towards the immaterial and separate being of the gods. In each part of the universe, and in nature herself, the Craftsman of all that the world holds set up likenesses of the unknowable hyparxis of the gods, so that all things might turn back to the divine nature through their kinship with it. In the same way, I believe, Plato's divine intellect weaves thoughts about the gods into all his writings, and leaves nothing without mention of the divine, so that from the whole of them a recollection of all things may be gained and given to the genuine lovers of divine things.

@p
But if I must set before the reader, out of many, the dialogues that above all unfold for us the sacred teaching about the gods, I would not be wrong to count among them the Phaedo and the Phaedrus, the Symposium and the Philebus, and with these the Sophist and the Statesman, the Cratylus and the Timaeus. All these are full, through and through, one may say, of Plato's divine science. In the second rank after these I would put the myth in the Gorgias and the one in the Protagoras; also what is said in the Laws about the gods' providence, and what is taught about the Fates, or the mother of the Fates, or the turnings of the universe, in the tenth book of the Republic. In the third rank you may, if you like, put those Letters through which we can reach the science of divine natures. For in them the three kings are mentioned, and very many other divine teachings worthy of Plato's vision are given. So we must look to these, and search out in them each order of the gods.
!note The Laws on providence: Book X (899d-905d). The Fates and their mother, Necessity, and the turning spindle of the universe: the myth of Er, Republic X (616b-617d). The "three kings" are in the Second Letter (312e): "all things are about the king of all ... the second about the second, and the third about the third".

@p
So from the Philebus we can receive the science of the one Good and of the two first principles of things, together with the triad that comes to light from them. You will find all of these set out clearly by Plato in that dialogue. From the Timaeus you can gain the study of the intelligibles, a divine account of the monad of the Craftsman, and the fullest truth about the gods of the world. From the Phaedrus, a scientific knowledge of all the intelligible and intellectual kinds, and of the liberated orders of gods, which are set just above the circuits of the heavens. From the Statesman, the study of the making of the world in the heavens, of the uneven periods of the universe, and of the intellectual causes of those periods. From the Sophist, the whole of becoming below the moon, and the distinctive character of the gods to whom the region below the moon is allotted, who preside over its comings to be and passings away. And about each of the gods we can find many ideas fit for sacred things in the Symposium, many in the Cratylus, and many in the Phaedo. For in each of these dialogues the divine names are mentioned, more or less, and from these it is easy for those trained in divine things to find out, by reasoning, the distinctive character of each.

!note The Phaedrus' place "above the heaven" and the gods' procession (246e-247e); the Statesman's two periods of the universe (269c-270a).

@p
But we must show that each teaching agrees with Plato's principles and with the sacred traditions of the theologians. For all Greek theology is the offspring of the sacred tradition of Orpheus. Pythagoras first learnt the rites of the gods from Aglaophemus, and Plato, second, received a complete science of the gods from the writings of the Pythagoreans and the Orphics. For in the Philebus, referring the study of the two kinds of principles, limit and the unlimited, to the Pythagoreans, he calls them men who live with the gods and are truly blessed. Philolaus the Pythagorean left us in writing many wonderful thoughts about these principles, praising their common procession into beings and the separate work of making that each performs. In the Timaeus, when Plato means to teach us about the gods below the moon and their order, he turns to the theologians, calls them the sons of the gods, and makes them the fathers of the truth about these gods. And at last he gives the orders of the gods below the moon as coming from the wholes, following the procession of the intellectual kings that they teach. Again, in the Cratylus he follows the theologians' traditions about the order of the divine processions. In the Gorgias he takes up Homer's teaching about the three Craftsmen. In short, wherever he speaks of the gods, he speaks in keeping with the theologians' principles. He rejects the tragic side of mythical invention, but he lays down the first principles in common with the makers of myths.

@ch I.6 | An objection: theology gathered from many dialogues is patchwork
Someone may object that a theology pieced together from many dialogues is not Plato's, has no whole, and cannot show how the orders of gods proceed from the one principle. Besides, the myths and divine passages in Plato are there for the sake of ethics or physics, not for their own sake. So such a theology is borrowed and forced, not genuine.

@p
But perhaps someone will object here that we are not presenting Plato's theology properly, scattered as it is everywhere, and that we are trying to heap together different pieces from different dialogues, as if we were eager to collect many things into one mixture, instead of drawing them all from one and the same source. If that were so, we could refer different teachings to different works of Plato, but we would by no means have a proper, primary teaching about the gods. Nor would there be any dialogue that gives us the complete and whole procession of the divine kinds and how they are ranked with each other. We would be like those who try to get a whole from parts, for want of a whole that is prior to the parts, and to weave the perfect together out of imperfect things, when the imperfect ought, on the contrary, to have the first cause of its coming to be in the perfect. The Timaeus, for example, will teach us the study of the intelligible kinds, and the Phaedrus seems to give us an orderly account of the first intellectual orders. But where will we find how the intellectual is ranked with the intelligible? And how the second comes to be from the first? In short, we will not be able to show how the procession of the divine orders comes about from the one principle of all things, and how, in the generations of the gods, the orders between the One and the complete number are filled in.

@p
Further, the objector may say, where will be the holiness of your much-praised science of divine natures? It is absurd to call these teachings Plato's when they are gathered from many places and, as you admit, brought into Plato's philosophy under names foreign to it; nor can you show one whole and complete truth about divine natures. Perhaps, they will say, certain people younger than Plato have written and left to their pupils one complete form of theology. You, then, can bring out one whole account of nature from the Timaeus, and from the Republic or the Laws the finest teachings about character, which tend to one form of philosophy. Only the work of Plato that holds all the good of the first philosophy, and may be called the summit of the whole study, you leave aside, and so you will lack the most perfect knowledge of beings, unless you are so foolish as to boast of mythical inventions, though the explaining of such things is full of the probable and empty of the demonstrative. Besides, such things are only brought into Plato's dialogues by the way. The myth in the Protagoras, for instance, is put in for the sake of the art of politics and the demonstrations about it. In the same way the myth in the Republic is put in for the sake of justice, and the one in the Gorgias for the sake of self-control. For Plato joins mythical stories to inquiries into ethical teachings not for the sake of the myths, but for the sake of the main aim: so that we may not only train the intellectual part of the soul through contending arguments, but so that the divine part of the soul may receive the knowledge of beings more perfectly, through its sympathy with more sacred things. For from other kinds of discourse we seem like people forced to accept the truth; but from myths we are moved in an inexpressible way, and we call up our unspoiled ideas, revering the sacred teaching they hold.

@p
So, I think, Timaeus rightly thinks it fitting that we should bring forth the divine kinds by following the makers of myths, as sons of the gods, and accept that they always generate the second natures from the first, even if they speak without demonstration. For this kind of discourse is not demonstrative but inspired, and the ancients invented it not from necessity but for the sake of persuasion, aiming not at mere teaching but at sympathy with the things themselves. And if you are willing to look at the causes not only of the myths but of the other teachings of theology, you will find that some are scattered in Plato's dialogues for the sake of ethical questions, and others for the sake of physical ones. In the Philebus, Plato speaks of limit and the unlimited for the sake of pleasure and of the life lived by intellect. For I think the second are kinds of the first. In the Timaeus the account of the intelligible gods is taken up for the sake of the study of nature that is proposed. So everywhere images must be known from their patterns, and the patterns of material things must be immaterial, of perceptible things intelligible, and the patterns of natural forms must be separate.

@p
Again, in the Phaedrus, Plato praises the place above the heaven, the depth below the heaven, and every kind beneath this, for the sake of the madness of love, and of how the souls' recollection comes about and their passage there from here. Everywhere, one may say, the main aim is either physical or political, and the thoughts about divine natures come in either for the sake of discovery or of perfection. How, then, can a study like yours still be holy and above nature, and worth studying above everything, when it can show neither the whole in itself, nor the perfect, nor what comes first in Plato's writings, but lacks all these, and is forced, not natural, and has an order that is not its own but brought in from outside, as in a play? Such are the objections that may be raised against our plan.

@ch I.7 | The answer: the whole of Plato's theology is in the Parmenides
Plato speaks of the gods in keeping with tradition and with the nature of things, sometimes as a means, sometimes as his main aim. But there is one dialogue that holds the whole of theology: the Parmenides. In it all the divine kinds proceed in order from the first cause, the highest simplest and most hidden, the last most many. The other dialogues give parts of the teaching, and must be referred to it, as the Timaeus holds the whole science of nature.

@p
To an objection of this kind I will give a fair and clear answer. I say that Plato everywhere speaks of the gods in keeping with ancient report and with the nature of things. Sometimes, for the sake of the cause of the things in hand, he carries them back to the principles of the teachings, and from there, as from a look-out, studies the nature of what is in hand. But sometimes he sets the science of theology as his main aim. In the Phaedrus his subject is intelligible beauty, and how beauty, shared from there, runs through all things; and in the Symposium it is the order of love.
!note Phaedrus 249d-250e: the beauty seen above the heaven, and its image here; Symposium 210a-212a: the ascent to the beautiful itself.

@p
But if we must survey, in one dialogue of Plato's, the whole of theology, complete and connected, reaching to its full number, I may perhaps say something surprising, which only those close to us will find plain. Still, we must be bold, since we have begun on such arguments, and say against our opponents that the Parmenides, and the sacred ideas it holds, will give you all you want. For in this dialogue all the divine kinds proceed in order from the first cause and show how they are linked to and depend on each other. The highest, those akin to the One and of a first nature, are given a unitary, hidden and simple form of hyparxis. The last are multiplied, divided into many parts, and rich in number, but weaker in power than those of a higher order. The middle ones, in fitting proportion, are more composite than their causes but simpler than their own offspring. In short, all the axioms of the science of theology appear complete in this dialogue, and all the divine orders are shown linked together. So this is nothing other than the famous generation of the gods, and the procession of every kind of being from the inexpressible and unknowable cause of all things. The Parmenides, then, kindles in the lovers of Plato the whole and perfect light of the science of theology. After it, the dialogues mentioned before give out parts of the sacred teaching about the gods. All of them, one may say, share in divine wisdom and wake our own ideas about the divine nature. All the parts of this sacred teaching must be referred to these dialogues, and these again to the one complete study of the Parmenides. In this way, I think, we will make the more imperfect depend on the perfect, and the parts on the wholes, and we will show accounts made like the things they explain, as Plato's Timaeus says. Such, then, is our answer to the objection that may be raised against us. And so we refer Plato's teaching on the gods to the Parmenides, just as everyone with the least understanding agrees that the Timaeus holds the whole science of nature.

!note The Timaeus (29b): accounts are akin to the things they explain. The school read the hypotheses of the Parmenides' second part (137c-166c) as an account of all the orders of reality from the One down; the next chapters set out this reading.

@ch I.8 | Two kinds of opponents on the Parmenides
Proclus faces two groups. One holds that the Parmenides is only an exercise in arguing both sides. The other, more serious, reads its first hypotheses as about the first God, intellect, and what comes after, but will not divide them further into the many orders of gods. Against both he will set out the reading of his teacher, Syrianus.

@p
By this, however, I seem to have stirred up for myself a contest on two fronts against those who try to study Plato's writings. I see two kinds of people who will oppose what has been said. The first thinks it right to look for no other aim in the Parmenides than practice in arguing both sides, and not to bring into this dialogue a crowd of secret and intellectual teachings foreign to its purpose. The second kind are more serious than these, and lovers of the Forms. They say that one of the hypotheses is about the first God, another about the second God and the whole of intellectual nature, and a third about the natures after this, whether they are the higher kinds, or souls, or some other kind of beings. For to inquire into these details does not belong to the present discussion.
!note The first group follows Parmenides' own words (135c-136c), that what follows is "an exercise". The second is the reading of Plotinus (V.1.8) and his school: the first hypothesis is about the One, the second about intellect, the third about soul. "The higher kinds" are the angels, daemons and heroes, the kinds above human souls.

@p
These, then, divide three of the hypotheses in this way. But they do not think it right to concern themselves with the many gods, the intelligible and intellectual kinds, the natures above the world and in the world, or to unfold all these by division and search them out with care. For according to them, though Plato speaks of intellectual beings in the second hypothesis, the nature of intellect is one, simple and undivided. Whoever holds the view of the Parmenides that we have stated must contend against both. But the contest is not equal against both. Those who make the Parmenides an exercise in logic are themselves attacked by those who hold the divine way of reading it. And those who do not unfold the many beings and the orders of divine natures are, as Homer says, in every way men to be revered and skilled. But for the sake of Plato's philosophy we must raise difficulties against them too, following in this our leader to the most holy and sacred truth. It is right also to set out, so far as it helps our aim, what we take to be the truth about the hypotheses of the Parmenides. For in this way, perhaps, by a process of reasoning, we may take in the whole of Plato's theology.
!note "To be revered and skilled" (or "awesome"): Helen's words to Priam in the Iliad (3.172), which Socrates uses of Parmenides in the Theaetetus (183e). "Our leader" is Syrianus.

@ch I.9 | Against reading the Parmenides as an exercise in logic
Proclus answers those who make the Parmenides a mere exercise in arguing both sides. Parmenides himself calls the method a serious labour; his subject is the One and being, which mere opinion cannot reach; Plato gives every speaker a subject fitted to him; Socrates praises Parmenides' depth in the Theaetetus and the Sophist; and dialectic, as the Republic describes it, climbs to the Good itself. So the dialogue is about the first principles of things.

@p
First, then, let us look at those who drag the aim of this dialogue down from the truth of things to an exercise in logic, and see whether they can possibly agree with Plato's writings. It is clear to everyone that Parmenides means to hand on the dialectical method in earnest. With this in view he runs through it in the same way for each of the things that really are, such as sameness, difference, likeness, unlikeness, motion and remaining at rest, and he urges those who want to find the nature of each in an orderly way to take up this exercise as a great contest. He also says that it was no easy task for a man of his age. He likens himself to the horse of Ibycus, and gives every sign that this method is serious work and not a contest of mere words. How, then, can we reduce to empty arguments the ideas about which the great Parmenides composed this discourse, showing that they need serious discussion? And how is it reasonable to suppose that an old man would busy himself with contests of words, and that a lover of the truth of things would spend so much study on this method: he who thought that nothing else really is, and who had climbed to the high look-out of being itself? Whoever accepts this must suppose that Plato is mocking Parmenides in this dialogue, showing him dragged down from the soul's most intellectual visions to the contests of the young.
!flag assimilates himself to the Ibycean horse | In the Parmenides (137a) the old Parmenides compares himself to the ageing racehorse in a poem of Ibycus, who trembles before the race because he knows what is coming; so Parmenides fears to swim "so great a sea of words".
!note Parmenides 135c-137b: the method of supposing that each thing is and is not, for sameness, likeness, motion and the rest, which Parmenides calls "a great labour". Taylor's footnote corrects a word of the Greek he translated.

@p
If you like, let us also consider what Parmenides promises, and on what subject he began this discussion. Was it not about being, as he taught it, and about the unity of all beings? He reached out to this, though his aim was hidden from the many, and he urges us to gather the many beings into one undivided union. If this is the one being, the highest thing, set wholly above the reasonings that belong to opinion, is it not absurd to mix teachings about intelligibles with arguments from opinion? Such a form of discourse does not suit the subject of true beings, and thinking the unseen and separate causes does not go with dialectical exercises. These differ from each other as far as intellect stands above opinion, as Timaeus tells us; and not Timaeus only, but also the daemonic Aristotle, who, speaking of a power of this kind, urges us to make our inquiries neither about things wholly hidden from us nor about things already very well known.
!flag the dæmoniacal Aristotle | Taylor's "dæmoniacal" means "more than human, inspired": a title of honour, not of reproach. The power Aristotle speaks of is dialectic, the art of arguing from accepted opinions (Topics I, 100a-101b).
!note The Timaeus (27d-28a, 51d-e) sets intellect, which grasps what always is, above opinion, which grasps what comes to be.

@p
So Parmenides, who puts the science of beings above what seems true to those who rank sense before intellect, is far from bringing knowledge by opinion to an intellectual nature, since that kind of knowledge is doubtful, varied and unsteady. Nor would he study true being with this wisdom of opinion and with empty discussion. A varied form of knowing does not fit what is simple, nor the many-formed what is one-like, nor opinion the intelligible.
!note Taylor's footnote corrects the Greek he translated (to "the form does not fit").

@p
Further, we must not leave out this point either: such a way of discussion is wholly foreign to Parmenides' subject. He speaks about all beings, and sets out the order of all things, their procession beginning from the One and their return ending in the One. But the method of arguing both sides is very far from the study that belongs to science. Would it not seem, then, that Plato has given Parmenides an unfitting subject, if it is said that he looks only to an exercise in opposing arguments, and stirs up this whole unrolling of reasonings for the sake of the skill the exercise trains? In fact we find that in all the other dialogues Plato gives each philosopher a subject suited to his own teaching. To Timaeus he gives the teaching about nature; to Socrates, that about the city; to the Stranger from Elea, that about being; and to the priestess Diotima, that about love. Then each of the other dialogues keeps to the arguments that suit the writings of the dialogue's main speaker. Parmenides alone would seem wise in his poems and in his careful search for true being, but on Plato's stage he would lead a childish Muse. So this view charges Plato with an unlike imitation, though he himself blames the poets for giving the sons of the gods a love of money and a life ruled by the passions. How, then, can we refer a discussion of opinions and empty arguments to the leader of the truth of beings?
!flag accuses Plato of dissimilitude of imitation | That is, of making Parmenides speak out of character, an imitation unlike its original: the fault Plato finds in the poets (Republic III 390e-391e, Achilles and the gifts).

@p
But if we must leave aside many arguments and call Plato himself as a witness to the subject of this discussion, let us cite what is written in the Theaetetus and the Sophist, for from these what we say will be clear. In the Theaetetus a young man urges Socrates to refute those who say that being is unmoved, and Socrates, coming to Parmenides' view among them, gives at the same time the reason for his care. "I am more ashamed before Parmenides," he says, "who is one of these, than before all the rest. For I met him when I was very young and he was very old, and he seemed to me to have a depth that was altogether noble. So I am afraid that we may not understand what was said, and much more afraid that we fall short of what Parmenides meant." So we are right to say that the discussion before us is not about an exercise in logic, nor does it make this the end of the whole, but that it belongs to the science of the first principles of things. For how could Socrates, if Parmenides had used a skill of this kind and neglected the knowledge of things, bear witness that his discourse had a depth that was altogether noble? And what dignity could there be in taking up a method that goes by opinion through opposing arguments, and in setting out to invent such arguments?
!note Theaetetus 183e-184a. Socrates' meeting with Parmenides is the scene of the Parmenides itself.

@p
Again, in the Sophist, Socrates urges the Stranger from Elea to unfold clearly what he has proposed, and shows that he was by now used to deeper discussions. "Tell me," he says, "is it your custom to go through a subject at length by yourself, when you are able to show it to someone by questions? I mean the kind of discussion Parmenides himself once used, with arguments of great beauty, which I heard when I was very young and he was very old." What reason can be given, then, for not believing Socrates when he says that Parmenides' arguments were of great beauty and had a noble depth? Why should we lower Parmenides' discussion, throw it down from being and what is, and turn it into a common, trifling and empty contest, without considering that discussions of that kind suit only the young, or regarding the subject of the being that has the character of the One, or anything else that tells against such an opinion?
!note Sophist 217c.

@p
I also think that the authors of this view should consider the power of dialectic as Socrates shows it in the Republic: how, as he says, it surrounds all the studies like a coping wall; how it lifts those who use it up to the Good itself and the first henads; how it cleans the eye of the soul, settles it in true beings and in the one principle of all things, and ends at last in what is no longer a hypothesis. If the power of this dialectic is so great, and the end of this road so mighty, we must not confuse arguments from opinion with a method of this kind. The first looks to the opinions of men, while the second is called idle talk by the many. The first has no share at all in the sciences of learning; the second is their coping wall, and the way to it lies through them. Again, reasoning by opinion has as its end what appears, while the dialectical method tries to reach the One itself, always using steps of ascent for this, and at last ends beautifully in the nature of the Good.

@p
So it is not right to drag down to arguments from opinion a method that has its place among the most exact sciences. The merely logical method, which governs imagination as it follows demonstrations, is of a second rank, and is pleased only with contentious debates. But our dialectic uses chiefly division and analysis as first sciences, imitating the procession of beings from the One and their return to it again. It also sometimes uses definitions and demonstrations, and before these the method of defining, and before that the method of dividing. The method of opinion, on the other hand, lacks the irrefutable reasonings of demonstration. Must not these powers, then, be kept apart from each other? And must not Parmenides' discussion, which uses our dialectic, be free of the empty variety of mere argument, and build its reasonings with a view to being itself and not to what appears? This is enough in answer to those who find fault with our view. For if all this cannot convince them, we would try in vain to persuade them and urge them on to the study of things.

@ch I.10 | Those who read the hypotheses as about the first principles, and what Syrianus adds
The harder contest is with those who rightly read the Parmenides as about first causes but do not divide its second hypothesis. The dialogue has nine hypotheses: five suppose that the One is, four that it is not. All agree that the first is about the principle beyond being. But Syrianus taught that the conclusions of the second must be divided among the orders of gods, first conclusions to first orders, middle to middle, last to last, since being is not one simple thing, and the order of the proofs mirrors the order of things.

@p
But a greater and harder contest remains for me, against those lovers of the study of beings who look to the science of the first causes as the end set before them in the hypotheses of Plato's Parmenides. If you like, we will carry out this contest with many and better-known arguments.

@p
First, we will mark out what our argument against them will be about, since this, I think, will make Plato's sacred teaching about divine natures as clear as it can be. There are nine hypotheses discussed by Parmenides in this dialogue, as we have shown in our commentary on it. The five that come first suppose that the One is, and through this supposition all beings, the middles of the whole and the ends of the processions of things, can be supposed to be. The four that follow bring in the One as not being, as the dialectical method directs. They show that if the One is taken away, all beings, and everything that appears to exist, must be wholly overturned, and they set out to refute this supposition. Some of the hypotheses plainly reach every conclusion by reason, but others (if I may put it so) show things more impossible than impossibilities. Some before us saw, I think, that this must happen in these hypotheses, and thought it worth discussing in their treatises on this dialogue.
!note Proclus' Commentary on the Parmenides survives in part (to the end of the first hypothesis); the Greek of it is on the Neoplatonists' shelf. Taylor's footnote corrects a word of the Greek he translated.

@p
As to the first of the hypotheses, almost everyone agrees that through it Plato praises the principle of all things that is beyond being, as inexpressible, unknowable and above all being. But not everyone explains the next hypothesis in the same way. The older Platonists, and those who shared in Plotinus' philosophy, say that an intellectual nature appears in this hypothesis, coming from the principle that is beyond being, and they try to fit the conclusions of this hypothesis to the one and complete power of intellect. But our leader to the truth about the gods, Plato's companion in talk (to use Homer's words), who brought what was unlimited in the theory of the older philosophers to limit, and turned the confusion of the different orders into an intellectual distinction in the writings he shared with his circle: this leader of ours, in his treatise on this subject, bids us divide the conclusions distinctly, carry this division over to the divine orders, and fit the first and simplest of what is shown to the first of beings; to suit those of the middle rank to the middle natures, by the order they have among beings; and the last and many-formed to the last processions. For the nature of being is not one, simple and undivided. Among perceptible things, the mighty heaven is one, yet holds in itself a multitude of bodies. The monad holds multitude together, but in the multitude there is an order of procession, and of perceptible things some are first, some middle and some last. And before these, among souls, from one soul there comes a multitude of souls, and some of these are set nearer to their whole and others further from it, while others fill in the middle between the extremes. In the same way, no doubt, among the wholly true beings, the one-like and hidden kinds must be set in the one and first cause of all things; others must proceed into all multitude and into a whole number; and others, in the middle, must hold the bond between them. Nor is it right to fit the characters of the first natures to the second, or those of a lower order to those more unitary. Among them some must have powers different from others, and there must be an order in this procession of true beings, and an unfolding of the second from the first.

!note "Our leader" is Syrianus, whose commentary on the Parmenides is lost; Proclus follows it here. The view of the "older Platonists" is Plotinus' (V.1.8): the second hypothesis is about intellect.

@p
In short, the being that has the character of the One proceeds from the unity prior to beings, but generates the whole divine kind: the intelligible, the intellectual, that above the world, and that which proceeds as far as the order in the world. Our teacher also says that each of the conclusions points to a divine character. And though all the conclusions fit all the processions of the one being, that is, of being with the character of the One, I think it is no wonder that some conclusions agree with some orders more than with others. For what expresses the character of certain orders does not necessarily belong to all the gods, while what belongs to all is surely present, all the more, in each. So if we credit Plato with a division of the divine orders brought in from outside, and cannot show clearly that in other dialogues he praises the processions of the gods from on high to the furthest end of things, sometimes in myths about the soul and at other times in other ways of theology, we would be wrong to credit him with such a division of being, and with it of the procession of the One. But if we can show from other dialogues that he has in some way praised all the kingdoms of the gods (as will be plain in the course of this work), is it not impossible that in the most sacred of all his works he should set out, in the first hypothesis, the One's transcendence over all the kinds of beings, over being itself, the being of soul, Form and matter, and yet make no mention of the divine processions and their orderly separation? If we should study only the last things, why do we touch on the first principle before the others? If we think it right to unfold the multitude of the hypotheses, why do we pass over the kind of the gods and the divisions it holds? And if we unfold the natures between the first and last of things, why do we leave unknown the whole orders of the divine beings that lie between the One and the natures that are made divine in any way? All this shows that such an account of the whole falls short of the science of divine things.
!note Taylor's footnote corrects the Greek he translated ("orders" for "actions").

@p
Further, Socrates in the Philebus calls on those who love the study of beings to use the method of division, and always to search out the monads of the whole orders, and the dyads, triads, or any other numbers that proceed from them. If this is rightly laid down, then the Parmenides, which uses the whole dialectical method and speaks about the being that has the character of the One, should neither look for multitude in the One, nor stay in the one monad of beings, nor, in short, bring the whole multitude of first beings straight up to the One above all beings. It should unfold, as in the first order, the beings that are hidden and akin to the One; as in the middle rank, those kinds of gods that are in procession, more divided than the most united, but given a more perfect union than those that have proceeded furthest; and as in the last rank, those that are by the last division of powers, and with them those whose being has been made divine. So if the first of the hypotheses is about the One above all multitude, the hypothesis that follows must not unfold being itself in an unlimited and indistinct way, but must give all the orders of beings. For the method of division does not allow us to bring the whole of multitude at once up to the One, as Socrates teaches us in the Philebus.
!note Philebus 16c-17a: we must look for one Form, then two or three or some other number, and not pass at once from the one to the unlimited.

@p
Besides, we can show the truth of what we say from the very method of the demonstrations. The first conclusions become plain at once from the fewest, simplest and best-known ideas, which are, as it were, common notions. Those next in order become plain through more, and more varied, ideas. And the last conclusions are the most composite of all. For he always uses the first conclusions to serve the proof of those that follow, and in the way he links these conclusions he gives us an intellectual pattern of the order kept in geometry and the other mathematical studies. So if accounts carry an image of the things they explain, and if the order of what is shown must be as the unfolding of the demonstrations is, it seems to me that what takes its start from the simplest principles must be in every way of a more primary nature, and set as joined to the One, while what is always multiplied, and depends on varied demonstrations, must have proceeded further from the existence of the One.
!note Taylor's two footnotes correct words of the Greek he translated.

@p
For demonstrations that have two conclusions must hold the conclusions that come before them. But those that hold first, self-evident and simple ideas are not necessarily united with the more composite ones, which are shown through more middle terms and are further from the principle of beings. So it appears that some conclusions point to more divine orders and others to lower ones; some to more united orders and others to more multiplied ones; some to more one-like processions and others to more many-formed ones. For demonstrations always proceed from causes and from what is first. If, then, the first conclusions are the causes of the second, there is an order of causes and effects in the multitude of the conclusions. To confuse all things and study them without distinction as one agrees neither with the nature of things nor with Plato's science.

@ch I.11 | Many proofs that the second hypothesis is divided by the divine orders
Grant that the second hypothesis is about true being: being is a multitude with union in it. If every conclusion fitted the whole only, or every part alike, absurdities follow; so different conclusions fit different orders, and in order from the first. The order of the hypotheses, Plotinus on numbers, Porphyry on what in intellect is prior to the eternal, and Iamblichus on the kinds of being all agree. Only this reading explains why there are fourteen conclusions, in this order, proved as they are, and why the hypothesis is so long.

@p
Let us, then, take up this question in another way, and see with discursive reason where anything empty is said. Let it be said, if you like, and we will grant it first, that the conclusions of this second hypothesis are about true being. But true being is a multitude, and not just one itself like the One before beings. For being has unity as something it undergoes, as the Stranger from Elea tells us in the Sophist. And our opponents themselves agree on all sides, for they set what is first as the One, intellect as one-many, soul as one and many, and body as many and one. Since, then, this has been said a thousand times, that in true being there is multitude together with union, will they say that these conclusions fit the whole of being but not its parts, or both the whole and its parts? And again we ask them: do they give all the conclusions to each part of being, or different ones to different parts?
!note Sophist 245a-b. The ladder one, one-many, one and many, many and one is the reading of the first three hypotheses that Plotinus gives (V.1.8), applied down to bodies.

@p
If they think that each conclusion fits the whole of being alone, being will be made of non-beings, what is moved of unmoved things, what remains of things without rest, and in general all things will be made of their opposites. Then we will no longer agree with Parmenides' account, which says that the parts of the being that has the character of the One are in a way wholes, and that each of them is one and being, just like the whole. But if we give all the conclusions to each part, and there is nothing we do not make all things, how can the summit of being, what is most of all one, hold a wholeness and a boundless multitude of parts? How can it hold at one and the same time the whole of number, shape, motion and rest, and in short all Forms and kinds? These differ from each other, and the hypothesis will assert impossible things. Things near the One will be as multiplied as things far from it; what is first will be no less a multitude than what is last; the last will be no less one than the first; and the things in the middle will be no different, in division, from the extremes.
!note Parmenides 142d-143a: each part of the one being has both one and being, and so is itself a whole.

@p
So it is not right to give all this multitude of conclusions to the whole alone, nor to see everything alike in all the parts of being. It remains that different conclusions must fit different things. Then the listing of the conclusions must be either disordered or ordered. If they say it is disordered, they speak in keeping neither with the dialectical method, nor with the way of demonstrations, which always bring second things out of first, nor with Plato's science, which always follows the order of things. If they say the conclusions are ordered, I think they must begin either from the things first by nature or from the last. If from the last, then the being that has the character of the One will be last, and what moves in time will be first. But this is impossible. For what shares in time must, far before that, share in first being; but what shares in first being need not share in time. So first being is above time. If, then, Plato begins from first being and ends with what shares in time, he proceeds from above, from the first to the last parts of true being. So the first conclusions are to be referred to the first orders, the middle ones, for the same reason, to the middle orders, and the last, plainly, to the last. For, as our argument has shown, different conclusions must be given to different things, and such a distribution must begin from the highest.
!note The second hypothesis begins with "the one, if it is" (142b) and ends with the one that shares in time, becoming older and younger (151e-155d).

@p
The order of the hypotheses, too, is to my mind enough proof of what we say. For with us the One that is set apart from all multitude is given the first order, and the unfolding of all the arguments begins from it. The second order after it is about true beings and the unity they share in. And the third, next in line, is about soul. Is it about every soul, or not? We answer that our leader Syrianus has finely shown that the account of the whole souls is included in the second hypothesis. If, then, the order of these three hypotheses follows the nature of things, it is clear that the second is produced from the first and the last from the second. For I would ask those who are not wholly untrained in such discussions: what can be more akin to the One than the being that has the character of the One, which the first conclusion of the second hypothesis unfolds? Or what can be more akin to soul than what shares in time, which is divided, and is the last thing shown in this hypothesis? For the life of partial souls, as of whole souls, is in time. First being is what first shares in the One, and through its link with being it has a hyparxis that goes beyond the unshared unity. If this hypothesis is the middle one, and we fit the highest conclusions to the highest things, we should surely fit the middle to the middle. For this hypothesis begins from first being and proceeds through all the kinds after it, until it ends in a nature that shares in time.

!note "Partial souls" are individual souls such as ours; "whole souls" are the souls of the world and of the heavenly bodies. The third hypothesis (155e-157b), on the one that both is and is not, Proclus takes to be about partial souls.

@p
Further, we can show the same things as we have said from what is agreed by the interpreters of Plato who were skilled in divine things. Plotinus, in his book On Numbers, asks whether beings are before numbers or numbers before beings, and plainly says that first being is before numbers and generates the divine number. If he is right in this, and being generates the first number, while number is produced by being, it is not right to confuse the order of these kinds, or gather them into one reality, or, since Plato brings out first being separately and number separately, refer each of the conclusions to the same order. For it is in no way lawful for a cause and its effect to have either the same power or the same order. They are distinct from each other, the science of them is distinct too, and their nature and their definition are not one and the same.
!note Plotinus, Ennead VI.6, On Numbers (chapters 8-9): being is prior to number, and being in its unity generates number. In the Parmenides, number comes after the one being (143a-144a).

@p
After Plotinus, Porphyry, in his treatise On Principles, shows by many fine arguments that intellect is eternal, but holds in itself something prior to the eternal, through which it is joined to the One. For the One is above all eternity, while the eternal has the second, or rather the third, rank in intellect. It seems to me necessary that eternity be set in the middle between what is prior to the eternal and the eternal. But of this later. All the same, this much can be gathered from what has been said: intellect holds in itself something better than the eternal. Granting this, we ask the father of this view whether this something better than the eternal is only the being that has the character of the One, or is also whole and parts, all multitude, number and shape, what is moved and what remains at rest; or whether we are to give some of the conclusions to it and not others. All these cannot fit a nature prior to eternity, since every intellectual motion, and rest too, is set in eternity. But if we are to give some of the conclusions to it and not others, it is clear that we must look for other orders in intellect, and refer each conclusion to the order it seems best suited to. For intellect is not one in number and indivisible like an atom, as it seemed to some of the ancients, but holds in itself the whole procession of first being.
!note Porphyry's On Principles is lost; this is one of the few reports of it. "Eternity in the middle" is the Timaeus' "eternity remaining in one" (37d), which Proclus sets between being and the eternal living being.

@p
Third after these, the divine Iamblichus helps our case. In his treatise On the Gods he blames those who place the kinds of being among the intelligibles, because their number and variety are further from the One. Then he tells us where they should be placed: they are produced at the end of the intellectual order, by the gods there. How the kinds of being both are and are not among the intelligibles will be made clear later. But if, in his arrangement of the divine orders, the intelligibles are set above the kinds of being, much more are they above likeness and unlikeness, equality and inequality. So each conclusion should not be fitted alike to everything, so as to refer them to the whole breadth of the intelligible or the intellectual order. So, from what the best of the interpreters have said when they philosophized by their own teachings, both the multitude of the divine orders and that of Plato's arguments are to be seen as proceeding by an orderly distinction.
!note Iamblichus' On the Gods is lost. The "kinds of being" are the Sophist's greatest kinds (254b-255e): being, rest, motion, sameness and difference.

@p
In addition to what has been said, this too can be stated: on no other view can we find a reasoned answer to the many difficulties that arise here, and we would ignorantly credit this work of Plato's with what is rash and empty. First, why are there just so many conclusions, neither more nor fewer? There are fourteen. But we cannot give the reason why there are so many unless we distribute them together with the things themselves. Second, we will not be able to find why the conclusions are ordered as they are, some placed before and others after by the reason of science, unless the order of the conclusions follows the procession of beings. Third, why are some conclusions known from what was proved just before, and others from earlier proofs? That the One is a whole and has parts is proved from the being that has the character of the One; but that it is in itself and in another is placed next after having a shape, yet is proved from whole and parts. Or why are some things often proved from two of the points shown before, and others from one? We will be ignorant of each of these, and unable to study their number, their order or their kinship by science, unless we follow the things themselves and show that this whole hypothesis is a dialectical arrangement, proceeding from on high through all the middle kinds down to the end of first being.
!note The Parmenides (144e-145b): the one being is a whole with parts, then has shape, then is in itself and in another (145b-e), proved from whole and parts.

@p
Again, if we say that all the conclusions prove only by syllogism, how will we differ from those who say that this whole discussion is made of arguments from opinion and is only a contest of words? But if it proves not only by syllogism but by demonstration, then the middle term must be the cause of the conclusion and prior to it by nature. Since, then, we make the conclusions of the earlier reasonings the middle terms of those that follow, the things the arguments are about must have a like order in being. What they produce must be the causes of what lies below them and generate what is second. If this is granted, how can we allow that all of them have the same character and nature? For a cause and what is produced by a cause are set apart from each other.
!note Aristotle, Posterior Analytics I.2 (71b): demonstration is a syllogism from premises that are prior to the conclusion and its causes.

@p
Those who say that one nature is to be looked for in all the arguments will also fail to see how, in the first three conclusions, the One remains unseparated from being, and is first separated in the fourth, while in all the conclusions that follow the One is looked at as being by itself. Must not these orders, then, differ from each other? For what is without separation, being hidden and undivided, is more akin to the One, while what is separated has proceeded further from the first principle of things.

@p
Again, if you will consider the multitude of the arguments and the length of the hypothesis, how much it differs from the one that follows it, from this too it will not seem to you to be wholly about one unseparated nature. For reasonings about divine things are drawn together in the higher causes, because in these the hidden is more than the plain, and the inexpressible more than the known. But they become many and are unfolded as they go on to divine orders nearer to our nature. For what is more akin to the inexpressible, the unknowable and what is set apart in places no one can reach, has a hyparxis further from being put into words. But what has proceeded further is better known to us, and plainer to the imagination, than what exists before it.

@p
This, then, being fully proved, the second hypothesis must unfold all the divine orders. It must proceed from on high, from the simplest and most unitary, to the whole multitude, all the number of divine natures, in which the order of true being ends. This true being is spread beneath the henads of the gods, and is at the same time divided together with their hidden and inexpressible characters. If we are not mistaken in granting this, it follows that from this hypothesis we must take the continuity of the divine orders, the procession of the second from the first, and the distinctive character of all the divine kinds. Their communion with each other, their distinction proceeding by measure, and the helps that may be found in other dialogues on the truth of real beings or the henads they hold: all are to be referred to this hypothesis. For here we may contemplate the whole processions of the gods and their complete orders, by the science of theology. Since we have already shown that the whole work of the Parmenides looks to the truth of things, and was not devised as an empty unrolling of words, the nine hypotheses it discusses, using the dialectical method but studying with divine science, must be about things and certain natures, either middle or last. If Parmenides admits that his whole discourse will be about the One, and how it stands with respect to itself and all other things, it is clear that the study of the One must begin from the highest and end in the last of all things. For the hyparxis of the One proceeds from on high as far as the dimmest reality of things.
!note Parmenides 136b-c, 137b: Parmenides will speak "about the one itself", what follows for it and for the others if it is and if it is not.

@ch I.12 | What each hypothesis is about, and how they hang together
The first hypothesis shows by negations the first principle, above all being and knowledge; the second unfolds all the orders of gods, from the summit of the intelligibles to the divine souls; the third, the partial souls; the fourth, the forms in bodies; the fifth, matter. The last four take the One away and show that all things then vanish, so the One is the cause that makes all things be and keeps them. Next Proclus turns to Plato's common teachings about the gods.

@p
The first hypothesis shows by negations the inexpressible transcendence of the first principle of things, and shows that it is set apart from all being and all knowledge. So the next hypothesis, as nearest to it, must clearly unfold the whole order of the gods. For Parmenides takes up through the whole of this hypothesis not only the intellectual character of the gods and the character that belongs to their being, but also the divine mark of their hyparxis. For what other one can be shared by being than the one that is in every being divine, and through which all things are joined to the unshared One? As bodies are joined to soul through their life, and as souls reach out through their intellectual part to the whole intellect and the first act of thinking, so true beings, through the one they hold, are led back to a union set apart, and remain in union with this first cause without going out from it.
!flag subsist in unproceeding union with this first cause | Taylor's "unproceeding" means "without going forth": though true beings proceed from the One, through the one in them they also remain in it.

@p
But this hypothesis begins from the one being, the being that has the character of the One, sets the summit of the intelligibles as the first after the One, ends in a being that shares in time, and brings the divine souls down to the last of the divine orders. So the third hypothesis must show, by its varied conclusions, the whole multitude of partial souls and the differences among them. And this is how far the separate and bodiless reality proceeds.

@p
After this comes the nature that is divided about bodies and cannot be separated from matter, which the fourth hypothesis gives, as hanging from the gods above. The last is the coming forth of matter, whether taken as one or as many, which the fifth hypothesis shows by negations, through its unlike likeness to the first. But sometimes the negations are privations, and sometimes they are the causes set above all that is produced. And most wonderful of all, the highest negations only deny, but some of them by transcendence and others by lack. Each of the hypotheses after these affirms, the one as a pattern and the other as an image. The middle hypothesis answers to the order of soul, for it is made of affirmative and negative conclusions, but it has negations ranked together with its affirmations. Nor is it simply multiplied, as material natures are, nor does it have a one brought in from outside. The one it holds, though still one, is in motion and multiplication, and in its processions is, as it were, swallowed up by being. These are the hypotheses that unfold all beings, both separable and inseparable, together with the causes of all things, both those set apart and those in things themselves, by the hyparxis of the One.

But there are four other hypotheses beside these. By taking away the One they show that all things must be wholly overturned, both beings and things that come to be, and that no being could any longer exist. This is so that he may show the One to be the cause of being and of keeping in being: that through it all things share in the nature of being, and each has its hyparxis hanging from the One. In short, through all beings we gather by reasoning that if the One is, all things are, down to the last reality, and if it is not, nothing is. The One, then, is the cause that makes all things exist and keeps them; and Parmenides himself draws this conclusion at the end of the dialogue. But we have treated fully, in our commentaries on that dialogue, the hypotheses of the Parmenides, their division and the study of their parts, so that a long discussion of these points here would be superfluous. Since from what has been said it is clear where we may take the whole of theology from, and from which dialogues we may gather into one the theology given out in parts, we will next treat of Plato's common teachings, which are fitted to sacred things and reach to all the divine orders, and show that each of them is defined by him with the most perfect science. For common things come before peculiar ones, and are better known by nature.

@ch I.13 | The Laws' three rules: the gods exist, and the argument from motion
The first of Plato's common teachings are the three in the Laws: the gods exist, they provide for all things, and they cannot be turned from justice. First, that they exist. Of beings some are only moved, some move and are moved, some move themselves, and one is unmoved. Bodies are only moved; soul moves itself; intellect is unmoved; and above intellect is the divine unity. So the world, and every sphere in it, is a living being with soul and intellect, hanging from the gods.

@p
First, then, we will take up what is proved in the Laws, and see how it leads the way in the truth about the gods and is the oldest of all the other sacred ideas about the divine nature. Plato asserts three things in this work: that there are gods; that their providence reaches to all things; and that they govern all things by justice and are not turned aside by anything worse.
!note Laws X (885b, 907b): there are three errors about the gods, that they do not exist, that they do not care for men, and that they can be won over by prayers and sacrifices. Book X answers all three.

@p
It is plain that these hold first place among all the teachings of theology. For what can lead more than the hyparxis of the gods, their providence, which has the form of the good, and their unchanging and unswerving power? Through these the gods produce what is second to them in a one-like way, keep themselves undefiled, and turn what they produce back to themselves. The gods govern other things but are not acted on by what is below them, and they do not change with the variety of the things their providence reaches. We will learn how these things are defined by nature if we try to take in, by reasoning, Plato's scientific method about each of them; and before these, if we look at the irrefutable arguments by which he proves that there are gods, and then consider the questions linked with this teaching.
!note Taylor's footnote corrects a word of the Greek he translated ("more ancient").

@p
Of all beings, then, some must only move, others must only be moved, and those between must both move and be moved. Of these last, some must move others while being moved by others, and others must move themselves. These four realities, too, must be set in order one after another. What is only moved, and is acted on, depends on other causes before it. What moves others and is moved by another comes before it. What moves itself is above what moves and is moved: it begins from itself, and through its own motion gives other things the appearance of being moved. And what is unmoved comes before everything that shares in motion, whether in producing it or in undergoing it. For everything that moves itself has its perfection in passage and in an extension of life, and so depends on another, older cause, which is always the same and in the same way, and whose life is not in time but in eternity. For time is an image of eternity.
!note Laws X 893b-895b: the ten kinds of motion, among which the motion that moves itself and others is first. "Time is an image of eternity": Timaeus 37d.

@p
So if everything moved by itself is moved in time, while the eternal form of motion is above what is carried along in time, the self-moving nature will be second in order, and not the first of beings. What moves others and is moved by others must depend on a self-moving nature: and not this alone, but every production that is moved by another, as the Athenian Stranger shows. For if all things stood still, he says, and there were no self-moving natures among things, there would be nothing that is first moved. What is unmoved is not by nature fit to be moved, so there would then be no first moved; and what is moved by another needs another power to move it. Only the self-moving nature, then, beginning from its own activity, will move both itself and, in a second way, others. Such a thing gives the power of being moved to the natures moved by others, just as an unmoved nature gives the power of moving to all beings. Third, what is only moved must first depend on what is moved by another but moves others. For the other things, and the series of moved things that stretches in order from on high to the last, must be filled with their own middle terms.
!note Laws X 895a-b: if all things came to a standstill, the first motion to arise would be the self-moving one.

@p
So all bodies belong among the things that by nature are only moved and are acted on. For they produce nothing, since they have an extended existence and share in size and bulk; and everything that produces and moves others produces and moves by using a bodiless power.

@p
Of bodiless natures, some are divided about bodies, while others are set above such division about the last of things. The bodiless things divided about the bulk of bodies, whether they are qualities or forms in matter, are among those moved by another while moving others. Because they have a bodiless lot, they share in the power of moving. But because they are divided about bodies, they lack the power of turning towards themselves, are divided along with what underlies them, and are filled with sluggishness from it. So they need a moving nature that is not carried in a seat foreign to it but has its existence in itself. Where, then, shall we find what moves itself? Things stretched out into natures with bulk and extension, or divided in them and inseparable from them, must either be only moved or move through others. But, as we said before, a self-moving nature must come before these, set wholly in itself and not in others, and fixing its activities in itself and not in things other than itself. So there is a certain other nature, set apart from bodies, both in the heavens and in these changeable elements, from which bodies first get the power of being moved. If we must find what this kind of being is (rightly following Socrates, and looking to the end of things), which by its presence gives to the natures moved by others an appearance of self-motion, to which of the natures mentioned shall we give the power by which things are moved from themselves? All lifeless things are only moved by another, and whatever they undergo, they are fit to undergo through some power that moves and compels them from outside. It remains, then, that ensouled things have this appearance, and are self-moving in a second way, while the soul in them moves itself and is moved by itself first; and that, by a power from itself, as it gives life to bodies, so it also extends to them from itself an appearance of being moved by themselves.
!flag rightly following Socrates, and considering what the end of things is | Proclus seems to mean Socrates in the Phaedrus (245c-e), who proves the soul immortal as the self-moving source of motion; Taylor's "the end of things" is unclear, and may mean "the result", what follows.

@p
If, then, self-moving being is older than the natures moved by others, and soul is first of all self-moving, giving bodies an image of self-motion, soul will be above bodies, and the motion of every body will be the offspring of soul and of the motion in it. So the whole heaven, and all the bodies in it, having various motions and moved with these different motions by nature (for circling is natural to every such body), must have ruling souls, older in being than bodies, moved in themselves, and lighting up the bodies from above with the power of being moved. These souls, which set in order the whole world and its parts and give to every body, lifeless in itself, the power of being moved, breathing into it the cause of motion for this, must move all things either by reason or in the opposite way, which it is not lawful to say. If this world, and everything in it that is set in order and moves evenly and for ever by nature, as is proved partly in the mathematical studies and partly in the study of nature, hung from an irrational soul, which moves itself and others, then neither the order of the circuits, nor the motion bounded by one reason, nor the position of the bodies, nor anything else that comes about by nature, would have a stable cause, able to set out everything in order and always in the same way. For everything irrational is by nature fit to be ordered by something other than itself, and is unlimited and unordered in its own nature. To hand over the whole heaven, and a circuit turning by reason and always the same, to such a thing fits neither the nature of things nor our untaught ideas. But if an intellectual soul, which uses reason, governs all things, and if everything moved in an everlasting motion is governed by such a soul, and none of the wholes in the universe lacks soul (for "no body is honourable without such a power", as Theophrastus says somewhere), then does this soul have its intellectual, perfect and beneficent power by sharing, or by its very being? If by its being, then every soul must be of this kind, since each is self-moving by its own nature. But if by sharing, there will be another intellect, actual and older than soul, which has thinking in its very being and, by its very being, holds first in itself the one-like knowledge of all things. For the soul whose being is defined by reason must have what belongs to intellect by sharing, and the intellectual nature must be twofold: one first, in divine intellect itself; the other proceeding from it, second, in soul. You may add to these, if you like, the presence of intellectual light in body. For how is this whole heaven spherical, or moved in a circle? How does it turn with the same circuit in one fixed order? How could it always keep the same Form and power, unchanged, by nature, if it did not share in being formed by intellect? For soul supplies motion. But the cause of firm stability, which brings the unsteady change of moving things back to sameness, and of a life bounded by one reason and a circuit always the same, will clearly be above soul.
!flag possess this intellectual, perfect, and beneficent power, according to participation, or according to essence | That is, whether the world-soul is intelligent by its very being (so that every soul would be), or by sharing in an intellect above it. Proclus takes the second: intellect is above soul.
!note Laws X 896e-898c: the soul that drives the heaven must be the best soul, using intellect, since the heaven moves uniformly in a circle, as intellect does. Theophrastus' words come from a lost work.

@p
So body, and the whole of this perceptible nature, belongs among the things moved by another. Soul moves itself, and binds all bodily motions in itself. And before soul is intellect, which is unmoved. But let no one think that I mean this stillness of intellect to be like something sluggish, lifeless and without breath. It is the leading cause of all motion, and the source, if you like to call it so, of all life: of the life that turns back to itself and of the life that has its existence in other things. Through these causes, too, Timaeus calls the world a living being with soul and intellect. He calls it a living being by its own nature and by the life that reaches it from soul and is spread about it; ensouled, by the presence of a divine soul in it; and with intellect, by the rule of intellect. For the supply of life, the government of soul and the sharing in intellect hold together and contain the whole heaven.
!note Timaeus 30b: the world came to be "a living being, ensouled and with intellect". Taylor's footnote corrects a word of the Greek he translated ("without breath").

@p
But this intellect is intellect by its very being, since Timaeus, pointing out that the being of intellect is the same as its thinking, calls it divine: he says that soul, taking hold of a divine intellect, led a straight and wise life. If this is so, the whole world must hang from its divinity. Motion must come to this universe from soul; its everlasting remaining and sameness from intellect; and its one union, the breathing together and sympathy in it, and its complete measure, from that unity by which intellect is one-like, soul is one, every being is whole and perfect in its own nature, and every second thing, besides its perfection in its own nature, shares in another, higher character from an order always set above it. For what is bodily, moved by another, gets from soul the appearance of self-moving power, and through soul is a living being. Soul, which moves itself, shares in a life by intellect, and though it acts in time it has an unceasing activity and an ever-wakeful life from its nearness to intellect. And intellect, having its life in eternity, always actual in its very being, and fixing all its stable thinking at once in itself, is wholly made divine through the cause before it. For it has two kinds of activity, as Plotinus says: some as intellect, and others as drunk with nectar. And elsewhere he says that this intellect, by what is before it and is not intellect, is a god, just as soul, by its summit, which is above soul, is intellect, and body, by the power before body, is soul.
!note "Taking hold of a divine intellect": the Laws (897b), of the soul that governs the heaven. Plotinus VI.7.35: intellect has one power for thinking and another, "drunk with nectar", by which it sees what is beyond it. Taylor's three footnotes correct the Greek he translated.

@p
So all things, as we have said, hang from the One, through intellect and soul as middles. Intellect has the form of the One; soul has the form of intellect; and the body of the world is alive. Everything is joined to what is before it. Of the natures after these, one enjoys the divine more nearly and another more distantly. Divinity is before intellect, carried first in an intellectual nature. Intellect is most divine, as made divine before the others. Soul is divine so far as it needs intellect as a middle. And the body that shares in such a soul is itself divine as body, since the light of the divine reaches from above even to the last things that hang from it. Yet it is not simply divine. Soul, by looking to intellect and living from itself, is divine first.
!flag but soul, by looking to intellect, and living from itself, is primarily divine | This seems to contradict what was just said, that divinity is first in intellect. Proclus means that soul is the first of the things that are divine by sharing, before body, while intellect is divine by its very being; Taylor's footnote supplies the word "divine" earlier in the paragraph.

@p
My argument is the same about each of the whole spheres and the bodies they hold. All these imitate the whole heaven, since they too have an everlasting lot. As for the elements below the moon, they do not wholly change in their being: they remain in the universe as wholes, and hold in themselves partial living beings. For every whole has after it more partial beings. As in the heavens the number of the stars proceeds together with the whole spheres, and on earth the multitude of partial earthly animals exists together with their whole, so, I think, in the wholes in between each element must be filled with fitting numbers. For how could the wholes that exist before parts be ranked together with parts in the extremes, unless they have the same proportion in the natures between?
!note "The wholes in between" are water and air, between the heaven and the earth; their "numbers" are the living beings proper to them (daemons, in Proclus' reading of Timaeus 40a and Epinomis 984b-985b).

@p
If each of the spheres is a living being, always the same, and completes the universe, then, as having life, it will always share first in soul; as keeping its own order in the world unchanged, it will be held by intellect; and as one and a whole, the leader and ruler of its own parts, it will be lit by divine union. So not only the universe but each of its everlasting parts has soul and intellect, and is, as far as possible, like the universe. For each of these parts is a universe in relation to the multitude akin to it. In short, there is one bodily whole of the universe, and many others below it, depending on this one. There is one soul of the universe, and after it other souls which, together with it, set in order the whole parts of the universe with undefiled purity. There is one intellect, and an intellectual number below it, shared by these souls. And there is one god who holds together at once all natures in the world and above the world, and a multitude of other gods, who give out intellectual beings, the souls that hang from them, and all the parts of the world. For we must not suppose that each work of nature generates things like itself, but that the wholes and the first of the beings in the world do not much more hold in themselves the pattern of such a generation. For the like is more akin to the nature of a cause than the unlike, as the same is more than the different, and limit more than the unlimited. These things, however, we will study exactly later on. Now we will turn to the second of the things proved in the Laws, that the gods take care of wholes and parts at once, and briefly discuss Plato's faultless idea of the providence of the gods.

@ch I.14 | The gods provide for all things, and how
The gods are causes of every motion: some as souls, some as intellects, the highest as henads, and all things share in the One. Causes must care for what they produce. So the gods, who know what is fitting, have the power to do it, and are good, must provide for all things, great and small. Their providence costs them no labour: they act by their very being, will and power in one. It reaches all things without turning to them or mixing with them, in a way proper to each order of gods.

@p
From what has been said, then, it is clear to everyone that the gods are the causes of all motion. Some of them are of the order of being and life-giving, by a power that moves itself, lives of itself and acts of itself. Others are intellectual: by their very being they rouse all second natures to perfection of life, as the source and principle of all second and third processions of motion. Others are unitary, having the character of the One. These make divine, by sharing, all the whole kinds below them, by a first, complete and unknowable power of activity, and they lead one kind of motion without being the principle of another. Others again give second natures motion in place or in quality, but are the causes of motion to themselves by their very being. For everything that causes being in others is, far before this, the cause to itself of its own activities and perfection. Further, what moves itself is again the principle of motion: being and life are given by soul to everything in the world, and not motion in place only and the other kinds of motion, but the coming forth into being comes from soul, and far before that from intellectual being, which binds to itself the life of self-moving natures and goes before every activity in time as its cause. And still more do motion, being and life proceed from a unitary hyparxis, which holds together intellect and soul, is the source of all good, and proceeds to the last of things. Not all the parts of the world can share in life, nor in intellect and the power of knowing; but all things share in the One, down to matter itself: wholes and parts, things according to nature and things against it. Nothing lacks a cause of this kind, and nothing could ever share in being if it lacked the One. If, then, the gods produce all things and hold all things in their own unknowable embrace, how could there not be in that embrace a providence of all things, reaching from above to the most partial natures? Everywhere it is fitting that offspring should enjoy the care of their causes. All things moved by another are offspring of self-moving natures. Things in time, whether in the whole of time or in part of it, are effects of eternal natures, since what always is is the cause of what sometimes is. And the divine and unitary kinds, as they give existence to all multiplied natures, come before them. In short, there is no being and no multitude of powers that does not have its birth from the One. So all these must share in the providence of the causes before them: given life by the gods of the order of soul, and moving round in periods of time; sharing in sameness and in the stable state of Forms from the intellectual gods; and receiving into themselves the presence of union, of measure and of the giving out of good from the first gods. So either the gods know that caring for their own offspring is natural to them, and do not only give existence to second beings and supply them with life, being and union, but also hold first in themselves the first cause of the goods these beings have; or else, which it is not lawful to say, though they are gods they do not know what is proper and fitting.
!note Laws X 899b: the gods are the souls (and intellects) that cause the motions of the heaven, so "all things are full of gods" (Thales' saying). Taylor's footnotes correct the Greek he translated and supply "from the intellectual gods".

@p
For how can those who are the causes of beauty be ignorant of beautiful things, or those whose hyparxis is defined by the nature of the good be ignorant of good things? If they are ignorant, then souls do not govern the universe by intellect, nor are intellects carried in souls as in a vehicle, nor, before these, do the henads of the gods hold all knowledge drawn together in themselves, as we granted through the earlier proofs. If, then, they do not lack knowledge, being the fathers, leaders and governors of everything in the world, and the care of what they govern, of what follows them and what they produce belongs to them as such, shall we say that they know the law of nature and fulfil it, or that through weakness they fail to care for their possessions or offspring? (It makes no difference to the present argument which of these two names you choose.) If they neglect the care of wholes for lack of power, what is the cause of this lack? For they do not move things from outside, while other things are the causes of being. They take charge of the things they have produced, and they rule over all things as from the stern of a ship, themselves supplying being, themselves holding the measures of life, and themselves giving out to things their activities.
!note Laws X 902b-903a: since all things are the possessions of the gods, and the gods are the best of masters, they cannot neglect them. "As from the stern of a ship": Statesman 272e, of the god who steers the universe, and Critias 109c.

@p
Are they, then, unable to provide for all things at once, or do they leave none of the parts without their care? And if they do not care for everything in the world, do they look after greater things and neglect the smaller, or attend to the smaller and neglect the greater? If we deny them care of all things alike through lack of power, how can we grant them the greater thing, the making of all things, and refuse them what follows naturally from it, care for what they have made? For it belongs to the power that produces the greater to arrange the smaller well. But if they care for the smaller and neglect the greater, how can this kind of providence be right? For what is more akin and more like a thing is fitted by nature to share more properly in the good it gives. If, again, the gods think the first of the natures in the world worthy of their care, and of the perfection of which they are the sources, but cannot reach to the last of things, what can keep the presence of the gods from reaching all things? What can hinder their ungrudging and overflowing activity? How can those able to do greater things be unable to govern lesser ones? Or how can those who produce the being even of the smallest things not be masters of their perfection, through lack of power? All these things are hostile to our natural ideas. It remains, then, that the gods know what is fitting and proper, and that they have a power suited to the perfection of their own nature and to the government of all things. But if they know what is according to nature, and for the generating causes of all things this is to care for all things, and they have an overflowing power, then they are not without this kind of care. Together with what has been said, is there also in them the will to provide? Or is this alone missing from their knowledge and power, so that things are left without their care? For if, knowing what is fitting for them and able to do what they know, they are unwilling to provide for their own offspring, they will lack goodness, their ungrudging overflowing will perish, and we will be doing nothing else than doing away with the hyparxis that is their very being. For the very being of the gods is defined by the good, and they exist in it. To provide for what is below is to give it some good. How, then, can we take providence from the gods without at the same time taking away their goodness? And if we do away with their goodness, how can we not also, unknowingly, do away with their hyparxis, which we established in the earlier proofs? So we must admit, as following from the very being of the gods, that they are good with every virtue. And again it follows from this that they do not withdraw from caring for second natures, whether through idleness, weakness or ignorance. And from this, I think, it follows too that in them are the best knowledge, undefiled power, and an ungrudging and overflowing will. From which it appears that they provide for the whole of things and leave out nothing needed for the giving of good.
!note Laws X 900c-903a: the gods know, can and will care for small things as well as great, as a doctor or a builder cares for the small parts with the whole. Taylor's footnote corrects a word of the Greek he translated.

@p
But let no one think that the gods exercise their providence over second things in a busy or laborious way, or that this is so with their transcendence, set far from mortal toil. For their blessedness will not be soiled by the trouble of administration, since even the life of good men goes easily and is free of annoyance and pain. All labour and annoyance come from the hindrances of matter. If we must define the manner of the gods' providence, we must admit that it is unforced, undefiled, immaterial and inexpressible. For the gods do not govern all things by searching out what is fitting, or exploring the good of each thing by uncertain reasonings, or by looking outside themselves and following their effects, as men do in the care they take of their own affairs. Having in themselves first the measures of all things, producing the being of each thing from themselves, and looking to themselves, they lead and perfect all things on a silent path by their very being, and fill them with good. Nor do they produce as nature does, acting only by their being without choice; nor, acting as partial souls do, with will, do they lack production by their being. They draw both together into one union. They will what they can do by their very being; and, able by their very being to produce all things, they hold the cause of production in their ungrudging and overflowing will. With what busy activity, then, with what trouble, or with the punishment of what Ixion, is the providence of the whole souls, or of intellectual beings, or of the gods themselves carried out, unless someone says that to give good in any way is laborious to the gods? But nothing is laborious to anything that is according to its nature. It is not laborious for fire to give heat, or for snow to cool, or, in short, for bodies to act by their own powers. Before bodies, it is not laborious for natures to nourish, generate or increase, for these are the works of natures. Nor, again, before these, is it laborious for souls. They produce many activities by choice, many by their very being, and are the causes of many motions merely by being present. So if giving good is according to nature for the gods, providence too is according to nature. And we must say that these things are done by the gods with ease, and by their very being alone. But if these things are not according to nature, the gods will not be good by nature either. For the good supplies good, as life is the source of other life and intellect of intellectual light. And everything that is first in each nature generates what is second in it.
!flag with the punishment of what Ixion | Ixion was bound for ever to a turning wheel; Proclus means a providence that would be endless forced labour, like his.
!note "On a silent path": the Trojan Women of Euripides (887-888), of Zeus who "leads all mortal things by justice on a silent path". Aristotle, On the Heavens II.1 (284a), also says that the heaven's motion would be laborious if it were forced, like Ixion's wheel.

@p
But what I would call the glorious distinction of Plato's theology is this. By it, the set-apart being of the gods is not turned towards second natures through caring for what is below; nor is their providential presence to all things lessened by their being above the whole of things in undefiled purity. Plato's theology gives them, at the same time, a separate existence, unmixed with every lower nature, and also a reach to all things, and the care and ordering of their own offspring. The way they pass through all things is not bodily, as light passes through the air; nor divided about bodies, as in nature; nor turned towards lower things, as with a partial soul. It is separate from body and does not turn towards it; it is immaterial, unmixed, unhindered, one-like, first and set apart. In short, this is how we must think of the gods' providence for now. Clearly it will be proper to each order of gods. Soul is said to provide for second natures in one way, and intellect in another. The providence of the divinity before intellect works by a transcendence over both intellect and soul. And among the gods themselves, the providence of the gods below the moon differs from that of the heavenly gods. Of the gods above the world, too, there are many orders, and the manner of providence differs in each.

@ch I.15 | The gods provide without being turned from justice
Every true ruler, pilot, doctor or guardian aims only at the good of those he rules. So the gods, good and with every virtue, cannot be bribed by the gifts of the wicked to neglect the good of those in their care: otherwise the images would rule better than their causes, and the whole would be in disorder. The three teachings of the Laws, existence, providence and unswerving justice, belong to every god.

@p
The third question we will join to the earlier ones, and consider how we are to understand that the gods cannot be turned aside: that they do all things by justice and do not in the least overturn its limit or its straight rule in their care for all other things and in the changes of human affairs. I think it is clear to everyone that whatever rules according to nature, and gives all possible care to the happiness of those it rules, thereby leads what it rules and directs it to what is best. The pilot who rules the sailors and the ship has no other main aim than the safety of those who sail in it and of the ship itself. The doctor who has charge of the sick does everything for nothing else than the health of those in his care, whether he must cut them or give them a purge. Nor would a general or a guardian say they look to any other end than, one, the freedom of those he guards, and the other, of his soldiers. Nor will anyone else whose task is to lead or take care of others try to overturn the good of those who follow him, which it is his business to bring about, and with a view to which he arranges everything for those he governs. If, then, we grant that the gods are the leaders of the whole of things, and that their providence reaches all things, since they are good and have every virtue, how can they neglect the happiness of those in their care? Or how can they be worse than other leaders in their care for lower natures? For the gods always look to what is better, and set this as the end of all their government, while other leaders overlook the good of men and embrace vice rather than virtue, when they are corrupted by the gifts of the wicked.
!note Laws X 905d-907b: the gods are like pilots, charioteers, generals, doctors, farmers and shepherds, and none of these would betray those in their charge for gifts.

@p
In general, whether you choose to call the gods leaders, rulers, guardians or fathers, the divine nature will be seen to lack none of these names. For everything holy and honourable is in them first. And this is why here too some things are by nature more holy and honourable than others: they show the last likeness of the gods. But why say more on this? For I think we hear those wise in divine things praise fatherly, guarding, ruling and healing powers. How, then, can the images of the gods that exist by nature, looking to the end suited to them, care for the order of the things they govern, while the gods themselves, with whom are the whole of good, true and real virtue and a blameless life, would not direct their government to the virtue and vice of men? And how, on this view, could we admit that they show virtue victorious in the universe and vice defeated? Would they not then corrupt the measures of justice through the worship paid them by the wicked, overturn the limit of unswerving knowledge, and make the gifts of vice appear more honourable than the pursuits of virtue? This kind of providence is good neither for these leaders nor for those who follow them. For those who have become wicked there will be no release from guilt, since they will always try to get ahead of justice and bend the measures of what is due. And it would follow, which it is not lawful to say, that the gods aim at the vice of those in their care, neglect their true safety, and so are the causes only of a shadow of good. This universe, too, and the whole world will be filled with disorder and incurable confusion, vice remaining in it, full of the discord found in badly governed cities. And is it not wholly impossible that parts should be governed according to nature more than wholes, human things more than divine, and images more than first causes?
!flag paternal, guardian, ruling and pæonian powers | "Paeonian" means healing, from Paeon, the healer of the gods, a name of Apollo. Those "wise in divine things" are the theologians, the Orphics and the Chaldean Oracles, which name such orders of gods.
!flag consequently be alone the causes of adumbrant good | Taylor's "adumbrant" means shadowy: a good in appearance only.

@p
So if men rightly look after the welfare of men in governing them, honouring some and disgracing others, and everywhere correcting the works of vice by the measures of virtue, much more must the gods be the unswerving governors of the whole of things. For men get this virtue by being like the gods. But if we grant that men who corrupt the safety and well-being of those they govern imitate the gods' providence more, we will unknowingly overturn at one and the same time the truth about the gods and the high worth of virtue. For this, I think, is clear to everyone: what is more like the gods is happier than what is cut off from them by unlikeness and difference. So if among men the uncorrupted and unswerving form of providence is honourable, it must surely be much more honourable among the gods. But if among them mortal gifts were more holy than the divine measures of justice, then among men too earthly gifts would be more honourable than Olympian goods, and the flatteries of vice than the works of virtue. With a view, then, to the most perfect happiness, Plato in the Laws gives us through these proofs the hyparxis of the gods, their care reaching to all things, and their unchanging activity. These are common to all the gods, but are first and chief by nature in the teaching about them. For this triad seems to reach as far as the most partial natures in the divine orders, starting from above, from the hidden kinds of gods. For a one-like hyparxis, a power that cares for all second natures, and an unswerving and unchanging intellect are in all the gods, both before the world and in it.
!note "Earthly gifts" and "Olympian goods": the Laws (V 727e-728a) sets the goods of the soul above gold "on earth or under it". Taylor's footnote corrects a word of the Greek he translated.

@ch I.16 | The Republic's axioms: goodness, unchangeableness, truth
In the Republic Socrates gives the poets two laws for speaking of the gods: the divine is the cause of good only and of no evil; and it is unchanging and does not deceive. These give three common ideas about the divine, goodness, unchangeableness and truth, answering to the source of good, to eternity, and to the first intellect and its truth.

@p
Again, from another starting point we can grasp the proofs of theology in the Republic. These are common to all the divine orders, reach alike through the whole discussion of the gods, and unfold the truth for us in unbroken connection with what has been said. In the second book of the Republic Socrates sets out certain patterns of theology for the poets who make myths, and urges his pupils to clean themselves of the tragic teachings that some do not refuse to bring to the divine nature, hiding in them, as in veils, the secret mysteries about the gods. As I said, Socrates sets out the patterns and laws of myths about the gods, which give both this outward meaning and the inner hidden aim, which looks to the beautiful and the natural in the inventions about the gods. First he sees fit to show, by our unspoiled idea of the gods and their goodness, that they supply all good but are never the cause of any evil to any being. Second, he says that they are unchanging in their being, that they do not take on various shapes to deceive and bewitch, and are not the authors of the greatest evil, lying in deed or word, or of error and folly. These, then, are two laws, and the first has two conclusions: that the gods are not the causes of evils, and that they are the causes of all good. The second law likewise has two other conclusions: that every divine nature is unchanging, and that it is set pure of falsehood and contrived variety. So all that is proved depends on these three common ideas about the divine nature: its goodness, its unchangeableness and its truth. For the first and inexpressible source of good is with the gods; together with eternity, the cause of a power that is always the same; and the first intellect, which is the beings themselves, and the truth that is in real beings.

@ch I.17 | The goodness of the gods, and evil
What is good by its very being produces every good and no evil. All goods come from the gods as light from the sun, each thing receiving as much as it can. Evil is not in the wholes, but only in partial natures, through the weakness of what receives the gods' gifts, and never wholly without good; even it is ordered and used for good by the gods. There are no causes or patterns of evil in nature, intellect or the gods.

@p
What has its hyparxis and its whole being defined in the good, and produces all things by its very being, must produce every good and no evil. If there were something good first that was not god, perhaps one could say that the divine is a cause of good, but does not give every good to beings. But not only is every god good: what is first of all good in form and beneficent is god. For what is good first will not come second after the gods, since everywhere what is second receives the character of its hyparxis from what is first. So it is wholly necessary that the divine be the cause of good, and of all the goods that proceed in the second descents, down to the last of things. For as the power that causes life gives existence to all life, the power that causes knowledge produces all knowledge, and the power that causes beauty produces everything beautiful, the beauty in words and the beauty in what appears; and as every first cause produces from itself everything like it and binds to itself the one reality of the things that exist by one form, so, I think, the first and chief good, the one-like hyparxis, establishes in and about itself the causes and embraces of all goods at once. There is nothing good that does not have this power from it, nothing beneficent that does not share in this cause by turning back to it. For all goods are produced, perfected and kept from there, and the one series and order of all good depends on that source. Through the same cause of hyparxis, then, the gods supply all good and no evil. What is first of all good gives existence to every good from itself, and is not the cause of a lot contrary to itself; since what produces life is not the cause of the loss of life, and the source of beauty is set apart from the nature of what lacks beauty and is ugly, and from its causes. So of what first establishes the good it is not lawful to say that it is the cause of contrary offspring; the nature of goods proceeds from it undefiled, unmixed and one-like.
!flag for that which is primarily good will not be the second after the Gods | The point is that nothing can be good first and yet below the gods: whatever is good first is itself divine, so the gods are the causes of all good.

@p
The divine cause of goods is set eternally in itself, and gives all second natures an ungrudging and overflowing share of good. Of those that share, some keep their share with incorruptible purity, receiving their proper good in undefiled embraces, and so, through an abundance of power, have without fail a lot of goods suited to them. But the natures set at the last of the whole of things also enjoy, by their nature, the goodness of the gods, for what wholly lacks good could not be or come to be at all. Yet when they receive such an outflow, they do not keep the gift that reaches them pure and unmixed, nor hold their own good steadily and always the same. Becoming weak, partial and material, and filled with the lack of life of what underlies them, they show disorder in place of order, unreason in place of reason, and vice, the contrary of virtue, in place of virtue. The natures ranked as wholes are each set free of this kind of perversion, since what is more perfect in them always rules by nature. But partial natures, through a lessening of power, always turning aside into multitude, division and extension, darken their share of good and put its contrary in its place, mixed with the good and overcome in the mixture. For here too it is not lawful for evil to exist unmixed and wholly without good. Though a thing may be evil to a part, it is wholly good to the whole and to the universe. For the universe is always happy, and is always made of perfect parts that are according to nature. But what is against nature is always evil to partial natures, and in these are ugliness, lack of proportion, perversion and an appearance of existence. What is destroyed is destroyed to itself, and falls away from its proper perfection, but to the universe it is incorruptible and indestructible.

@p
Everything that lacks good, so far as concerns itself and its own existence, lacks it through weakness of nature; but it is good to the whole, so far as it is a part of the universe. For no lack of life, no ugliness or lack of measure, no privation at all can be put into the universe. Its whole number is always perfect, held together by the goodness of wholes. Life is present everywhere, together with existing and being perfect, so far as each thing completes the whole. So the divine, as we have said, is the cause of good; but the shadowy existence of evil comes not from power but from the weakness of the natures that receive the gods' light. Nor is evil in wholes, but in partial natures, and not in all of these. For the first of partial natures and the partial intellectual kinds have the form of the good eternally. But those in the middle among them, which act in time and join their share in the good to change and motion in time, cannot keep the gift of the gods unmoved, one-like and simple: by their variety they darken its simplicity, by their many forms its oneness of form, and by their mixture its purity and incorruptibility. For they are not made of incorruptible first kinds, nor do they have a simple being or one-like powers, but powers made of the contraries of these, as Socrates says somewhere in the Phaedrus. And the last of partial natures, which are also material, much more pervert their own good. They are mixed with lack of life and have an existence like an image's, full of much non-being, made of things hostile to each other and of circumstances that change and are scattered through the whole of time. So they never stop showing in everything that they are given over to corruption, lack of proportion, ugliness and all kinds of change. They are not only extended in their activities, like the natures before them, but are full, in their powers and activities, of what is against nature and of the weakness of matter. For things that come to be in a foreign place, bringing in the whole together with form, rule over the underlying nature; but falling back again from their proper whole into the partial, and sharing in divisibility, weakness, war and the division that is the source of becoming, they must change in every way. So not every being is wholly good, or there would be no destruction and generation of bodies, nor purification and punishment of souls. Nor is there any evil in wholes, or the world would not be a blessed god, if the chief parts it is made of were imperfect. Nor are the gods the causes of evils as they are of goods. Evil comes from the weakness of what receives good, and from being set at the last of things. Nor is the evil that has a shadowy existence in partial natures unmixed with good. It shares in good in a way, being held in existence by good. In short, evil that wholly lacks all good cannot exist. For evil itself is beyond even what in no way exists, just as the Good itself is beyond what wholly is. Nor is the evil in partial natures left in disorder: even this is made to serve good ends by the gods, and for this reason justice cleans souls of vice, and another order of gods cleans away the vice in bodies. All things, though, are turned as far as possible to the goodness of the gods. The wholes remain within their own limits, and so do the perfect and beneficent kinds of beings. But the more partial and imperfect natures are ordered and arranged as is fitting, serve the completing of wholes, are called up to the beautiful, are changed, and in every way enjoy their share in the good, as far as they can.

For there can be no greater good for each of these than what the gods give to their offspring by measure. All things, each one apart and all in common, receive as much good as they can share in. If some are filled with greater goods and others with lesser, the cause must be put down to the power of what receives and to the measures of the giving. Different things suit different beings by their nature. But the gods always hold out good, as the sun always sends out light. Each thing receives this light differently, by its rank, and receives the largest share of light it can. All things are led by justice, and good is absent from nothing, but present to everything, by the proper limit of its share. And, as the Athenian Stranger says, all things are in a good state and set in order by the gods. So let no one say that there are original forming principles of evil in nature, or intellectual patterns of evils as there are of goods, or an evil-working soul, or a cause that produces evil among the gods; nor let anyone bring in revolt and eternal war against the first Good. All these are foreign to Plato's science, and, being further from the truth, wander off into barbarian folly and the myths of the giants. And if certain people, speaking darkly in secret stories, devise things of this kind, we will make no change in the outward dress of what they point to. But we must search out the truth of those things, and meanwhile receive Plato's science purely into the pure embraces of the soul, and keep it undefiled and unmixed with contrary opinions.
