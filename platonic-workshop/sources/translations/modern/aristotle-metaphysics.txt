@treatise Metaph.I | Metaphysics, Book Α (I)
The Metaphysics is the work the Platonists read last of Aristotle's, as the summit of the course: the science of the first causes and principles, which Aristotle calls wisdom, first philosophy and theology. Book Α opens the inquiry. All men by nature desire to know; knowledge rises from the senses through memory and experience to art and science, and wisdom is the knowledge of the first causes, sought for its own sake, the most divine of the sciences (chapters 1 and 2). There are four causes: the matter, the source of motion, the form or what it is to be, and the end (3). Aristotle then reviews the earlier thinkers, to see whether they found any cause beyond these four: the first philosophers found only the material cause; others added a source of motion, love and strife, or intellect; the Pythagoreans made number the principle; the Eleatics made the whole one (3 to 5). Plato's philosophy, the Forms, the great and the small and the One, is set out (6), and the review is summed up (7). Then come the objections: to the materialists and the Pythagoreans (8), and at length to the Forms (9), where Aristotle speaks as one of Plato's school ("we"). The book closes by saying that all the earlier thinkers sought these four causes, but dimly, as a child lisps (10).

@ch Metaph.I.1 | From the senses to wisdom
All men by nature desire to know, as their love of the senses shows. From sense comes memory, from many memories experience, and from experience art and science. Experience knows that something is so; art knows why, and so can teach. Wisdom is knowledge of causes and principles, and the more theoretical a knowledge, the more it is wisdom.

@p
All human beings by nature desire to know. A sign of this is the delight we take in our senses: even apart from their use, we love them for their own sake, and above all the sense of sight. Not only when we mean to act, but even when we mean to do nothing, we prefer seeing, one might say, to everything else. The reason is that of all the senses sight most makes us know things, and shows us many differences between them.
!note The first sentence of the Metaphysics. "To know" (εἰδέναι) is the same verb as "to see" in its perfect form: to know is to have seen. Aristotle draws the argument from the love of sight.

@p
@greek φύσει μὲν οὖν αἴσθησιν ἔχοντα γίγνεται τὰ ζῷα
Animals are born with sensation by nature. In some of them sensation gives rise to memory, in others not. That is why those that can remember are more intelligent and more apt at learning than those that cannot. Those that cannot hear sounds, such as the bee and any other kind of animal like it, are intelligent without being able to learn; those that have this sense of hearing besides memory can be taught. The other animals live by appearances and memories, and have only a little share of experience; but the human race lives also by art and by reasoning.

@p
@greek γίγνεται δʼ ἐκ τῆς μνήμης ἐμπειρία
In human beings, experience comes from memory: many memories of the same thing amount in the end to the power of a single experience. Experience seems very like knowledge and art, but in fact knowledge and art come to human beings through experience. For "experience made art", as Polus says, and rightly, "but inexperience, luck". Art comes about when, out of many notions gained by experience, one universal judgment arises about things alike. To judge that when Callias had this disease this remedy helped him, and Socrates too, and so in many single cases, is a matter of experience. But to judge that it helped all persons of a certain kind, marked off as one class, when they had this disease (the phlegmatic, say, or the bilious, when burning with fever) is a matter of art.
!note Polus is the student of the rhetorician Gorgias who appears in Plato's Gorgias; the saying is quoted there (448c).

@p
@greek πρὸς μὲν οὖν τὸ πράττειν ἐμπειρία τέχνης
For action, experience seems no worse than art, and we even see people of experience succeed better than those who have the theory without the experience. The reason is that experience is knowledge of particulars, and art of universals; and actions and productions are all about the particular. The physician does not cure "man", except incidentally, but Callias or Socrates or someone else called by a particular name, who happens to be a man. So if someone has the theory without the experience, and knows the universal but not the particular within it, he will often fail to cure, since it is the particular that has to be cured. Still, we think that knowing and understanding belong to art rather than to experience, and we take those who have an art to be wiser than those who have only experience, since wisdom in every case goes with knowing. This is because the one kind knows the cause and the other does not. People of experience know that a thing is so, but not why; the others know the why, the cause. That is why we think the master-craftsmen in each craft more to be honoured than the manual workers: they know more truly and are wiser, because they know the causes of what is done. (The manual workers we think like some lifeless things, which act, but act without knowing what they do, as fire burns; the lifeless things do each of their work by a natural tendency, the workers by habit.) So we think them wiser not because they can act, but because they have the account and know the causes. And in general it is a sign of the one who knows, as against the one who does not, that he can teach. So we think art is knowledge more truly than experience is: those who have an art can teach, and those who have only experience cannot.
!note The sentence on the manual workers, in parentheses, Ross thinks was probably added later, in Aristotle's school.

@p
@greek ἔτι δὲ τῶν αἰσθήσεων οὐδεμίαν ἡγούμεθα εἶναι σοφίαν
Again, we do not regard any of the senses as wisdom, though they give the most authoritative knowledge of particulars. But they do not tell us the why of anything: they do not tell us why fire is hot, only that it is hot. At first, then, whoever discovered any art beyond the common perceptions was naturally admired by people, not only because something in his discoveries was useful, but because he was thought wise and superior to the rest. As more arts were discovered, some for the necessities of life and some for its leisure, the discoverers of the latter were always thought wiser than those of the former, because their kinds of knowledge were not aimed at use. So when all such arts had been established, the sciences that aim neither at pleasure nor at the necessities of life were discovered, and first in the places where people first had leisure. That is why the mathematical arts first arose in Egypt: there the priestly class was allowed leisure.

@p
@greek εἴρηται μὲν οὖν ἐν τοῖς ἠθικοῖς
We have said in the Ethics what the difference is between art, science and the other things of this kind. The point of the present discussion is this: everyone supposes that what is called wisdom is about the first causes and principles. So, as was said before, the person of experience is thought wiser than those who have any sensation whatever, the person with an art wiser than those with experience, the master-craftsman wiser than the manual worker, and the theoretical kinds of knowledge more of the nature of wisdom than the productive. It is clear, then, that wisdom is knowledge about certain principles and causes.
!note The Ethics: the Nicomachean Ethics, Book VI, chapters 3 to 7, where art, science, practical wisdom, intellect and wisdom are told apart.

@ch Metaph.I.2 | What wisdom is: the science of the first causes
From what we think of the wise, wisdom must be the most universal, exact and authoritative knowledge, sought for its own sake: knowledge of first principles and causes, among them the good. Philosophy began in wonder, as a free science, not for use. It is the most divine science, both because God would have it and because it is about God; it ends, unlike our first wonder, in knowing why things must be as they are.

@p
Since this is the knowledge we are seeking, we must inquire what kind of causes and principles they are whose knowledge is wisdom. If one takes the notions we have about the wise, the answer may become clearer. We suppose, first, that the wise person knows all things, as far as possible, without having knowledge of each of them one by one. Next, that the one who can know things that are hard and not easy for a human being to know is wise (perceiving is common to everyone, and so it is easy and nothing wise). Again, that in every branch of knowledge the one who is more exact, and better able to teach the causes, is wiser. And that among the sciences, the one chosen for its own sake and for the sake of knowing is more of the nature of wisdom than the one chosen for its results; and the ruling science more than the subordinate one. For the wise person must not be ordered but must order, and must not obey another, but the less wise must obey him.

@p
@greek τὰς μὲν οὖν ὑπολήψεις τοιαύτας καὶ τοσαύτας ἔχομεν
Such and so many are the notions we have about wisdom and the wise. Of these, knowing all things must belong to the one who has universal knowledge in the highest degree, since he knows in a way all that falls under it. And these things, the most universal, are the hardest for human beings to know, since they are furthest from the senses. The most exact of the sciences are those most concerned with first things: those built on fewer principles are more exact than those that take in additions, as arithmetic is more exact than geometry. The science that studies causes is also the more able to teach, since those who teach are those who state the causes of each thing. And knowing and understanding for their own sake belong most to the science of what is most knowable. For whoever chooses knowing for its own sake will choose most of all the knowledge that is most truly knowledge, and that is the knowledge of what is most knowable; and the first things and the causes are most knowable, since it is through them and from them that the rest are known, and not they through what falls under them. The science that most rules, and rules more than any subordinate science, is the one that knows for what end each thing is to be done; and that end is the good of each thing, and in general the best in the whole of nature.

@p
@greek ἐξ ἁπάντων οὖν τῶν εἰρημένων ἐπὶ τὴν αὐτὴν ἐπιστήμην
By every test we have named, then, the name we are seeking, wisdom, falls to the same science. It must be a science that studies the first principles and causes; for the good, that for the sake of which, is one of the causes.

@p
@greek ὅτι δʼ οὐ ποιητική
That it is not a science of making is clear from the first who philosophised. It is through wonder that human beings now begin, and first began, to philosophise. At first they wondered at the puzzles close at hand; then, going forward little by little, they raised puzzles about greater things, such as what happens to the moon, the sun and the stars, and how the universe came to be. Someone who is puzzled and wonders thinks himself ignorant; that is why the lover of myths is in a way a lover of wisdom, since a myth is made of wonders. So if they philosophised to escape ignorance, it is plain that they pursued knowing for the sake of understanding, and not for any use. What happened bears this out: it was when nearly everything needed for life, ease and pastime was at hand that this kind of understanding began to be sought. Clearly, then, we do not seek it for any other use. As we call a man free who exists for his own sake and not for another's, so this alone of the sciences is free, since it alone is for its own sake.
!note Compare Plato, Theaetetus 155d: "wonder is the feeling of a philosopher, and philosophy begins in wonder."

@p
@greek διὸ καὶ δικαίως ἂν οὐκ ἀνθρωπίνη νομίζοιτο
That is why its possession might rightly be thought more than human; for in many ways human nature is in bondage, so that, as Simonides says, "God alone could have this prize", and it is not fitting for a man not to seek the knowledge that suits him. If there is anything in what the poets say, and the divine is by nature jealous, it would be jealous here most of all, and all who excelled in this would be unfortunate. But the divine cannot be jealous (rather, as the proverb says, "bards tell many a lie"), and no other science should be thought more honourable than this one. For the most divine science is also the most honourable, and this science alone is most divine, in two ways. A science would be divine if God would have it most of all, and so would any science that is about divine things. This science alone is both: God is thought by everyone to be among the causes of all things and a principle, and such a science God alone could have, or God above all others. All the sciences, then, are more necessary than this one, but none is better.
!note Plato too denies that the divine is jealous (Phaedrus 247a, Timaeus 29e): "he was good, and in the good no jealousy ever arises about anything."

@p
@greek δεῖ μέντοι πως καταστῆναι τὴν κτῆσιν αὐτῆς
Yet having it must in a way bring us to the opposite of where our inquiry began. As we said, all begin by wondering whether things are so, as people wonder at self-moving puppets before they see the cause, or at the solstices, or at the incommensurability of the diagonal; for it seems wonderful to everyone that something cannot be measured by even the smallest unit. But we must end in the opposite and, as the proverb says, the better state, as happens in these cases too once one has learned: nothing would make a geometer wonder so much as if the diagonal turned out to be commensurable. We have said, then, what the nature of the science we are seeking is, and what mark our search and our whole inquiry must reach.

@ch Metaph.I.3 | The four causes, and the first philosophers on matter
The causes are spoken of in four ways: the substance, what it is to be; the matter, the underlying thing; the source of motion; and that for the sake of which, the good. The first philosophers saw only the material cause (water for Thales, air, fire, the four elements, Anaxagoras' infinitely many). The facts themselves then drove them to seek the source of motion, and at last intellect, as the cause of order and beauty.

@p
Clearly we must acquire knowledge of the first causes; for we say we know each thing when we think we know its first cause. Causes are spoken of in four ways. One of them we say is the substance, what it is to be (for the why is traced back finally to the account of the thing, and the first why is a cause and principle); another is the matter, the underlying thing; a third, the source of motion; and the fourth, the cause opposite to this, that for the sake of which and the good (for this is the end of all coming to be and motion). We have studied these sufficiently in our work on nature. Still, let us take with us those who before us came to the study of what is and philosophised about the truth. Plainly they too speak of certain principles and causes; so going over their views will help our present inquiry. Either we shall find some other kind of cause, or we shall trust the ones we have just named the more.
!flag In one of these we mean the substance, i.e. the essence | Ross's "essence" stands for a phrase of Aristotle's own making, τὸ τί ἦν εἶναι, literally "the what it was to be", the being of a thing that its definition states. The modern version says "what it is to be" throughout, and keeps "essence" for nothing else.
!note The work on nature is the Physics, Book II, chapters 3 and 7, where the four causes are first set out.

@p
@greek τῶν δὴ πρώτων φιλοσοφησάντων οἱ πλεῖστοι
Of the first to philosophise, most thought that principles in the form of matter were the only principles of all things. That of which all things consist, from which they first come to be and into which they are at last destroyed, the substance remaining while its attributes change: this they call the element and the principle of the things that are. That is why they think that nothing comes to be or perishes, since such a nature is always preserved. In the same way we do not say that Socrates comes to be without qualification when he becomes handsome or musical, nor that he perishes when he loses these states, because the underlying thing, Socrates himself, remains. So with everything else, they say: there must be some nature, either one or more than one, from which the rest come to be while it is preserved.
!flag alone subsisted in the species of matter | Taylor's "species of matter" renders ἐν ὕλης εἴδει word for word; the sense is "in the form of matter", that is, material principles only. Here εἶδος has no technical sense.

@p
@greek τὸ μέντοι πλῆθος καὶ τὸ εἶδος τῆς τοιαύτης ἀρχῆς
They do not all agree, though, about the number and kind of such a principle. Thales, the founder of this kind of philosophy, says it is water; that is why he declared that the earth rests on water. He took the notion, perhaps, from seeing that the nourishment of all things is moist, and that heat itself comes from the moist and lives by it (and that from which things come to be is their principle). He took the notion from this, and from the fact that the seeds of all things have a moist nature, and water is the principle of the nature of moist things. Some think that the ancients too, who lived long before our time and first wrote of the gods, held this view of nature. They made Ocean and Tethys the parents of coming to be, and the oath of the gods to be by water, which the poets call Styx; for what is oldest is most honoured, and what one swears by is most honoured. Whether this opinion about nature is old and ancient may perhaps be unclear; but Thales at least is said to have spoken so about the first cause. (No one would think fit to rank Hippo among these, because of the poverty of his thought.)
!note Ocean and Tethys: Homer, Iliad XIV.201 and 246. Plato's Cratylus (402b) and Theaetetus (152e, 180d) also cite them as the parents of all things, with Heraclitus' flux.

@p
@greek Ἀναξιμένης δὲ ἀέρα καὶ Διογένης
Anaximenes and Diogenes make air prior to water, and the most first of the simple bodies; Hippasus of Metapontum and Heraclitus of Ephesus make it fire; Empedocles the four, adding earth as a fourth to those named. These, he says, always remain and do not come to be, except in being more or fewer, as they are gathered into one and separated out of one. Anaxagoras of Clazomenae, older than Empedocles but later in his works, says the principles are infinite in number. For he says that nearly all things made of like parts, such as water or fire, come to be and perish in this way, by being gathered and separated only, and do not otherwise come to be or perish, but remain for ever.

@p
@greek ἐκ μὲν οὖν τούτων μόνην τις αἰτίαν
From these facts one might think the only cause is the one called the cause in the form of matter. But as they went forward, the facts themselves opened the way for them and forced them to inquire. However true it may be that all coming to be and perishing is out of some one or more things, why does it happen, and what is the cause? The underlying thing does not make itself change: neither the wood nor the bronze is the cause of its own change; the wood does not make a bed nor the bronze a statue, but something else is the cause of the change. To seek this is to seek the other principle, as we should say, the source of motion. Those who at the very beginning took up this inquiry, and said the underlying thing was one, were not troubled by this. But some of those who say it is one, as if beaten by this question, say that the one, and the whole of nature, is unmoved, not only in coming to be and perishing (that is an ancient view, and all agreed in it) but in every other change too; and this is peculiar to them. Of those who said the whole was one, none happened to see a cause of this kind, except perhaps Parmenides, and he only in so far as he posits not only one but in a way two causes. Those who make more than one element can more easily speak of it, those for instance who make the hot and the cold, or fire and earth: they treat fire as having a nature that moves things, and water, earth and the like as the contrary.

@p
@greek μετὰ δὲ τούτους καὶ τὰς τοιαύτας ἀρχάς
After these men, and such principles as theirs, since these were not enough to generate the nature of things, people were again forced, as we said, by the truth itself to seek the next principle. For that things are and come to be well and beautifully, some of them, is not likely to be caused by fire or earth or any such element, nor likely that those thinkers supposed so; nor again would it be right to hand over so great a matter to chance and luck. So when someone said that intellect is present, in nature as in animals, as the cause of the world and of all its order, he seemed like a sober man beside the idle talk of those before him. We know for certain that Anaxagoras took up these arguments, but Hermotimus of Clazomenae is said to have spoken of them first. Those who thought so posited a principle of things that is at once the cause of their beauty and the kind of cause from which motion comes to things.
!flag When one man said, then, that reason was present | Ross's "reason" stands for νοῦς, "intellect" (Taylor's word), the highest power of knowing and, for Anaxagoras, the cause that orders all things. The Platonists read this praise of Anaxagoras beside Socrates' in the Phaedo (97b-99c), where Socrates hopes for an intellect that orders everything for the best, and is disappointed.

@ch Metaph.I.4 | Love and strife; the atomists
Hesiod and Parmenides may have looked for a moving cause first, in love. Since there is evil as well as good in nature, Empedocles made two, love and strife, in a way the first to make the good and the bad principles. But these thinkers used their causes like untrained boxers, by chance: Anaxagoras brings in intellect only when at a loss. Leucippus and Democritus made the full and the empty the elements, and their differences of shape, order and position the causes of everything else; motion they neglected.

@p
One might suspect that Hesiod was the first to look for such a thing, or anyone else who put love or desire among the things that are as a principle, as Parmenides did. For he, building up the coming to be of the universe, says: "First of all the gods she devised Love." And Hesiod says: "First of all things was Chaos, and then broad-breasted Earth, and Love, who is foremost among all the immortals." This implies that among the things that are there must be some cause that will move things and bring them together. How these should be ranked as to who was first, let us decide later.
!note The lines are from Parmenides' poem (fragment 13 in Diels) and Hesiod's Theogony 116-120. Plato's Symposium (178b) quotes the same lines of both, in Phaedrus' praise of Love as the oldest of the gods.

@p
@greek ἐπεὶ δὲ καὶ τἀναντία τοῖς ἀγαθοῖς ἐνόντα
But since the contraries of good things were also seen to be present in nature, not only order and beauty but also disorder and ugliness, and bad things more than good, and base things more than beautiful, so another thinker brought in friendship and strife, each the cause of one of these. For if one follows Empedocles and takes him by his meaning, and not by his lisping words, one will find that friendship is the cause of good things, and strife of bad. So if one said that Empedocles in a way speaks of the bad and the good as principles, and was the first to do so, one would perhaps be right, if indeed the cause of all good things is the good itself.

@p
@greek οὗτοι μὲν οὖν, ὥσπερ λέγομεν, καὶ μέχρι τούτου
These thinkers, as we say, plainly grasped two of the causes we have marked out in our work on nature, the matter and the source of motion, and only so far; and dimly, and with no clearness, but as untrained men do in fights. They rush about and often strike good blows, but not from knowledge; and so these thinkers do not seem to know what they are saying, for they plainly make almost no use of their causes, or only a little. Anaxagoras uses intellect as a god from the machine to make the world: when he is at a loss for the cause why something is of necessity, he drags it in, but in everything else he names anything rather than intellect as the cause of what comes to be. Empedocles uses his causes more than this, but neither enough nor consistently. In many places, at least, friendship for him separates and strife unites. When the whole is divided into its elements by strife, fire is gathered into one, and so is each of the other elements; but when they come together again into one under friendship, the parts must be separated out of each element again.
!note "A god from the machine": the crane of the Athenian theatre, which brought a god on stage to resolve a plot. Socrates' complaint against Anaxagoras in the Phaedo (98b-c) is the same.

@p
@greek Ἐμπεδοκλῆς μὲν οὖν παρὰ τοὺς πρότερον πρῶτος
Empedocles, then, unlike those before him, was the first to divide this cause, positing not one source of motion but two different and contrary ones. Again, he was the first to speak of four elements in the form of matter; yet he does not use them as four, but as two only: fire by itself, and its opposites, earth, air and water, as one nature. One may see this by studying his verses.

@p
@greek οὗτος μὲν οὖν, ὥσπερ λέγομεν, οὕτω τε καὶ τοσαύτας
He, then, as we say, spoke of the principles in this way and made them so many. Leucippus and his companion Democritus say the elements are the full and the empty, calling the one what is and the other what is not: the full and solid is what is, the empty and rare is what is not (which is why they say that what is no more is than what is not, since the solid no more is than the empty); and they make these the causes of things as matter. And as those who make the underlying substance one generate everything else by its attributes, positing the rare and the dense as the principles of the attributes, in the same way these men say that the differences are the causes of everything else. These differences, they say, are three: shape, order and position. For they say that what is differs only by "rhythm", "touching" and "turning"; and of these rhythm is shape, touching is order, and turning is position. A differs from N in shape, AN from NA in order, and Z from H in position. As to motion, where it comes from or how it belongs to things, these too, like the others, lazily let it go.
!note The letters are the Greek Α, Ν, Ζ and Η. The atoms differ as letters do: in shape, in their order, and in which way they are turned.

@p
@greek περὶ μὲν οὖν τῶν δύο αἰτιῶν
About the two causes, then, as we say, the inquiry of those before us seems to have gone so far.

@ch Metaph.I.5 | The Pythagoreans and the Eleatics
The Pythagoreans, trained in mathematics, took the principles of numbers for the principles of all things: number is both the matter and the attributes of things, and its elements are the even and the odd, the unlimited and the limited. Some of them set out ten pairs of contraries. The Eleatics, who make the whole one, belong to another inquiry: Parmenides meant one in account, Melissus one in matter, Xenophanes looked to the whole heaven and called the One God. The chapter ends with what can be learned from all of them.

@p
In their time, and before them, the so-called Pythagoreans took up mathematics and were the first to advance it. Being brought up in it, they thought its principles were the principles of all things. Since of these principles numbers are by nature first, and they thought they saw in numbers many likenesses to the things that are and come to be, more than in fire, earth and water (such and such an attribute of numbers being justice, another soul and intellect, another opportunity, and so with nearly everything else); and since, again, they saw in numbers the attributes and ratios of the musical scales; since, then, everything else seemed in its whole nature to be modelled on numbers, and numbers seemed to be the first things in all of nature, they took the elements of numbers to be the elements of all things, and the whole heaven to be a harmony and a number.

And whatever agreements they could show in numbers and scales with the attributes and parts of the heaven and its whole arrangement, they collected and fitted together; and if there was a gap anywhere, they eagerly made additions so that their whole system would hang together. For example, since ten is thought to be complete and to take in the whole nature of numbers, they say that the bodies moving in the heaven are ten; and since only nine are visible, they make the counter-earth the tenth. But the purpose of our review is to learn from these too what they posit as principles, and how these fall under the causes we have named. They too plainly think that number is the principle, both as matter for the things that are and as their attributes and states; and that the elements of number are the even and the odd, the even being unlimited and the odd limited; and that the one comes from both of these (for it is both even and odd), and number from the one; and that the whole heaven, as was said, is numbers.

@p
@greek ἕτεροι δὲ τῶν αὐτῶν τούτων τὰς ἀρχὰς δέκα λέγουσιν
Others of this same school say there are ten principles, which they set in two columns: limit and unlimited, odd and even, one and many, right and left, male and female, at rest and moving, straight and curved, light and darkness, good and bad, square and oblong. Alcmaeon of Croton seems to have thought in this way too, and either he took the view from them or they from him; for he spoke like them. He says that most human things go in pairs, meaning not definite contraries, as the Pythagoreans do, but any that come to hand, such as white and black, sweet and bitter, good and bad, great and small. He threw out undefined suggestions about the rest; but the Pythagoreans declared how many the contraries are and which they are.
!note Ross's Greek of 1924 brackets the words on Alcmaeon's age ("he lived when Pythagoras was old") as a later addition; the modern version leaves them out, as Ross's translation does.

@p
@greek παρὰ μὲν οὖν τούτων ἀμφοῖν τοσοῦτον ἔστι λαβεῖν
From both of these, then, one can learn this much: that the contraries are the principles of things. How many they are, and which, one can learn from one of the two. But how they can be brought under the causes we have named, these thinkers have not clearly worked out. They seem to rank the elements in the form of matter; for they say that substance is composed and moulded out of them, as out of parts within it.

@p
@greek τῶν μὲν οὖν παλαιῶν καὶ πλείω λεγόντων
From these, then, one may see well enough the meaning of the ancients who said the elements of nature are more than one. But there are some who spoke of the whole as one nature, though not all in the same way, either in how well they spoke or in how they spoke of nature. The discussion of them does not belong at all to our present inquiry into causes. For they do not, like some of the natural philosophers, posit that what is is one and yet generate it out of the one as out of matter; they speak in another way. Those add motion, since they generate the whole; these say it is unmoved. Still, this much is proper to our present inquiry. Parmenides seems to grasp what is one in account, Melissus what is one in matter; which is why the one says it is limited and the other unlimited. Xenophanes, the first of them to make all one (Parmenides is said to have been his pupil), made nothing clear, and does not seem to have touched the nature of either of these; but looking up at the whole heaven, he says that the One is God. These, as we said, must be left aside for the present inquiry, two of them entirely, as being a little too crude, Xenophanes and Melissus. But Parmenides seems to speak with more insight. Claiming that besides what is, what is not is nothing, he thinks that of necessity what is is one and nothing else is (we have spoken of this more clearly in our work on nature). But being forced to follow the appearances, and supposing that what is is one in account but more than one in perception, he posits two causes and two principles, the hot and the cold, that is, fire and earth; and of these he ranks the hot with what is, and the other with what is not.

@greek ἐκ μὲν οὖν τῶν εἰρημένων καὶ παρὰ τῶν συνηδρευκότων
From what has been said, then, and from the wise men who have now sat in council with us, we have this much. From the first, that the principle is bodily (water, fire and the like are bodies), one bodily principle for some, more than one for others, but both putting them in the form of matter; and from some, that there is this cause and besides it the source of motion, one for some and two for others. Down to the Italians, and apart from them, the others spoke of these things rather obscurely, except that, as we said, they used two causes, and of these some make the second, the source of motion, one and others two. The Pythagoreans spoke of two principles in the same way, but added this much, which is their own: that the limited and the unlimited and the one are not attributes of some other nature, such as fire or earth or anything of that kind, but that the unlimited itself and the one itself are the substance of the things they are predicated of. That is why number is the substance of all things. On these matters they declared themselves in this way. About what a thing is they began to speak and to define, but treated it too simply. For they defined superficially, and took the first thing to which a given definition belongs to be the substance of the thing; as if one thought that double and the number two are the same, because two is the first thing to which double belongs. But to be double and to be two are surely not the same; if they were, the one would be many, which they did conclude. This much, then, may be learned from those before, and from the others.
!note The Italians: the Pythagoreans, whose school was in southern Italy, at Croton and Metapontum.

@ch Metaph.I.6 | Plato
Plato followed the Pythagoreans in most things, but had his own views. From Heraclitus he took that sensible things are in flux and cannot be known; from Socrates, the search for definitions; so the definitions must be of other things, the Forms, in which sensible things participate. Between the two are the objects of mathematics. The elements of the Forms are the great and the small as matter and the One as substance, and the Forms are numbers. Aristotle concludes that Plato used only two causes, what it is to be and matter, and gave the cause of good and of evil to his two elements.

@p
After the philosophies we have named came the work of Plato, which in most things followed these thinkers, but had its own features beside the philosophy of the Italians. In his youth he first became familiar with Cratylus and the views of Heraclitus, that all sensible things are always flowing and there is no knowledge of them; and he held these views later too. Socrates was busy with ethics and not at all with nature as a whole, but in ethics he sought the universal, and was the first to fix his thought on definitions. Plato accepted him, but for this reason held that definition is about other things and not about sensible ones: the common definition cannot be of any of the sensible things, since they are always changing. Things of this other kind he called Ideas, and sensible things, he said, are beside these and are all named after them; for the many things that share the name of the Forms are by participation in them. The name "participation" was all he changed: the Pythagoreans say things are by imitation of numbers, and Plato by participation, changing the name. But what the participation or imitation of the Forms might be, they left to be inquired into in common.
!note The Platonists answered that participation is not a word only: Proclus, on the Parmenides, and Syrianus, on Books Μ and Ν of this work, set out how the Forms are present in what participates in them while remaining in themselves.

@p
@greek ἔτι δὲ παρὰ τὰ αἰσθητὰ καὶ τὰ εἴδη
Further, besides sensible things and the Forms, he says there are the objects of mathematics, between them, differing from sensible things in being eternal and unmoved, and from the Forms in that there are many alike, while each Form itself is one only.

@p
@greek ἐπεὶ δʼ αἴτια τὰ εἴδη τοῖς ἄλλοις
Since the Forms are the causes of everything else, he thought their elements were the elements of all things. As matter, the great and the small are principles; as substance, the One. For out of the great and the small, by participation in the One, come the numbers. In saying that the One is substance, and not something else that is said to be one, he spoke like the Pythagoreans; and in saying that the numbers are the causes of the substance of everything else, he spoke as they did. But to make a dyad instead of the unlimited as one, and to make the unlimited out of the great and the small, is his own; and so is this, that he puts the numbers apart from sensible things, while they say the things themselves are numbers, and do not place the objects of mathematics between. His putting the One and the numbers apart from things, unlike the Pythagoreans, and his bringing in the Forms, came from his inquiry into accounts (for those before him had no share in dialectic); and his making the other nature a dyad came from his thinking that the numbers, except the first, could be neatly generated out of it, as out of a kind of plastic stuff.

@greek καίτοι συμβαίνει γʼ ἐναντίως
Yet what happens is the contrary; it is not reasonable so. They make many things out of the matter, while the form generates only once. But what we see is that one table comes from one matter, while the one who brings the form, though he is one, makes many. So too with the male and the female: the female is filled by one mating, but the male fills many. Yet these are likenesses of those principles.

@p
@greek Πλάτων μὲν οὖν περὶ τῶν ζητουμένων οὕτω διώρισεν
Plato, then, settled the questions we are asking in this way. It is clear from what has been said that he used only two causes, the cause of what a thing is and the cause in the form of matter: the Forms are the causes of what everything else is, and the One of what the Forms are. And it is clear what the underlying matter is, of which the Forms are said in the case of sensible things, and the One in the case of the Forms: that it is the dyad, the great and the small. Further, he gave the cause of good and of evil to the elements, one to each, as we say some of the earlier philosophers too tried to do, such as Empedocles and Anaxagoras.
!note The Platonists denied that the Good is only one of two elements: for them it is the first principle of all, the One itself, beyond being (Republic 509b), and Plato's dyad is not evil. Syrianus answers Aristotle on this in his commentary on Book Ν.

@ch Metaph.I.7 | The review summed up
None of the earlier thinkers named a cause beyond the four. Some spoke of matter, some of the source of motion; what it is to be was spoken of chiefly by the friends of the Forms; the end they named, but not as an end. So the four causes are rightly set out, and next their views are to be examined.

@taylor 6
@p
We have gone over briefly and in summary who said what about the principles and the truth, and in what way. Still, we have this much from them: that of those who speak of principle and cause, none has named any beyond those marked out in our work on nature, but all plainly touch on them, though dimly. Some speak of the principle as matter, whether they posit one or more, and whether they posit it as a body or as bodiless: Plato, for instance, the great and the small; the Italians, the unlimited; Empedocles, fire, earth, water and air; Anaxagoras, the infinity of things made of like parts. These have all grasped this kind of cause, and so have all who speak of air, fire or water, or something denser than fire and rarer than air; for some have said the first element is of that kind.

@p
@greek οὗτοι μὲν οὖν ταύτης τῆς αἰτίας ἥψαντο μόνον
These, then, touched on this cause only; but others on the source of motion, those for instance who make friendship and strife, or intellect, or love a principle. What it is to be, the substance, no one has stated clearly; those who posit the Forms come nearest to it. For they do not take the Forms to be the matter of sensible things, nor the One to be the matter of the Forms, nor take them as the source of motion (they say they are rather causes of rest and of being unmoved); but they offer the Forms as what each of the other things is, and the One as what the Forms are. That for the sake of which actions, changes and motions take place, they say is a cause in a way, but not in this way, not in the way it is by nature a cause. Those who speak of intellect or friendship posit these causes as good, but they do not speak as if anything were or came to be for their sake, but as if motions came from them. In the same way those who say the One or what is is the good say it is a cause of substance, but not that anything is or comes to be for its sake. So it turns out that in a way they both say and do not say that the good is a cause; they say it not without qualification but incidentally.
!note "What it is to be" is the formal cause; Aristotle grants that Plato's Forms were the first attempt at it, but holds that the form must be in the thing, not apart from it (I.9 and Book Ζ).

@p
@greek ὅτι μὲν οὖν ὀρθῶς διώρισται περὶ τῶν αἰτίων
That we have marked out the causes rightly, how many they are and of what kind, all these thinkers seem to bear witness, by not being able to touch on any other cause. Besides, it is plain that the principles must be sought either all in this way or in some one of these ways. Next let us go through the possible difficulties about how each of them has spoken, and how each stands concerning the principles.

@ch Metaph.I.8 | Against the materialists and the Pythagoreans
Those who make one bodily element the principle leave out the bodiless, the source of motion and what things are, and cannot say which element is first. Empedocles has these troubles and more; Anaxagoras, read charitably, means the One and the indefinite. The Pythagoreans use principles fit for higher things to explain only the sensible heaven, and say nothing of how there is motion, weight or lightness, or how number is the cause of the things in the heaven if it is also those things.

@taylor 7
@p
Those, then, who posit that the whole is one, and that there is one nature as matter, and that bodily and having size, plainly go wrong in many ways. They posit elements of bodies only, not of bodiless things, though there are bodiless things too. In trying to state the causes of coming to be and perishing, and in giving an account of the nature of all things, they do away with the cause of motion. Further, they go wrong in not positing the substance, what a thing is, as the cause of anything; and besides, in lightly calling any of the simple bodies except earth the principle, without considering how they come to be out of one another, fire, water, earth and air. Some come to be out of one another by combining, others by separating, and this makes the greatest difference to which is prior and which posterior. In one way, the most elementary of all would seem to be the first thing out of which things come to be by combining, and that would be the body of finest parts and the most subtle. So those who posit fire as the principle would speak most in agreement with this argument. And each of the others too agrees that the element of bodies is of this kind: at least none of those who said there is one element thought fit to say it is earth, plainly because of the size of its parts; while each of the other three has found a judge, some saying fire, some water, some air. Yet why do they not also say earth, as most people do? For people say all things are earth; and Hesiod too says that earth came first of bodies, so ancient and common has the belief happened to be. By this argument, then, no one would speak rightly who says it is any of these except fire, or who posits something denser than air and rarer than water. But if what is later in coming to be is prior in nature, and what is worked up and compounded is later in coming to be, the contrary of what we have said would be true: water would be prior to air, and earth to water.
!flag who consider the universe as one, and as being one certain nature, as matter | Taylor's words are close to the Greek, but "certain" is his: the Greek says simply "one nature as matter". The modern version keeps the plainer phrase.

@p
@greek περὶ μὲν οὖν τῶν μίαν τιθεμένων αἰτίαν
About those who posit one cause of the kind we described, let this be said. The same holds if one posits more of them, as Empedocles says the matter of things is four bodies. For the same consequences must meet him, some the same as have been said, others his own. We see the bodies coming to be out of one another, so that the same body does not always remain fire or earth (we have spoken of this in our works on nature). And about the cause of things' being moved, whether one should posit one or two, one must think he has spoken neither rightly nor altogether reasonably. In general, those who speak in this way must do away with change of quality; for on their view the cold will not come from the hot nor the hot from the cold. For then what would undergo the contraries themselves, and what would the one nature be that becomes fire and water? He does not say.
!note Ross thought the last three sentences (989a26-30) probably a gloss; his Greek of 1924 keeps them.

@p
@greek Ἀναξαγόραν δʼ εἴ τις ὑπολάβοι
If one supposed that Anaxagoras speaks of two elements, one would suppose it in full accord with an argument that he did not himself work out, but that he would have had to follow if someone had led him. It is absurd, indeed, in other ways too to say that at the beginning all things were mixed: because they must first have existed unmixed; because it is not natural for any chance thing to be mixed with any chance thing; and besides, because attributes and accidents would then be separable from substances (since the same things that are mixed are separable). Yet if one followed him, putting together what he means, he would perhaps appear to speak in a more modern way. For when nothing had been separated out, plainly nothing true could be said of that substance. I mean, for instance, that it was neither white nor black, nor grey nor of any other colour, but of necessity colourless, since otherwise it would have had one of these colours. Likewise, by the same argument, it was without flavour, and without anything else of the kind. It could not be of any quality, nor of any quantity, nor any something; for then one of the forms said in part would have belonged to it, and that is impossible when all are mixed; for it would already have been separated out. But he says all things are mixed except intellect, and that this alone is unmixed and pure. From this it follows that he must say the principles are the one (for this is simple and unmixed) and the other, such as we posit the indefinite to be before it is defined and shares in some form. So he speaks neither rightly nor clearly, but he means something like what was said later and what now appears more plainly.
!note Anaxagoras' fragment 12 (Diels): "Intellect is unlimited and self-ruling, and is mixed with no thing, but is alone by itself." Aristotle reads his two principles as the One and the indefinite, close to Plato's One and dyad.

@p
@greek ἀλλὰ γὰρ οὗτοι μὲν τοῖς περὶ γένεσιν λόγοις
But these thinkers are at home only in the arguments about coming to be, perishing and motion; for it is nearly only of this kind of substance that they seek the principles and the causes. But those who make their study about all the things that are, and posit some things that are as sensible and others as not sensible, plainly study both kinds. So one might spend more time on them, to see what they say well and what not, toward the inquiry now before us.

@p
@greek οἱ μὲν οὖν καλούμενοι Πυθαγόρειοι
The so-called Pythagoreans use principles and elements stranger than those of the natural philosophers. The reason is that they did not take them from sensible things; for the objects of mathematics are without motion, except those of astronomy. Yet everything they discuss and work at is about nature. They generate the heaven, and they watch what happens about its parts, attributes and workings, and use up the principles and the causes on these, as if they agreed with the others, the natural philosophers, that what is is just what is sensible and what the so-called heaven contains. But the causes and principles they name are, as we said, enough to lead up even to the higher kinds of things that are, and fit those better than the accounts of nature. Yet they do not say at all how there will be motion, if only limit and unlimited, odd and even, are assumed; nor how without motion and change there can be coming to be and perishing, or the doings of the bodies that move through the heaven. Further, even if one granted them that size comes from these, or this were shown, still how will some bodies be light and others heavy? From what they assume and say, they speak no more of mathematical bodies than of sensible ones. That is why they have said nothing at all about fire or earth or other such bodies, I suppose because they have nothing to say of sensible things in particular.

@p
@greek ἔτι δὲ πῶς δεῖ λαβεῖν
Further, how are we to take it that the attributes of number, and number itself, are causes of what is and comes to be in the heaven from the beginning and now, and yet that there is no other number besides this number out of which the cosmos is composed? When they place opinion and opportunity in one region, and a little above or below injustice and separation or mixture, and offer as proof that each of these is a number, while there already happens to be in that place a crowd of composite bodies, because these attributes of number go with their several places: is this number, which one must take each of these to be, the same number that is in the heaven, or another besides it? Plato says it is another. Yet he too thinks that both these things and their causes are numbers, but the causes are intelligible numbers, and these are sensible.

@ch Metaph.I.9 | Against the Forms
The arguments for the Forms either prove nothing or prove Forms of things the Platonists deny Forms of; the more exact ones lead to Forms of relatives or to the "third man". The Forms explain neither motion nor knowledge nor being; "pattern" and "participation" are empty words and poetic metaphors. If the Forms are numbers, they cannot be causes; the Platonists' account of lines, planes and solids fails; and to seek the elements of all things without distinguishing the senses of being is hopeless. Aristotle speaks here as one of Plato's school ("we").

@taylor 7
@p
About the Pythagoreans, then, let us leave things for now; it is enough to have touched on them so far. As for those who posit the Ideas as causes: first, in seeking to grasp the causes of the things here, they brought in others equal in number to them, as if someone who wanted to count things thought he could not while they were fewer, but counted them after making them more. For the Forms are about equal to, or not fewer than, the things in seeking whose causes they went from these to those. For to each thing there is something of the same name, apart from the substances; and for all the other things there is a one over many, both for the things here and for the eternal ones.
!note In this chapter Aristotle says "we" of the Platonists, as one of them ("the ways in which we prove that the Forms exist"). The same objections, often word for word, return in Book Μ, chapters 4 and 5 (1078b-1080a), and it is there that Syrianus answers them, in his commentary on Books Μ and Ν.

@p
@greek ἔτι δὲ καθʼ οὓς τρόπους δείκνυμεν
Further, of the ways in which we show that the Forms exist, none is convincing. From some, no conclusion necessarily follows; from others, there follow Forms even of things of which we think there are none. For by the arguments from the sciences there will be Forms of all the things there are sciences of; by the one over many, Forms even of negations; and by the argument from thinking of something that has perished, Forms of perishable things, since there is an image of these. Further, of the more exact arguments, some make Ideas of relatives, of which we say there is no kind by itself, and others state the "third man". And in general the arguments for the Forms do away with the things that those who speak of Forms want more than they want the Ideas to be. For it follows that not the dyad is first but number, and the relative prior to what is by itself, and all the other points on which some, following out the opinions about the Ideas, have come into conflict with the principles.

The argument from "equal itself" in Plato's Phaedo (74a-75b) makes such a Form.

@p
@greek ἔτι κατὰ μὲν τὴν ὑπόληψιν καθʼ ἣν εἶναί φαμεν
Further, by the assumption on which we say there are Ideas, there will be Forms not only of substances but of many other things too (for the thought is one not only of substances but of other things, and there are sciences not only of substance but of others, and countless other such consequences follow). But by necessity, and by the opinions held about the Forms, if the Forms are participated in, there must be Ideas of substances only. For they are not participated in incidentally; each Form must be participated in as not said of an underlying thing. I mean, for instance, if something participates in double itself, it participates also in the eternal, but incidentally, since being eternal is incidental to the double. So the Forms will be substance. But the same words signify substance here and there; or what will it mean to say that there is something apart from these, the one over many? And if the form of the Ideas and of what participates in them is the same, there will be something common to them; for why should two be one and the same in the perishable twos and in the many but eternal twos, and not in two itself and a particular two? But if the form is not the same, they will have only the name in common, as if one called both Callias and a piece of wood a man, without seeing anything common to them.

@p
@greek πάντων δὲ μάλιστα διαπορήσειεν ἄν τις
Above all one might ask what the Forms contribute to the eternal sensible things, or to those that come to be and perish. They are not causes of any motion or change in them. Nor do they help at all toward knowledge of the other things (for they are not their substance; else they would be in them), nor toward their being, since they are not in the things that participate in them. If they were, they might perhaps seem to be causes, as white is a cause of whiteness by being mixed in; but this argument, which Anaxagoras first and later Eudoxus and some others used, is very easily upset, for it is easy to gather many impossibilities against such a view. Nor do the other things come from the Forms in any of the usual senses of "from". To say that they are patterns and that the other things participate in them is to speak emptily and to use poetic metaphors. For what is it that works, looking to the Ideas? Anything can be and become like another without being copied from it; so whether Socrates is or is not, someone like Socrates might come to be; and plainly likewise even if Socrates were eternal. There will also be several patterns of the same thing, and so several Forms: of man, for instance, animal and two-footed, and also man himself. Further, the Forms will be patterns not only of sensible things but of Forms themselves, as the genus is a pattern of the species within it; so the same thing will be pattern and image.
!flag to use empty words and poetical metaphors | Ross's words are right; the note is the Platonists'. The "pattern" (παράδειγμα) and the maker "looking to" it are Plato's own, in the Timaeus (28a-29a), where the craftsman of the world looks to the eternal pattern; the Platonists held that this is not a metaphor but the way intellect makes.

@p
@greek ἔτι δόξειεν ἂν ἀδύνατον εἶναι χωρὶς
Further, it would seem impossible for a substance to be apart from that of which it is the substance; so how could the Ideas, being the substances of things, be apart from them? In the Phaedo it is said that the Forms are causes both of being and of coming to be. Yet even if the Forms exist, the things that participate in them do not come to be unless there is something to move them; and many other things come to be, a house or a ring, of which they say there are no Forms. So plainly the other things too can both be and come to be through causes like those of the things just named.
!note Phaedo 100b-101c: "it is by the beautiful that all beautiful things are beautiful". Aristotle's point is that a Form that does not move anything cannot make anything come to be; the Platonists replied that the Forms are causes as patterns, with the maker and the end, not as movers.

@p
@greek ἔτι εἴπερ εἰσὶν ἀριθμοὶ τὰ εἴδη
Further, if the Forms are numbers, how will they be causes? Is it because the things that are are other numbers, this number man, that Socrates, that Callias? Why then are those numbers the causes of these? It will make no difference if the ones are eternal and the others not. But if it is because the things here are ratios of numbers, as a harmony is, plainly there is something of which they are ratios. If this is something, the matter, plainly the numbers themselves too will be ratios of one thing to another. I mean, if Callias is a ratio in numbers of fire, earth, water and air, the Idea too will be a number of certain other underlying things; and man himself, whether or not it is a kind of number, will still be a ratio in numbers of certain things, and not a number; nor will it be any number on this account.

@p
@greek ἔτι ἐκ πολλῶν ἀριθμῶν εἷς ἀριθμὸς γίγνεται
Further, from many numbers one number comes to be; but how does one Form come from Forms? If it is not from the numbers themselves but from the units in them, as in ten thousand, how are the units? If they are alike in kind, many absurdities will follow; and if not alike in kind, neither those in the same number like one another nor all those in different numbers like all, in what will they differ, since they have no attributes? This is neither reasonable nor in keeping with thought. Further, they must set up another kind of number, which arithmetic is about, and all the things some call intermediates; and how are these, or from what principles? Or why will they be between the things here and the things themselves? Further, each of the units in two must come from a prior two; yet that is impossible. Further, why is a number, taken together, one? Further, besides what has been said, if the units are different, they should have spoken like those who say there are four or two elements: each of these calls element not what is common, body, but fire and earth, whether there is something common, body, or not. As it is, they speak as if the One were of like parts, like fire or water. But if so, the numbers will not be substances. Plainly, if there is a One itself and this is a principle, "one" is said in more than one way; otherwise it is impossible.

@p
@greek βουλόμενοι δὲ τὰς οὐσίας ἀνάγειν εἰς τὰς ἀρχὰς
When we want to lead substances back to their principles, we posit lengths as coming from the short and long, a kind of small and great; the plane from the broad and narrow; and the body from the deep and shallow. Yet how will the plane have a line in it, or the solid a line and a plane? For the broad and narrow is a different kind from the deep and shallow. So as number is not in these, since the many and few differ from them, plainly none of the higher will be in the lower. Nor is the broad a genus of the deep; for then the body would be a kind of plane. Further, from what will the points be present in the line? Plato fought against this kind as a geometrical notion; he called it the principle of the line, and often posited the indivisible lines. Yet these must have a limit; so by the argument by which there is a line, there is also a point.

@p
@greek ὅλως δὲ ζητούσης τῆς σοφίας περὶ τῶν φανερῶν
In general, though wisdom seeks the cause of the things that appear, we have let this go (for we say nothing of the cause from which change begins). While we think we are stating their substance, we say there are other substances; but how these are the substances of those, we explain emptily, for "participating", as we said before, is nothing. Nor do the Forms touch at all what we see to be the cause in the sciences, that for the sake of which every intellect and every nature makes things, the cause we say is one of the principles. But mathematics has become philosophy for those of today, though they say it should be studied for the sake of other things. Further, one might take the substance they posit as underlying, as matter, to be too mathematical: to be said of the substance and matter, and to be a difference of it, rather than matter. I mean the great and the small, which are like the rare and the dense the natural philosophers speak of, calling them the first differences of the underlying thing; for these are a kind of excess and defect. And as to motion, if these are motion, plainly the Forms will be moved; and if not, where did it come from? The whole study of nature has been done away with.
!note "Those of today": Plato's successors in the Academy, Speusippus and Xenocrates, for whom mathematics had become the whole of philosophy. Plato himself (Republic 531d, 533b-d) makes mathematics a prelude to dialectic.

@p
@greek ὅ τε δοκεῖ ῥᾴδιον εἶναι
And what is thought to be easy, to show that all things are one, is not done. For by setting out the universal, all things do not become one, but there is a One itself, if one grants everything; and not even this, unless one grants that the universal is a genus, and in some cases this is impossible. Nor is there any account of the things after the numbers, lengths, planes and solids: neither how they are or will be, nor what power they have. These cannot be Forms (for they are not numbers), nor the intermediates (for those are the objects of mathematics), nor the perishable things. They plainly appear to be another, a fourth, kind.

@p
@greek ὅλως τε τὸ τῶν ὄντων ζητεῖν στοιχεῖα μὴ διελόντας
In general, to seek the elements of the things that are without distinguishing them, when they are said in many ways, makes it impossible to find them, especially when one seeks in this way what kind of elements they consist of. From what acting or being acted on, or the straight, consists, surely cannot be found; if at all, only the elements of substances can. So to seek the elements of all the things that are, or to think one has them, is not true. And how could one learn the elements of all things? Plainly one could not already know anything before. As someone learning geometry may know other things before, but knows nothing in advance of what the science is about and what he is about to learn, so too with the rest. So if there is a science of all things, as some say, whoever learns it would know nothing before. Yet all learning is through things known before, all or some, whether by demonstration or by definitions; for the parts of the definition must be known before and be familiar. Learning by induction is likewise. But if the science were born in us, it would be strange that we fail to notice we have the greatest of sciences. Further, how will one know what things are made of, and how will it be made plain? This too is a difficulty; for one might dispute it, as about some syllables: some say ZA is made of S, D and A, while others say it is a different sound and none of the known ones. Further, how could one know the objects of a sense without having the sense? Yet one should, if the elements of which all things are composed are the same, as complex sounds are composed of their own letters.
!note "If the science were born in us": the Platonists' answer is Plato's recollection (Meno 81c-86b, Phaedo 72e-77a), that the soul has the knowledge and has forgotten it, and learning is remembering.

@ch Metaph.I.10 | All sought the four causes, but dimly
All the earlier thinkers sought the causes named in the Physics, and no other, but dimly: early philosophy lisps like a child. Empedocles, for instance, said bone is by its ratio, which is what it is to be, but did not say so clearly. The difficulties about these questions come next.

@taylor 7
@p
That all seem to seek the causes named in the Physics, and that we cannot name any beyond these, is plain even from what was said before. But they seek them dimly; and in a way all of them have been spoken of before, and in a way none at all. For the first philosophy seems to lisp about everything, being young and at its beginning. Even Empedocles says that bone is by its ratio; and that is what it is to be, the substance of the thing. But likewise flesh and each of the other things must be by its ratio, or none of them. It is because of this that flesh and bone and each of the others will be, and not because of the matter, which he names, fire, earth, water and air. If someone else had said this, he would of necessity have agreed, but he did not say it clearly. On these matters, then, the point has been made plain before. But let us come back to the difficulties one might raise about these same questions; for perhaps from them we shall get some help toward the later difficulties.
!note The difficulties promised here are set out in Book Β (III), the fourteen puzzles. Book α, which follows in our order, is thought by many to be a separate lecture placed here.

@treatise Metaph.II | Metaphysics, Book α (II), the "little alpha"
A short book, perhaps a separate lecture, placed between Α and Β, and named in the ancient lists "little alpha". It is about the study of truth in general. The truth is hard to reach as a whole but easy in part, and we owe thanks even to those who erred, since they trained us; philosophy is rightly called knowledge of the truth, and the principles of eternal things are most true (chapter 1). There is no infinite series of causes of any of the four kinds: there must be a first cause, or there would be no cause, no end, no knowledge (2). Finally, how one is to listen and learn: each kind of argument has its own exactness, and mathematical exactness is not for natural things (3).

@ch Metaph.II.1 | The study of truth, hard and easy
No one reaches the truth fully, and no one misses it entirely; the difficulty lies in us, as bats' eyes are to daylight. We owe thanks to all who have spoken of it. Philosophy is knowledge of the truth; and since what causes truth in others is most true, the principles of eternal things are most true, and each thing has as much of truth as of being.

@p
The study of the truth is in one way hard and in another easy. A sign of this is that no one can reach it as it deserves, nor does everyone miss it; each says something true about nature, and while each adds little or nothing to it, from all of them together something great comes. So if it is like the proverb, "who would miss the door?", in this way it would be easy; but having some whole and not being able to have the part shows how hard it is. And perhaps, since difficulty is of two kinds, its cause is not in the things but in us. For as the eyes of bats are to the light of day, so is the intellect of our soul to the things that are by nature most evident of all.
!flag so is the reason in our soul to the things | Ross's "reason" stands for νοῦς, "intellect". The Platonists read this sentence as theirs: what is clearest by nature, the intelligible and its principle, is dimmest to us, as the sun to eyes come from the cave (Republic 515e-516a).

@p
@greek οὐ μόνον δὲ χάριν ἔχειν δίκαιον τούτοις
It is just to be grateful not only to those whose opinions one shares, but also to those who have spoken more superficially; for they too contributed something, since they trained our habit of thought beforehand. If there had been no Timotheus, we should not have much of our lyric music; but if there had been no Phrynis, there would have been no Timotheus. It is the same with those who have spoken about the truth: from some we have taken over certain opinions, and others were the cause of these coming to be.
!note Timotheus of Miletus and his teacher Phrynis were poet-musicians of the late fifth century, famous for new kinds of music.

@p
@greek ὀρθῶς δʼ ἔχει καὶ τὸ καλεῖσθαι τὴν φιλοσοφίαν
It is right, too, to call philosophy knowledge of the truth. For the end of theoretical knowledge is truth, and of practical knowledge, work: practical people, even when they consider how things are, study not what is eternal but what is relative to something and now. We do not know the truth without its cause. And each thing that most has a certain character is the one in virtue of which that character belongs to the others too, under the same name: fire, for instance, is hottest, since it is the cause of heat in the others. So too that is most true which is the cause of truth in what comes after it. That is why the principles of eternal things must always be most true: they are not true only at times, nor is anything the cause of their being; they are the cause of being for the others. So as each thing stands in respect of being, so it stands in respect of truth.
!note "As each thing stands in respect of being, so in respect of truth": the Platonists took this as Aristotle's agreement with Plato's Republic (508e-509b), that the Good, the cause of being, is the cause of truth.

@ch Metaph.II.2 | No infinite series of causes
There is a first principle, and the causes are not infinite either in a line or in kinds. Of material, moving, final and formal causes, none can go on without end, since in a series only the first is a cause, and an infinite series has no first. Nor can things come to be without end downward. An infinite series of ends would destroy the good and intellect; of definitions, would destroy knowledge; and infinitely many kinds of cause could not be known.

@p
But it is clear that there is a first principle, and that the causes of the things that are are not infinite, either in a straight line or in kind. Things cannot go on without end as one thing from another as from matter, flesh from earth, earth from air, air from fire, and so on without stopping; nor as from the source of motion, a man moved by the air, the air by the sun, the sun by strife, and no limit to this. Likewise that for the sake of which cannot go on without end, walking for the sake of health, health for happiness, happiness for something else, and so always one thing for the sake of another. And what it is to be is likewise. For in intermediates that have a last term and a prior one, the prior must be the cause of what comes after it. If we had to say which of the three is the cause, we should say the first: surely not the last, since the end is the cause of nothing; nor the middle, since it is the cause of one thing only (and it makes no difference whether there is one or more, nor whether they are infinite or finite). But in series infinite in this way, and in the infinite in general, all the parts down to the present one are alike intermediates. So if nothing is first, there is no cause at all.
!note The first of Aristotle's arguments for a first cause, which Book Λ (XII) completes with the unmoved mover; the Platonists drew on it for the One as first cause (Proclus, Elements of Theology, propositions 11 and 12).

@p
@greek ἀλλὰ μὴν οὐδʼ ἐπὶ τὸ κάτω οἷόν τε εἰς ἄπειρον ἰέναι
Nor can things go on without end downward, when the upward series has a beginning, so that from fire comes water, from water earth, and so always some other kind comes to be. A thing comes to be from another in two ways, leaving aside the sense in which "from" means "after", as we say "from the Isthmian games come the Olympian": either as a man comes from a boy, by the boy's changing, or as air from water. By "as a man from a boy" we mean as what has come to be from what is coming to be, or what is completed from what is being completed. For as becoming is always between being and not being, so what is becoming is always between what is and what is not: the learner is a knower in the making, and that is what we mean when we say that from a learner comes a knower. But coming from another as air from water means the other is destroyed. That is why the first kind of change does not turn back, and the boy does not come from the man (for what comes to be does not come from the coming to be, but is after it; so the day comes from the dawn, in that it comes after it, and that is why the dawn does not come from the day); but the other kind does turn back. In both ways it is impossible to go on without end. Of the first kind, being intermediates, there must be an end; and those of the second change into one another, for the perishing of the one is the coming to be of the other.

@p
@greek ἅμα δὲ καὶ ἀδύνατον τὸ πρῶτον ἀΐδιον ὂν φθαρῆναι
At the same time, the first, being eternal, cannot be destroyed. For since coming to be is not infinite upward, the first from whose destruction something came to be cannot be eternal. Further, that for the sake of which is an end, and of such a kind that it is not for the sake of another, but the others are for its sake. So if there is such a last term, there will be no infinite series; and if there is none, there will be no that for the sake of which. Those who make an infinite series do not notice that they are doing away with the nature of the good; yet no one would try to do anything if he were not going to reach a limit. Nor would there be intellect in the things that are; for one who has intellect always acts for the sake of something, and that is a limit, since the end is a limit.
!flag Nor would there be reason in the world | Ross's "reason" is νοῦς, "intellect", as everywhere in this work; and the Greek says "in the things that are" (ἐν τοῖς οὖσιν), not "in the world".
!note Ross found this paragraph's first sentences very obscure: Aristotle seems to mean that since the upward series must stop, there is an eternal first cause, and an eternal cause cannot be one whose destruction produces something.

@p
@greek ἀλλὰ μὴν οὐδὲ τὸ τί ἦν εἶναι ἐνδέχεται ἀνάγεσθαι
Nor can what it is to be be traced back to another definition fuller in its account. For the earlier definition is always more a definition, and the later is not; and where the first is not, neither is what follows. Further, those who speak so do away with knowing: one cannot know before coming to things that cannot be divided. And there is no knowing at all; for how can one think of things infinite in this way? It is not like the line, which does not stop being divisible, but which cannot be thought without stopping (that is why one who goes through the infinite line will not count its divisions); but the matter too must be thought in something that does not move. And nothing infinite can be; or if it can, at least what it is to be infinite is not infinite.
!note The last sentences are hard in the Greek. Ross took "the matter too must be thought in something that does not move" to mean that even the matter of a changing thing must be grasped by a thought that comes to rest. Taylor's note says that matter, ever flowing, is known according to Plato by a bastard reasoning (Timaeus 52b), and according to Aristotle by analogy.

@p
@greek ἀλλὰ μὴν καὶ εἰ ἄπειρά γʼ ἦσαν πλήθει
And again, if the kinds of causes were infinite in number, knowing would not be possible that way either. For we think we know when we have come to know the causes; and what is infinite by addition cannot be gone through in a finite time.

@ch Metaph.II.3 | How to listen, and the right exactness
What we accept from a speaker depends on our habits: some want mathematics, some examples, some poets as witnesses, some exactness and some none. One must be trained beforehand in what to accept from each kind of argument. Mathematical exactness is not to be asked in everything, but only in what has no matter; so the method of the study of nature is not mathematical, and we must first ask what nature is.

@p
How lectures affect us depends on our habits. We expect things to be said as we are used to, and what is beside this does not seem the same, but less familiar and more foreign because we are not used to it; for the customary is more familiar. How strong the customary is, the laws show, where the mythical and childish elements have more force, from habit, than knowledge of them. Some do not accept what is said unless it is said mathematically, others unless it is shown by examples, as by patterns, while others expect a poet to be brought in as witness. Some want everything exact, while exactness annoys others, either because they cannot follow the connections or because of the pettiness of it; for exactness has something of this, so that, as in contracts, so in arguments it seems to some ungentlemanly. So one must be trained beforehand in how to take each kind of argument, since it is absurd to seek knowledge and the way of knowledge at the same time; and neither is easy to get.
!flag because they regard it as pettifoggery | Ross's "pettifoggery" is a lawyer's word for μικρολογία, "pettiness", the hair-splitting of the small-minded; the comparison that follows is with contracts, where haggling over trifles seems mean.

@p
@greek τὴν δʼ ἀκριβολογίαν τὴν μαθηματικὴν
The exactness of mathematics is not to be asked for in everything, but only in things that have no matter. That is why its method is not that of the study of nature, for presumably all nature has matter. So we must first consider what nature is; for so it will also be plain what natural science is about, and whether it belongs to one science or to more to study the causes and the principles.
!note Ross's Greek of 1924 keeps the last clause; his translation of 1908 brackets it, as not fitting here. The question whether one science studies all the causes is the first of the puzzles of Book Β.
