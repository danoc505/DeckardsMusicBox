@treatise Metaph.I | Metaphysics, Book Α (I)
The Metaphysics is the work the Platonists read last of Aristotle's, as the summit of the course: the science of the first causes and principles, which Aristotle calls wisdom, first philosophy and theology. Book Α opens the inquiry. All men by nature desire to know; knowledge rises from the senses through memory and experience to art and science, and wisdom is the knowledge of the first causes, sought for its own sake, the most divine of the sciences (chapters 1 and 2). There are four causes: the matter, the source of motion, the form or what it is to be, and the end (3). Aristotle then reviews the earlier thinkers, to see whether they found any cause beyond these four: the first philosophers found only the material cause; others added a source of motion, love and strife, or intellect; the Pythagoreans made number the principle; the Eleatics made the whole one (3 to 5). Plato's philosophy, the Forms, the great and the small and the One, is set out (6), and the review is summed up (7). Then come the objections: to the materialists and the Pythagoreans (8), and at length to the Forms (9), where Aristotle speaks as one of Plato's school ("we"). The book closes by saying that all the earlier thinkers sought these four causes, but dimly, as a child lisps (10).

@ch Metaph.I.1 | From the senses to wisdom
All men by nature desire to know, as their love of the senses shows. From sense comes memory, from many memories experience, and from experience art and science. Experience knows that something is so; art knows why, and so can teach. Wisdom is knowledge of causes and principles, and the more theoretical a knowledge, the more it is wisdom.

@p
All human beings by nature desire to know. A sign of this is the delight we take in our senses: even apart from their use, we love them for their own sake, and above all the sense of sight. Not only when we mean to act, but even when we mean to do nothing, we prefer seeing, one might say, to everything else. The reason is that of all the senses sight most makes us know things, and shows us many differences between them.
!note The first sentence of the Metaphysics. "To know" (εἰδέναι) is the same verb as "to see" in its perfect form: to know is to have seen. Aristotle draws the argument from the love of sight.

@p
@greek φύσει μὲν οὖν αἴσθησιν ἔχοντα γίγνεται τὰ ζῷα
Animals are born with sensation by nature. In some of them sensation gives rise to memory, in others not. That is why those that can remember are more intelligent and more apt at learning than those that cannot. Those that cannot hear sounds, such as the bee and any other kind of animal like it, are intelligent without being able to learn; those that have this sense of hearing besides memory can be taught. The other animals live by appearances and memories, and have only a little share of experience; but the human race lives also by art and by reasoning.

@p
@greek γίγνεται δʼ ἐκ τῆς μνήμης ἐμπειρία
In human beings, experience comes from memory: many memories of the same thing amount in the end to the power of a single experience. Experience seems very like knowledge and art, but in fact knowledge and art come to human beings through experience. For "experience made art", as Polus says, and rightly, "but inexperience, luck". Art comes about when, out of many notions gained by experience, one universal judgment arises about things alike. To judge that when Callias had this disease this remedy helped him, and Socrates too, and so in many single cases, is a matter of experience. But to judge that it helped all persons of a certain kind, marked off as one class, when they had this disease (the phlegmatic, say, or the bilious, when burning with fever) is a matter of art.
!note Polus is the student of the rhetorician Gorgias who appears in Plato's Gorgias; the saying is quoted there (448c).

@p
@greek πρὸς μὲν οὖν τὸ πράττειν ἐμπειρία τέχνης
For action, experience seems no worse than art, and we even see people of experience succeed better than those who have the theory without the experience. The reason is that experience is knowledge of particulars, and art of universals; and actions and productions are all about the particular. The physician does not cure "man", except incidentally, but Callias or Socrates or someone else called by a particular name, who happens to be a man. So if someone has the theory without the experience, and knows the universal but not the particular within it, he will often fail to cure, since it is the particular that has to be cured. Still, we think that knowing and understanding belong to art rather than to experience, and we take those who have an art to be wiser than those who have only experience, since wisdom in every case goes with knowing. This is because the one kind knows the cause and the other does not. People of experience know that a thing is so, but not why; the others know the why, the cause. That is why we think the master-craftsmen in each craft more to be honoured than the manual workers: they know more truly and are wiser, because they know the causes of what is done. (The manual workers we think like some lifeless things, which act, but act without knowing what they do, as fire burns; the lifeless things do each of their work by a natural tendency, the workers by habit.) So we think them wiser not because they can act, but because they have the account and know the causes. And in general it is a sign of the one who knows, as against the one who does not, that he can teach. So we think art is knowledge more truly than experience is: those who have an art can teach, and those who have only experience cannot.
!note The sentence on the manual workers, in parentheses, Ross thinks was probably added later, in Aristotle's school.

@p
@greek ἔτι δὲ τῶν αἰσθήσεων οὐδεμίαν ἡγούμεθα εἶναι σοφίαν
Again, we do not regard any of the senses as wisdom, though they give the most authoritative knowledge of particulars. But they do not tell us the why of anything: they do not tell us why fire is hot, only that it is hot. At first, then, whoever discovered any art beyond the common perceptions was naturally admired by people, not only because something in his discoveries was useful, but because he was thought wise and superior to the rest. As more arts were discovered, some for the necessities of life and some for its leisure, the discoverers of the latter were always thought wiser than those of the former, because their kinds of knowledge were not aimed at use. So when all such arts had been established, the sciences that aim neither at pleasure nor at the necessities of life were discovered, and first in the places where people first had leisure. That is why the mathematical arts first arose in Egypt: there the priestly class was allowed leisure.

@p
@greek εἴρηται μὲν οὖν ἐν τοῖς ἠθικοῖς
We have said in the Ethics what the difference is between art, science and the other things of this kind. The point of the present discussion is this: everyone supposes that what is called wisdom is about the first causes and principles. So, as was said before, the person of experience is thought wiser than those who have any sensation whatever, the person with an art wiser than those with experience, the master-craftsman wiser than the manual worker, and the theoretical kinds of knowledge more of the nature of wisdom than the productive. It is clear, then, that wisdom is knowledge about certain principles and causes.
!note The Ethics: the Nicomachean Ethics, Book VI, chapters 3 to 7, where art, science, practical wisdom, intellect and wisdom are told apart.

@ch Metaph.I.2 | What wisdom is: the science of the first causes
From what we think of the wise, wisdom must be the most universal, exact and authoritative knowledge, sought for its own sake: knowledge of first principles and causes, among them the good. Philosophy began in wonder, as a free science, not for use. It is the most divine science, both because God would have it and because it is about God; it ends, unlike our first wonder, in knowing why things must be as they are.

@p
Since this is the knowledge we are seeking, we must inquire what kind of causes and principles they are whose knowledge is wisdom. If one takes the notions we have about the wise, the answer may become clearer. We suppose, first, that the wise person knows all things, as far as possible, without having knowledge of each of them one by one. Next, that the one who can know things that are hard and not easy for a human being to know is wise (perceiving is common to everyone, and so it is easy and nothing wise). Again, that in every branch of knowledge the one who is more exact, and better able to teach the causes, is wiser. And that among the sciences, the one chosen for its own sake and for the sake of knowing is more of the nature of wisdom than the one chosen for its results; and the ruling science more than the subordinate one. For the wise person must not be ordered but must order, and must not obey another, but the less wise must obey him.

@p
@greek τὰς μὲν οὖν ὑπολήψεις τοιαύτας καὶ τοσαύτας ἔχομεν
Such and so many are the notions we have about wisdom and the wise. Of these, knowing all things must belong to the one who has universal knowledge in the highest degree, since he knows in a way all that falls under it. And these things, the most universal, are the hardest for human beings to know, since they are furthest from the senses. The most exact of the sciences are those most concerned with first things: those built on fewer principles are more exact than those that take in additions, as arithmetic is more exact than geometry. The science that studies causes is also the more able to teach, since those who teach are those who state the causes of each thing. And knowing and understanding for their own sake belong most to the science of what is most knowable. For whoever chooses knowing for its own sake will choose most of all the knowledge that is most truly knowledge, and that is the knowledge of what is most knowable; and the first things and the causes are most knowable, since it is through them and from them that the rest are known, and not they through what falls under them. The science that most rules, and rules more than any subordinate science, is the one that knows for what end each thing is to be done; and that end is the good of each thing, and in general the best in the whole of nature.

@p
@greek ἐξ ἁπάντων οὖν τῶν εἰρημένων ἐπὶ τὴν αὐτὴν ἐπιστήμην
By every test we have named, then, the name we are seeking, wisdom, falls to the same science. It must be a science that studies the first principles and causes; for the good, that for the sake of which, is one of the causes.

@p
@greek ὅτι δʼ οὐ ποιητική
That it is not a science of making is clear from the first who philosophised. It is through wonder that human beings now begin, and first began, to philosophise. At first they wondered at the puzzles close at hand; then, going forward little by little, they raised puzzles about greater things, such as what happens to the moon, the sun and the stars, and how the universe came to be. Someone who is puzzled and wonders thinks himself ignorant; that is why the lover of myths is in a way a lover of wisdom, since a myth is made of wonders. So if they philosophised to escape ignorance, it is plain that they pursued knowing for the sake of understanding, and not for any use. What happened bears this out: it was when nearly everything needed for life, ease and pastime was at hand that this kind of understanding began to be sought. Clearly, then, we do not seek it for any other use. As we call a man free who exists for his own sake and not for another's, so this alone of the sciences is free, since it alone is for its own sake.
!note Compare Plato, Theaetetus 155d: "wonder is the feeling of a philosopher, and philosophy begins in wonder."

@p
@greek διὸ καὶ δικαίως ἂν οὐκ ἀνθρωπίνη νομίζοιτο
That is why its possession might rightly be thought more than human; for in many ways human nature is in bondage, so that, as Simonides says, "God alone could have this prize", and it is not fitting for a man not to seek the knowledge that suits him. If there is anything in what the poets say, and the divine is by nature jealous, it would be jealous here most of all, and all who excelled in this would be unfortunate. But the divine cannot be jealous (rather, as the proverb says, "bards tell many a lie"), and no other science should be thought more honourable than this one. For the most divine science is also the most honourable, and this science alone is most divine, in two ways. A science would be divine if God would have it most of all, and so would any science that is about divine things. This science alone is both: God is thought by everyone to be among the causes of all things and a principle, and such a science God alone could have, or God above all others. All the sciences, then, are more necessary than this one, but none is better.
!note Plato too denies that the divine is jealous (Phaedrus 247a, Timaeus 29e): "he was good, and in the good no jealousy ever arises about anything."

@p
@greek δεῖ μέντοι πως καταστῆναι τὴν κτῆσιν αὐτῆς
Yet having it must in a way bring us to the opposite of where our inquiry began. As we said, all begin by wondering whether things are so, as people wonder at self-moving puppets before they see the cause, or at the solstices, or at the incommensurability of the diagonal; for it seems wonderful to everyone that something cannot be measured by even the smallest unit. But we must end in the opposite and, as the proverb says, the better state, as happens in these cases too once one has learned: nothing would make a geometer wonder so much as if the diagonal turned out to be commensurable. We have said, then, what the nature of the science we are seeking is, and what mark our search and our whole inquiry must reach.

@ch Metaph.I.3 | The four causes, and the first philosophers on matter
The causes are spoken of in four ways: the substance, what it is to be; the matter, the underlying thing; the source of motion; and that for the sake of which, the good. The first philosophers saw only the material cause (water for Thales, air, fire, the four elements, Anaxagoras' infinitely many). The facts themselves then drove them to seek the source of motion, and at last intellect, as the cause of order and beauty.

@p
Clearly we must acquire knowledge of the first causes; for we say we know each thing when we think we know its first cause. Causes are spoken of in four ways. One of them we say is the substance, what it is to be (for the why is traced back finally to the account of the thing, and the first why is a cause and principle); another is the matter, the underlying thing; a third, the source of motion; and the fourth, the cause opposite to this, that for the sake of which and the good (for this is the end of all coming to be and motion). We have studied these sufficiently in our work on nature. Still, let us take with us those who before us came to the study of what is and philosophised about the truth. Plainly they too speak of certain principles and causes; so going over their views will help our present inquiry. Either we shall find some other kind of cause, or we shall trust the ones we have just named the more.
!flag In one of these we mean the substance, i.e. the essence | Ross's "essence" stands for a phrase of Aristotle's own making, τὸ τί ἦν εἶναι, literally "the what it was to be", the being of a thing that its definition states. The modern version says "what it is to be" throughout, and keeps "essence" for nothing else.
!note The work on nature is the Physics, Book II, chapters 3 and 7, where the four causes are first set out.

@p
@greek τῶν δὴ πρώτων φιλοσοφησάντων οἱ πλεῖστοι
Of the first to philosophise, most thought that principles in the form of matter were the only principles of all things. That of which all things consist, from which they first come to be and into which they are at last destroyed, the substance remaining while its attributes change: this they call the element and the principle of the things that are. That is why they think that nothing comes to be or perishes, since such a nature is always preserved. In the same way we do not say that Socrates comes to be without qualification when he becomes handsome or musical, nor that he perishes when he loses these states, because the underlying thing, Socrates himself, remains. So with everything else, they say: there must be some nature, either one or more than one, from which the rest come to be while it is preserved.
!flag alone subsisted in the species of matter | Taylor's "species of matter" renders ἐν ὕλης εἴδει word for word; the sense is "in the form of matter", that is, material principles only. Here εἶδος has no technical sense.

@p
@greek τὸ μέντοι πλῆθος καὶ τὸ εἶδος τῆς τοιαύτης ἀρχῆς
They do not all agree, though, about the number and kind of such a principle. Thales, the founder of this kind of philosophy, says it is water; that is why he declared that the earth rests on water. He took the notion, perhaps, from seeing that the nourishment of all things is moist, and that heat itself comes from the moist and lives by it (and that from which things come to be is their principle). He took the notion from this, and from the fact that the seeds of all things have a moist nature, and water is the principle of the nature of moist things. Some think that the ancients too, who lived long before our time and first wrote of the gods, held this view of nature. They made Ocean and Tethys the parents of coming to be, and the oath of the gods to be by water, which the poets call Styx; for what is oldest is most honoured, and what one swears by is most honoured. Whether this opinion about nature is old and ancient may perhaps be unclear; but Thales at least is said to have spoken so about the first cause. (No one would think fit to rank Hippo among these, because of the poverty of his thought.)
!note Ocean and Tethys: Homer, Iliad XIV.201 and 246. Plato's Cratylus (402b) and Theaetetus (152e, 180d) also cite them as the parents of all things, with Heraclitus' flux.

@p
@greek Ἀναξιμένης δὲ ἀέρα καὶ Διογένης
Anaximenes and Diogenes make air prior to water, and the most first of the simple bodies; Hippasus of Metapontum and Heraclitus of Ephesus make it fire; Empedocles the four, adding earth as a fourth to those named. These, he says, always remain and do not come to be, except in being more or fewer, as they are gathered into one and separated out of one. Anaxagoras of Clazomenae, older than Empedocles but later in his works, says the principles are infinite in number. For he says that nearly all things made of like parts, such as water or fire, come to be and perish in this way, by being gathered and separated only, and do not otherwise come to be or perish, but remain for ever.

@p
@greek ἐκ μὲν οὖν τούτων μόνην τις αἰτίαν
From these facts one might think the only cause is the one called the cause in the form of matter. But as they went forward, the facts themselves opened the way for them and forced them to inquire. However true it may be that all coming to be and perishing is out of some one or more things, why does it happen, and what is the cause? The underlying thing does not make itself change: neither the wood nor the bronze is the cause of its own change; the wood does not make a bed nor the bronze a statue, but something else is the cause of the change. To seek this is to seek the other principle, as we should say, the source of motion. Those who at the very beginning took up this inquiry, and said the underlying thing was one, were not troubled by this. But some of those who say it is one, as if beaten by this question, say that the one, and the whole of nature, is unmoved, not only in coming to be and perishing (that is an ancient view, and all agreed in it) but in every other change too; and this is peculiar to them. Of those who said the whole was one, none happened to see a cause of this kind, except perhaps Parmenides, and he only in so far as he posits not only one but in a way two causes. Those who make more than one element can more easily speak of it, those for instance who make the hot and the cold, or fire and earth: they treat fire as having a nature that moves things, and water, earth and the like as the contrary.

@p
@greek μετὰ δὲ τούτους καὶ τὰς τοιαύτας ἀρχάς
After these men, and such principles as theirs, since these were not enough to generate the nature of things, people were again forced, as we said, by the truth itself to seek the next principle. For that things are and come to be well and beautifully, some of them, is not likely to be caused by fire or earth or any such element, nor likely that those thinkers supposed so; nor again would it be right to hand over so great a matter to chance and luck. So when someone said that intellect is present, in nature as in animals, as the cause of the world and of all its order, he seemed like a sober man beside the idle talk of those before him. We know for certain that Anaxagoras took up these arguments, but Hermotimus of Clazomenae is said to have spoken of them first. Those who thought so posited a principle of things that is at once the cause of their beauty and the kind of cause from which motion comes to things.
!flag When one man said, then, that reason was present | Ross's "reason" stands for νοῦς, "intellect" (Taylor's word), the highest power of knowing and, for Anaxagoras, the cause that orders all things. The Platonists read this praise of Anaxagoras beside Socrates' in the Phaedo (97b-99c), where Socrates hopes for an intellect that orders everything for the best, and is disappointed.

@ch Metaph.I.4 | Love and strife; the atomists
Hesiod and Parmenides may have looked for a moving cause first, in love. Since there is evil as well as good in nature, Empedocles made two, love and strife, in a way the first to make the good and the bad principles. But these thinkers used their causes like untrained boxers, by chance: Anaxagoras brings in intellect only when at a loss. Leucippus and Democritus made the full and the empty the elements, and their differences of shape, order and position the causes of everything else; motion they neglected.

@p
One might suspect that Hesiod was the first to look for such a thing, or anyone else who put love or desire among the things that are as a principle, as Parmenides did. For he, building up the coming to be of the universe, says: "First of all the gods she devised Love." And Hesiod says: "First of all things was Chaos, and then broad-breasted Earth, and Love, who is foremost among all the immortals." This implies that among the things that are there must be some cause that will move things and bring them together. How these should be ranked as to who was first, let us decide later.
!note The lines are from Parmenides' poem (fragment 13 in Diels) and Hesiod's Theogony 116-120. Plato's Symposium (178b) quotes the same lines of both, in Phaedrus' praise of Love as the oldest of the gods.

@p
@greek ἐπεὶ δὲ καὶ τἀναντία τοῖς ἀγαθοῖς ἐνόντα
But since the contraries of good things were also seen to be present in nature, not only order and beauty but also disorder and ugliness, and bad things more than good, and base things more than beautiful, so another thinker brought in friendship and strife, each the cause of one of these. For if one follows Empedocles and takes him by his meaning, and not by his lisping words, one will find that friendship is the cause of good things, and strife of bad. So if one said that Empedocles in a way speaks of the bad and the good as principles, and was the first to do so, one would perhaps be right, if indeed the cause of all good things is the good itself.

@p
@greek οὗτοι μὲν οὖν, ὥσπερ λέγομεν, καὶ μέχρι τούτου
These thinkers, as we say, plainly grasped two of the causes we have marked out in our work on nature, the matter and the source of motion, and only so far; and dimly, and with no clearness, but as untrained men do in fights. They rush about and often strike good blows, but not from knowledge; and so these thinkers do not seem to know what they are saying, for they plainly make almost no use of their causes, or only a little. Anaxagoras uses intellect as a god from the machine to make the world: when he is at a loss for the cause why something is of necessity, he drags it in, but in everything else he names anything rather than intellect as the cause of what comes to be. Empedocles uses his causes more than this, but neither enough nor consistently. In many places, at least, friendship for him separates and strife unites. When the whole is divided into its elements by strife, fire is gathered into one, and so is each of the other elements; but when they come together again into one under friendship, the parts must be separated out of each element again.
!note "A god from the machine": the crane of the Athenian theatre, which brought a god on stage to resolve a plot. Socrates' complaint against Anaxagoras in the Phaedo (98b-c) is the same.

@p
@greek Ἐμπεδοκλῆς μὲν οὖν παρὰ τοὺς πρότερον πρῶτος
Empedocles, then, unlike those before him, was the first to divide this cause, positing not one source of motion but two different and contrary ones. Again, he was the first to speak of four elements in the form of matter; yet he does not use them as four, but as two only: fire by itself, and its opposites, earth, air and water, as one nature. One may see this by studying his verses.

@p
@greek οὗτος μὲν οὖν, ὥσπερ λέγομεν, οὕτω τε καὶ τοσαύτας
He, then, as we say, spoke of the principles in this way and made them so many. Leucippus and his companion Democritus say the elements are the full and the empty, calling the one what is and the other what is not: the full and solid is what is, the empty and rare is what is not (which is why they say that what is no more is than what is not, since the solid no more is than the empty); and they make these the causes of things as matter. And as those who make the underlying substance one generate everything else by its attributes, positing the rare and the dense as the principles of the attributes, in the same way these men say that the differences are the causes of everything else. These differences, they say, are three: shape, order and position. For they say that what is differs only by "rhythm", "touching" and "turning"; and of these rhythm is shape, touching is order, and turning is position. A differs from N in shape, AN from NA in order, and Z from H in position. As to motion, where it comes from or how it belongs to things, these too, like the others, lazily let it go.
!note The letters are the Greek Α, Ν, Ζ and Η. The atoms differ as letters do: in shape, in their order, and in which way they are turned.

@p
@greek περὶ μὲν οὖν τῶν δύο αἰτιῶν
About the two causes, then, as we say, the inquiry of those before us seems to have gone so far.

@ch Metaph.I.5 | The Pythagoreans and the Eleatics
The Pythagoreans, trained in mathematics, took the principles of numbers for the principles of all things: number is both the matter and the attributes of things, and its elements are the even and the odd, the unlimited and the limited. Some of them set out ten pairs of contraries. The Eleatics, who make the whole one, belong to another inquiry: Parmenides meant one in account, Melissus one in matter, Xenophanes looked to the whole heaven and called the One God. The chapter ends with what can be learned from all of them.

@p
In their time, and before them, the so-called Pythagoreans took up mathematics and were the first to advance it. Being brought up in it, they thought its principles were the principles of all things. Since of these principles numbers are by nature first, and they thought they saw in numbers many likenesses to the things that are and come to be, more than in fire, earth and water (such and such an attribute of numbers being justice, another soul and intellect, another opportunity, and so with nearly everything else); and since, again, they saw in numbers the attributes and ratios of the musical scales; since, then, everything else seemed in its whole nature to be modelled on numbers, and numbers seemed to be the first things in all of nature, they took the elements of numbers to be the elements of all things, and the whole heaven to be a harmony and a number.

The modern version keeps "harmony", since the word carries both the music and the fitting together of the cosmos, as in Plato's Timaeus (35b-36b), where the world soul is built on the ratios of a scale.

And whatever agreements they could show in numbers and scales with the attributes and parts of the heaven and its whole arrangement, they collected and fitted together; and if there was a gap anywhere, they eagerly made additions so that their whole system would hang together. For example, since ten is thought to be complete and to take in the whole nature of numbers, they say that the bodies moving in the heaven are ten; and since only nine are visible, they make the counter-earth the tenth. We have set this out more exactly elsewhere.

@p
@greek ἀλλʼ οὗ δὴ χάριν ἐπερχόμεθα
But the purpose of our review is to learn from these too what they posit as principles, and how these fall under the causes we have named. They too plainly think that number is the principle, both as matter for the things that are and as their attributes and states; and that the elements of number are the even and the odd, the even being unlimited and the odd limited; and that the one comes from both of these (for it is both even and odd), and number from the one; and that the whole heaven, as was said, is numbers.

@p
@greek ἕτεροι δὲ τῶν αὐτῶν τούτων τὰς ἀρχὰς δέκα λέγουσιν
Others of this same school say there are ten principles, which they set in two columns: limit and unlimited, odd and even, one and many, right and left, male and female, at rest and moving, straight and curved, light and darkness, good and bad, square and oblong. Alcmaeon of Croton seems to have thought in this way too, and either he took the view from them or they from him; for he spoke like them. He says that most human things go in pairs, meaning not definite contraries, as the Pythagoreans do, but any that come to hand, such as white and black, sweet and bitter, good and bad, great and small. He threw out undefined suggestions about the rest; but the Pythagoreans declared how many the contraries are and which they are.
!note Ross's Greek of 1924 brackets the words on Alcmaeon's age ("he lived when Pythagoras was old") as a later addition; the modern version leaves them out, as Ross's translation does.

@p
@greek παρὰ μὲν οὖν τούτων ἀμφοῖν τοσοῦτον ἔστι λαβεῖν
From both of these, then, one can learn this much: that the contraries are the principles of things. How many they are, and which, one can learn from one of the two. But how they can be brought under the causes we have named, these thinkers have not clearly worked out. They seem to rank the elements in the form of matter; for they say that substance is composed and moulded out of them, as out of parts within it.

@p
@greek τῶν μὲν οὖν παλαιῶν καὶ πλείω λεγόντων
From these, then, one may see well enough the meaning of the ancients who said the elements of nature are more than one. But there are some who spoke of the whole as one nature, though not all in the same way, either in how well they spoke or in how they spoke of nature. The discussion of them does not belong at all to our present inquiry into causes. For they do not, like some of the natural philosophers, posit that what is is one and yet generate it out of the one as out of matter; they speak in another way. Those add motion, since they generate the whole; these say it is unmoved. Still, this much is proper to our present inquiry. Parmenides seems to grasp what is one in account, Melissus what is one in matter; which is why the one says it is limited and the other unlimited. Xenophanes, the first of them to make all one (Parmenides is said to have been his pupil), made nothing clear, and does not seem to have touched the nature of either of these; but looking up at the whole heaven, he says that the One is God. These, as we said, must be left aside for the present inquiry, two of them entirely, as being a little too crude, Xenophanes and Melissus. But Parmenides seems to speak with more insight. Claiming that besides what is, what is not is nothing, he thinks that of necessity what is is one and nothing else is (we have spoken of this more clearly in our work on nature). But being forced to follow the appearances, and supposing that what is is one in account but more than one in perception, he posits two causes and two principles, the hot and the cold, that is, fire and earth; and of these he ranks the hot with what is, and the other with what is not.

@greek ἐκ μὲν οὖν τῶν εἰρημένων καὶ παρὰ τῶν συνηδρευκότων
From what has been said, then, and from the wise men who have now sat in council with us, we have this much. From the first, that the principle is bodily (water, fire and the like are bodies), one bodily principle for some, more than one for others, but both putting them in the form of matter; and from some, that there is this cause and besides it the source of motion, one for some and two for others. Down to the Italians, and apart from them, the others spoke of these things rather obscurely, except that, as we said, they used two causes, and of these some make the second, the source of motion, one and others two. The Pythagoreans spoke of two principles in the same way, but added this much, which is their own: that the limited and the unlimited and the one are not attributes of some other nature, such as fire or earth or anything of that kind, but that the unlimited itself and the one itself are the substance of the things they are predicated of. That is why number is the substance of all things. On these matters they declared themselves in this way. About what a thing is they began to speak and to define, but treated it too simply. For they defined superficially, and took the first thing to which a given definition belongs to be the substance of the thing; as if one thought that double and the number two are the same, because two is the first thing to which double belongs. But to be double and to be two are surely not the same; if they were, the one would be many, which they did conclude. This much, then, may be learned from those before, and from the others.
!note The Italians: the Pythagoreans, whose school was in southern Italy, at Croton and Metapontum.

@ch Metaph.I.6 | Plato
Plato followed the Pythagoreans in most things, but had his own views. From Heraclitus he took that sensible things are in flux and cannot be known; from Socrates, the search for definitions; so the definitions must be of other things, the Forms, in which sensible things participate. Between the two are the objects of mathematics. The elements of the Forms are the great and the small as matter and the One as substance, and the Forms are numbers. Aristotle concludes that Plato used only two causes, what it is to be and matter, and gave the cause of good and of evil to his two elements.

@p
After the philosophies we have named came the work of Plato, which in most things followed these thinkers, but had its own features beside the philosophy of the Italians. In his youth he first became familiar with Cratylus and the views of Heraclitus, that all sensible things are always flowing and there is no knowledge of them; and he held these views later too. Socrates was busy with ethics and not at all with nature as a whole, but in ethics he sought the universal, and was the first to fix his thought on definitions. Plato accepted him, but for this reason held that definition is about other things and not about sensible ones: the common definition cannot be of any of the sensible things, since they are always changing. Things of this other kind he called Ideas, and sensible things, he said, are beside these and are all named after them; for the many things that share the name of the Forms are by participation in them. The name "participation" was all he changed: the Pythagoreans say things are by imitation of numbers, and Plato by participation, changing the name. But what the participation or imitation of the Forms might be, they left to be inquired into in common.
!note The Platonists answered that participation is not a word only: Proclus, on the Parmenides, and Syrianus, on Books Μ and Ν of this work, set out how the Forms are present in what participates in them while remaining in themselves.

@p
@greek ἔτι δὲ παρὰ τὰ αἰσθητὰ καὶ τὰ εἴδη
Further, besides sensible things and the Forms, he says there are the objects of mathematics, between them, differing from sensible things in being eternal and unmoved, and from the Forms in that there are many alike, while each Form itself is one only.

@p
@greek ἐπεὶ δʼ αἴτια τὰ εἴδη τοῖς ἄλλοις
Since the Forms are the causes of everything else, he thought their elements were the elements of all things. As matter, the great and the small are principles; as substance, the One. For out of the great and the small, by participation in the One, come the numbers. In saying that the One is substance, and not something else that is said to be one, he spoke like the Pythagoreans; and in saying that the numbers are the causes of the substance of everything else, he spoke as they did. But to make a dyad instead of the unlimited as one, and to make the unlimited out of the great and the small, is his own; and so is this, that he puts the numbers apart from sensible things, while they say the things themselves are numbers, and do not place the objects of mathematics between. His putting the One and the numbers apart from things, unlike the Pythagoreans, and his bringing in the Forms, came from his inquiry into accounts (for those before him had no share in dialectic); and his making the other nature a dyad came from his thinking that the numbers, except the first, could be neatly generated out of it, as out of a kind of plastic stuff.

@greek καίτοι συμβαίνει γʼ ἐναντίως
Yet what happens is the contrary; it is not reasonable so. They make many things out of the matter, while the form generates only once. But what we see is that one table comes from one matter, while the one who brings the form, though he is one, makes many. So too with the male and the female: the female is filled by one mating, but the male fills many. Yet these are likenesses of those principles.

@p
@greek Πλάτων μὲν οὖν περὶ τῶν ζητουμένων οὕτω διώρισεν
Plato, then, settled the questions we are asking in this way. It is clear from what has been said that he used only two causes, the cause of what a thing is and the cause in the form of matter: the Forms are the causes of what everything else is, and the One of what the Forms are. And it is clear what the underlying matter is, of which the Forms are said in the case of sensible things, and the One in the case of the Forms: that it is the dyad, the great and the small. Further, he gave the cause of good and of evil to the elements, one to each, as we say some of the earlier philosophers too tried to do, such as Empedocles and Anaxagoras.
!note The Platonists denied that the Good is only one of two elements: for them it is the first principle of all, the One itself, beyond being (Republic 509b), and Plato's dyad is not evil. Syrianus answers Aristotle on this in his commentary on Book Ν.

@ch Metaph.I.7 | The review summed up
None of the earlier thinkers named a cause beyond the four. Some spoke of matter, some of the source of motion; what it is to be was spoken of chiefly by the friends of the Forms; the end they named, but not as an end. So the four causes are rightly set out, and next their views are to be examined.

@taylor 6
@p
We have gone over briefly and in summary who said what about the principles and the truth, and in what way. Still, we have this much from them: that of those who speak of principle and cause, none has named any beyond those marked out in our work on nature, but all plainly touch on them, though dimly. Some speak of the principle as matter, whether they posit one or more, and whether they posit it as a body or as bodiless: Plato, for instance, the great and the small; the Italians, the unlimited; Empedocles, fire, earth, water and air; Anaxagoras, the infinity of things made of like parts. These have all grasped this kind of cause, and so have all who speak of air, fire or water, or something denser than fire and rarer than air; for some have said the first element is of that kind.

@p
@greek οὗτοι μὲν οὖν ταύτης τῆς αἰτίας ἥψαντο μόνον
These, then, touched on this cause only; but others on the source of motion, those for instance who make friendship and strife, or intellect, or love a principle. What it is to be, the substance, no one has stated clearly; those who posit the Forms come nearest to it. For they do not take the Forms to be the matter of sensible things, nor the One to be the matter of the Forms, nor take them as the source of motion (they say they are rather causes of rest and of being unmoved); but they offer the Forms as what each of the other things is, and the One as what the Forms are. That for the sake of which actions, changes and motions take place, they say is a cause in a way, but not in this way, not in the way it is by nature a cause. Those who speak of intellect or friendship posit these causes as good, but they do not speak as if anything were or came to be for their sake, but as if motions came from them. In the same way those who say the One or what is is the good say it is a cause of substance, but not that anything is or comes to be for its sake. So it turns out that in a way they both say and do not say that the good is a cause; they say it not without qualification but incidentally.
!note "What it is to be" is the formal cause; Aristotle grants that Plato's Forms were the first attempt at it, but holds that the form must be in the thing, not apart from it (I.9 and Book Ζ).

@p
@greek ὅτι μὲν οὖν ὀρθῶς διώρισται περὶ τῶν αἰτίων
That we have marked out the causes rightly, how many they are and of what kind, all these thinkers seem to bear witness, by not being able to touch on any other cause. Besides, it is plain that the principles must be sought either all in this way or in some one of these ways. Next let us go through the possible difficulties about how each of them has spoken, and how each stands concerning the principles.

@ch Metaph.I.8 | Against the materialists and the Pythagoreans
Those who make one bodily element the principle leave out the bodiless, the source of motion and what things are, and cannot say which element is first. Empedocles has these troubles and more; Anaxagoras, read charitably, means the One and the indefinite. The Pythagoreans use principles fit for higher things to explain only the sensible heaven, and say nothing of how there is motion, weight or lightness, or how number is the cause of the things in the heaven if it is also those things.

@taylor 7
@p
Those, then, who posit that the whole is one, and that there is one nature as matter, and that bodily and having size, plainly go wrong in many ways. They posit elements of bodies only, not of bodiless things, though there are bodiless things too. In trying to state the causes of coming to be and perishing, and in giving an account of the nature of all things, they do away with the cause of motion. Further, they go wrong in not positing the substance, what a thing is, as the cause of anything; and besides, in lightly calling any of the simple bodies except earth the principle, without considering how they come to be out of one another, fire, water, earth and air. Some come to be out of one another by combining, others by separating, and this makes the greatest difference to which is prior and which posterior. In one way, the most elementary of all would seem to be the first thing out of which things come to be by combining, and that would be the body of finest parts and the most subtle. So those who posit fire as the principle would speak most in agreement with this argument. And each of the others too agrees that the element of bodies is of this kind: at least none of those who said there is one element thought fit to say it is earth, plainly because of the size of its parts; while each of the other three has found a judge, some saying fire, some water, some air. Yet why do they not also say earth, as most people do? For people say all things are earth; and Hesiod too says that earth came first of bodies, so ancient and common has the belief happened to be. By this argument, then, no one would speak rightly who says it is any of these except fire, or who posits something denser than air and rarer than water. But if what is later in coming to be is prior in nature, and what is worked up and compounded is later in coming to be, the contrary of what we have said would be true: water would be prior to air, and earth to water.
!flag who consider the universe as one, and as being one certain nature, as matter | Taylor's words are close to the Greek, but "certain" is his: the Greek says simply "one nature as matter". The modern version keeps the plainer phrase.

@p
@greek περὶ μὲν οὖν τῶν μίαν τιθεμένων αἰτίαν
About those who posit one cause of the kind we described, let this be said. The same holds if one posits more of them, as Empedocles says the matter of things is four bodies. For the same consequences must meet him, some the same as have been said, others his own. We see the bodies coming to be out of one another, so that the same body does not always remain fire or earth (we have spoken of this in our works on nature). And about the cause of things' being moved, whether one should posit one or two, one must think he has spoken neither rightly nor altogether reasonably. In general, those who speak in this way must do away with change of quality; for on their view the cold will not come from the hot nor the hot from the cold. For then what would undergo the contraries themselves, and what would the one nature be that becomes fire and water? He does not say.
!note Ross thought the last three sentences (989a26-30) probably a gloss; his Greek of 1924 keeps them.

@p
@greek Ἀναξαγόραν δʼ εἴ τις ὑπολάβοι
If one supposed that Anaxagoras speaks of two elements, one would suppose it in full accord with an argument that he did not himself work out, but that he would have had to follow if someone had led him. It is absurd, indeed, in other ways too to say that at the beginning all things were mixed: because they must first have existed unmixed; because it is not natural for any chance thing to be mixed with any chance thing; and besides, because attributes and accidents would then be separable from substances (since the same things that are mixed are separable). Yet if one followed him, putting together what he means, he would perhaps appear to speak in a more modern way. For when nothing had been separated out, plainly nothing true could be said of that substance. I mean, for instance, that it was neither white nor black, nor grey nor of any other colour, but of necessity colourless, since otherwise it would have had one of these colours. Likewise, by the same argument, it was without flavour, and without anything else of the kind. It could not be of any quality, nor of any quantity, nor any something; for then one of the forms said in part would have belonged to it, and that is impossible when all are mixed; for it would already have been separated out. But he says all things are mixed except intellect, and that this alone is unmixed and pure. From this it follows that he must say the principles are the one (for this is simple and unmixed) and the other, such as we posit the indefinite to be before it is defined and shares in some form. So he speaks neither rightly nor clearly, but he means something like what was said later and what now appears more plainly.
!note Anaxagoras' fragment 12 (Diels): "Intellect is unlimited and self-ruling, and is mixed with no thing, but is alone by itself." Aristotle reads his two principles as the One and the indefinite, close to Plato's One and dyad.

@p
@greek ἀλλὰ γὰρ οὗτοι μὲν τοῖς περὶ γένεσιν λόγοις
But these thinkers are at home only in the arguments about coming to be, perishing and motion; for it is nearly only of this kind of substance that they seek the principles and the causes. But those who make their study about all the things that are, and posit some things that are as sensible and others as not sensible, plainly study both kinds. So one might spend more time on them, to see what they say well and what not, toward the inquiry now before us.

@p
@greek οἱ μὲν οὖν καλούμενοι Πυθαγόρειοι
The so-called Pythagoreans use principles and elements stranger than those of the natural philosophers. The reason is that they did not take them from sensible things; for the objects of mathematics are without motion, except those of astronomy. Yet everything they discuss and work at is about nature. They generate the heaven, and they watch what happens about its parts, attributes and workings, and use up the principles and the causes on these, as if they agreed with the others, the natural philosophers, that what is is just what is sensible and what the so-called heaven contains. But the causes and principles they name are, as we said, enough to lead up even to the higher kinds of things that are, and fit those better than the accounts of nature. Yet they do not say at all how there will be motion, if only limit and unlimited, odd and even, are assumed; nor how without motion and change there can be coming to be and perishing, or the doings of the bodies that move through the heaven. Further, even if one granted them that size comes from these, or this were shown, still how will some bodies be light and others heavy? From what they assume and say, they speak no more of mathematical bodies than of sensible ones. That is why they have said nothing at all about fire or earth or other such bodies, I suppose because they have nothing to say of sensible things in particular.

@p
@greek ἔτι δὲ πῶς δεῖ λαβεῖν
Further, how are we to take it that the attributes of number, and number itself, are causes of what is and comes to be in the heaven from the beginning and now, and yet that there is no other number besides this number out of which the cosmos is composed? When they place opinion and opportunity in one region, and a little above or below injustice and separation or mixture, and offer as proof that each of these is a number, while there already happens to be in that place a crowd of composite bodies, because these attributes of number go with their several places: is this number, which one must take each of these to be, the same number that is in the heaven, or another besides it? Plato says it is another. Yet he too thinks that both these things and their causes are numbers, but the causes are intelligible numbers, and these are sensible.

@ch Metaph.I.9 | Against the Forms
The arguments for the Forms either prove nothing or prove Forms of things the Platonists deny Forms of; the more exact ones lead to Forms of relatives or to the "third man". The Forms explain neither motion nor knowledge nor being; "pattern" and "participation" are empty words and poetic metaphors. If the Forms are numbers, they cannot be causes; the Platonists' account of lines, planes and solids fails; and to seek the elements of all things without distinguishing the senses of being is hopeless. Aristotle speaks here as one of Plato's school ("we").

@taylor 7
@p
About the Pythagoreans, then, let us leave things for now; it is enough to have touched on them so far. As for those who posit the Ideas as causes: first, in seeking to grasp the causes of the things here, they brought in others equal in number to them, as if someone who wanted to count things thought he could not while they were fewer, but counted them after making them more. For the Forms are about equal to, or not fewer than, the things in seeking whose causes they went from these to those. For to each thing there is something of the same name, apart from the substances; and for all the other things there is a one over many, both for the things here and for the eternal ones.
!note In this chapter Aristotle says "we" of the Platonists, as one of them ("the ways in which we prove that the Forms exist"). The same objections, often word for word, return in Book Μ, chapters 4 and 5 (1078b-1080a), and it is there that Syrianus answers them, in his commentary on Books Μ and Ν.

@p
@greek ἔτι δὲ καθʼ οὓς τρόπους δείκνυμεν
Further, of the ways in which we show that the Forms exist, none is convincing. From some, no conclusion necessarily follows; from others, there follow Forms even of things of which we think there are none. For by the arguments from the sciences there will be Forms of all the things there are sciences of; by the one over many, Forms even of negations; and by the argument from thinking of something that has perished, Forms of perishable things, since there is an image of these. Further, of the more exact arguments, some make Ideas of relatives, of which we say there is no kind by itself, and others state the "third man". And in general the arguments for the Forms do away with the things that those who speak of Forms want more than they want the Ideas to be. For it follows that not the dyad is first but number, and the relative prior to what is by itself, and all the other points on which some, following out the opinions about the Ideas, have come into conflict with the principles.

The argument from "equal itself" in Plato's Phaedo (74a-75b) makes such a Form.

@p
@greek ἔτι κατὰ μὲν τὴν ὑπόληψιν καθʼ ἣν εἶναί φαμεν
Further, by the assumption on which we say there are Ideas, there will be Forms not only of substances but of many other things too (for the thought is one not only of substances but of other things, and there are sciences not only of substance but of others, and countless other such consequences follow). But by necessity, and by the opinions held about the Forms, if the Forms are participated in, there must be Ideas of substances only. For they are not participated in incidentally; each Form must be participated in as not said of an underlying thing. I mean, for instance, if something participates in double itself, it participates also in the eternal, but incidentally, since being eternal is incidental to the double. So the Forms will be substance. But the same words signify substance here and there; or what will it mean to say that there is something apart from these, the one over many? And if the form of the Ideas and of what participates in them is the same, there will be something common to them; for why should two be one and the same in the perishable twos and in the many but eternal twos, and not in two itself and a particular two? But if the form is not the same, they will have only the name in common, as if one called both Callias and a piece of wood a man, without seeing anything common to them.

@p
@greek πάντων δὲ μάλιστα διαπορήσειεν ἄν τις
Above all one might ask what the Forms contribute to the eternal sensible things, or to those that come to be and perish. They are not causes of any motion or change in them. Nor do they help at all toward knowledge of the other things (for they are not their substance; else they would be in them), nor toward their being, since they are not in the things that participate in them. If they were, they might perhaps seem to be causes, as white is a cause of whiteness by being mixed in; but this argument, which Anaxagoras first and later Eudoxus and some others used, is very easily upset, for it is easy to gather many impossibilities against such a view. Nor do the other things come from the Forms in any of the usual senses of "from". To say that they are patterns and that the other things participate in them is to speak emptily and to use poetic metaphors. For what is it that works, looking to the Ideas? Anything can be and become like another without being copied from it; so whether Socrates is or is not, someone like Socrates might come to be; and plainly likewise even if Socrates were eternal. There will also be several patterns of the same thing, and so several Forms: of man, for instance, animal and two-footed, and also man himself. Further, the Forms will be patterns not only of sensible things but of Forms themselves, as the genus is a pattern of the species within it; so the same thing will be pattern and image.
!flag to use empty words and poetical metaphors | Ross's words are right; the note is the Platonists'. The "pattern" (παράδειγμα) and the maker "looking to" it are Plato's own, in the Timaeus (28a-29a), where the craftsman of the world looks to the eternal pattern; the Platonists held that this is not a metaphor but the way intellect makes.

@p
@greek ἔτι δόξειεν ἂν ἀδύνατον εἶναι χωρὶς
Further, it would seem impossible for a substance to be apart from that of which it is the substance; so how could the Ideas, being the substances of things, be apart from them? In the Phaedo it is said that the Forms are causes both of being and of coming to be. Yet even if the Forms exist, the things that participate in them do not come to be unless there is something to move them; and many other things come to be, a house or a ring, of which they say there are no Forms. So plainly the other things too can both be and come to be through causes like those of the things just named.
!note Phaedo 100b-101c: "it is by the beautiful that all beautiful things are beautiful". Aristotle's point is that a Form that does not move anything cannot make anything come to be; the Platonists replied that the Forms are causes as patterns, with the maker and the end, not as movers.

@p
@greek ἔτι εἴπερ εἰσὶν ἀριθμοὶ τὰ εἴδη
Further, if the Forms are numbers, how will they be causes? Is it because the things that are are other numbers, this number man, that Socrates, that Callias? Why then are those numbers the causes of these? It will make no difference if the ones are eternal and the others not. But if it is because the things here are ratios of numbers, as a harmony is, plainly there is something of which they are ratios. If this is something, the matter, plainly the numbers themselves too will be ratios of one thing to another. I mean, if Callias is a ratio in numbers of fire, earth, water and air, the Idea too will be a number of certain other underlying things; and man himself, whether or not it is a kind of number, will still be a ratio in numbers of certain things, and not a number; nor will it be any number on this account.

@p
@greek ἔτι ἐκ πολλῶν ἀριθμῶν εἷς ἀριθμὸς γίγνεται
Further, from many numbers one number comes to be; but how does one Form come from Forms? If it is not from the numbers themselves but from the units in them, as in ten thousand, how are the units? If they are alike in kind, many absurdities will follow; and if not alike in kind, neither those in the same number like one another nor all those in different numbers like all, in what will they differ, since they have no attributes? This is neither reasonable nor in keeping with thought. Further, they must set up another kind of number, which arithmetic is about, and all the things some call intermediates; and how are these, or from what principles? Or why will they be between the things here and the things themselves? Further, each of the units in two must come from a prior two; yet that is impossible. Further, why is a number, taken together, one? Further, besides what has been said, if the units are different, they should have spoken like those who say there are four or two elements: each of these calls element not what is common, body, but fire and earth, whether there is something common, body, or not. As it is, they speak as if the One were of like parts, like fire or water. But if so, the numbers will not be substances. Plainly, if there is a One itself and this is a principle, "one" is said in more than one way; otherwise it is impossible.

@p
@greek βουλόμενοι δὲ τὰς οὐσίας ἀνάγειν εἰς τὰς ἀρχὰς
When we want to lead substances back to their principles, we posit lengths as coming from the short and long, a kind of small and great; the plane from the broad and narrow; and the body from the deep and shallow. Yet how will the plane have a line in it, or the solid a line and a plane? For the broad and narrow is a different kind from the deep and shallow. So as number is not in these, since the many and few differ from them, plainly none of the higher will be in the lower. Nor is the broad a genus of the deep; for then the body would be a kind of plane. Further, from what will the points be present in the line? Plato fought against this kind as a geometrical notion; he called it the principle of the line, and often posited the indivisible lines. Yet these must have a limit; so by the argument by which there is a line, there is also a point.

@p
@greek ὅλως δὲ ζητούσης τῆς σοφίας περὶ τῶν φανερῶν
In general, though wisdom seeks the cause of the things that appear, we have let this go (for we say nothing of the cause from which change begins). While we think we are stating their substance, we say there are other substances; but how these are the substances of those, we explain emptily, for "participating", as we said before, is nothing. Nor do the Forms touch at all what we see to be the cause in the sciences, that for the sake of which every intellect and every nature makes things, the cause we say is one of the principles. But mathematics has become philosophy for those of today, though they say it should be studied for the sake of other things. Further, one might take the substance they posit as underlying, as matter, to be too mathematical: to be said of the substance and matter, and to be a difference of it, rather than matter. I mean the great and the small, which are like the rare and the dense the natural philosophers speak of, calling them the first differences of the underlying thing; for these are a kind of excess and defect. And as to motion, if these are motion, plainly the Forms will be moved; and if not, where did it come from? The whole study of nature has been done away with.
!note "Those of today": Plato's successors in the Academy, Speusippus and Xenocrates, for whom mathematics had become the whole of philosophy. Plato himself (Republic 531d, 533b-d) makes mathematics a prelude to dialectic.

@p
@greek ὅ τε δοκεῖ ῥᾴδιον εἶναι
And what is thought to be easy, to show that all things are one, is not done. For by setting out the universal, all things do not become one, but there is a One itself, if one grants everything; and not even this, unless one grants that the universal is a genus, and in some cases this is impossible. Nor is there any account of the things after the numbers, lengths, planes and solids: neither how they are or will be, nor what power they have. These cannot be Forms (for they are not numbers), nor the intermediates (for those are the objects of mathematics), nor the perishable things. They plainly appear to be another, a fourth, kind.

@p
@greek ὅλως τε τὸ τῶν ὄντων ζητεῖν στοιχεῖα μὴ διελόντας
In general, to seek the elements of the things that are without distinguishing them, when they are said in many ways, makes it impossible to find them, especially when one seeks in this way what kind of elements they consist of. From what acting or being acted on, or the straight, consists, surely cannot be found; if at all, only the elements of substances can. So to seek the elements of all the things that are, or to think one has them, is not true. And how could one learn the elements of all things? Plainly one could not already know anything before. As someone learning geometry may know other things before, but knows nothing in advance of what the science is about and what he is about to learn, so too with the rest. So if there is a science of all things, as some say, whoever learns it would know nothing before. Yet all learning is through things known before, all or some, whether by demonstration or by definitions; for the parts of the definition must be known before and be familiar. Learning by induction is likewise. But if the science were born in us, it would be strange that we fail to notice we have the greatest of sciences. Further, how will one know what things are made of, and how will it be made plain? This too is a difficulty; for one might dispute it, as about some syllables: some say ZA is made of S, D and A, while others say it is a different sound and none of the known ones. Further, how could one know the objects of a sense without having the sense? Yet one should, if the elements of which all things are composed are the same, as complex sounds are composed of their own letters.
!note "If the science were born in us": the Platonists' answer is Plato's recollection (Meno 81c-86b, Phaedo 72e-77a), that the soul has the knowledge and has forgotten it, and learning is remembering.

@ch Metaph.I.10 | All sought the four causes, but dimly
All the earlier thinkers sought the causes named in the Physics, and no other, but dimly: early philosophy lisps like a child. Empedocles, for instance, said bone is by its ratio, which is what it is to be, but did not say so clearly. The difficulties about these questions come next.

@taylor 7
@p
That all seem to seek the causes named in the Physics, and that we cannot name any beyond these, is plain even from what was said before. But they seek them dimly; and in a way all of them have been spoken of before, and in a way none at all. For the first philosophy seems to lisp about everything, being young and at its beginning. Even Empedocles says that bone is by its ratio; and that is what it is to be, the substance of the thing. But likewise flesh and each of the other things must be by its ratio, or none of them. It is because of this that flesh and bone and each of the others will be, and not because of the matter, which he names, fire, earth, water and air. If someone else had said this, he would of necessity have agreed, but he did not say it clearly. On these matters, then, the point has been made plain before. But let us come back to the difficulties one might raise about these same questions; for perhaps from them we shall get some help toward the later difficulties.
!note The difficulties promised here are set out in Book Β (III), the fourteen puzzles. Book α, which follows in our order, is thought by many to be a separate lecture placed here.

@treatise Metaph.II | Metaphysics, Book α (II), the "little alpha"
A short book, perhaps a separate lecture, placed between Α and Β, and named in the ancient lists "little alpha". It is about the study of truth in general. The truth is hard to reach as a whole but easy in part, and we owe thanks even to those who erred, since they trained us; philosophy is rightly called knowledge of the truth, and the principles of eternal things are most true (chapter 1). There is no infinite series of causes of any of the four kinds: there must be a first cause, or there would be no cause, no end, no knowledge (2). Finally, how one is to listen and learn: each kind of argument has its own exactness, and mathematical exactness is not for natural things (3).

@ch Metaph.II.1 | The study of truth, hard and easy
No one reaches the truth fully, and no one misses it entirely; the difficulty lies in us, as bats' eyes are to daylight. We owe thanks to all who have spoken of it. Philosophy is knowledge of the truth; and since what causes truth in others is most true, the principles of eternal things are most true, and each thing has as much of truth as of being.

@p
The study of the truth is in one way hard and in another easy. A sign of this is that no one can reach it as it deserves, nor does everyone miss it; each says something true about nature, and while each adds little or nothing to it, from all of them together something great comes. So if it is like the proverb, "who would miss the door?", in this way it would be easy; but having some whole and not being able to have the part shows how hard it is. And perhaps, since difficulty is of two kinds, its cause is not in the things but in us. For as the eyes of bats are to the light of day, so is the intellect of our soul to the things that are by nature most evident of all.
!flag so is the reason in our soul to the things | Ross's "reason" stands for νοῦς, "intellect". The Platonists read this sentence as theirs: what is clearest by nature, the intelligible and its principle, is dimmest to us, as the sun to eyes come from the cave (Republic 515e-516a).

@p
@greek οὐ μόνον δὲ χάριν ἔχειν δίκαιον τούτοις
It is just to be grateful not only to those whose opinions one shares, but also to those who have spoken more superficially; for they too contributed something, since they trained our habit of thought beforehand. If there had been no Timotheus, we should not have much of our lyric music; but if there had been no Phrynis, there would have been no Timotheus. It is the same with those who have spoken about the truth: from some we have taken over certain opinions, and others were the cause of these coming to be.
!note Timotheus of Miletus and his teacher Phrynis were poet-musicians of the late fifth century, famous for new kinds of music.

@p
@greek ὀρθῶς δʼ ἔχει καὶ τὸ καλεῖσθαι τὴν φιλοσοφίαν
It is right, too, to call philosophy knowledge of the truth. For the end of theoretical knowledge is truth, and of practical knowledge, work: practical people, even when they consider how things are, study not what is eternal but what is relative to something and now. We do not know the truth without its cause. And each thing that most has a certain character is the one in virtue of which that character belongs to the others too, under the same name: fire, for instance, is hottest, since it is the cause of heat in the others. So too that is most true which is the cause of truth in what comes after it. That is why the principles of eternal things must always be most true: they are not true only at times, nor is anything the cause of their being; they are the cause of being for the others. So as each thing stands in respect of being, so it stands in respect of truth.
!note "As each thing stands in respect of being, so in respect of truth": the Platonists took this as Aristotle's agreement with Plato's Republic (508e-509b), that the Good, the cause of being, is the cause of truth.

@ch Metaph.II.2 | No infinite series of causes
There is a first principle, and the causes are not infinite either in a line or in kinds. Of material, moving, final and formal causes, none can go on without end, since in a series only the first is a cause, and an infinite series has no first. Nor can things come to be without end downward. An infinite series of ends would destroy the good and intellect; of definitions, would destroy knowledge; and infinitely many kinds of cause could not be known.

@p
But it is clear that there is a first principle, and that the causes of the things that are are not infinite, either in a straight line or in kind. Things cannot go on without end as one thing from another as from matter, flesh from earth, earth from air, air from fire, and so on without stopping; nor as from the source of motion, a man moved by the air, the air by the sun, the sun by strife, and no limit to this. Likewise that for the sake of which cannot go on without end, walking for the sake of health, health for happiness, happiness for something else, and so always one thing for the sake of another. And what it is to be is likewise. For in intermediates that have a last term and a prior one, the prior must be the cause of what comes after it. If we had to say which of the three is the cause, we should say the first: surely not the last, since the end is the cause of nothing; nor the middle, since it is the cause of one thing only (and it makes no difference whether there is one or more, nor whether they are infinite or finite). But in series infinite in this way, and in the infinite in general, all the parts down to the present one are alike intermediates. So if nothing is first, there is no cause at all.
!note The first of Aristotle's arguments for a first cause, which Book Λ (XII) completes with the unmoved mover; the Platonists drew on it for the One as first cause (Proclus, Elements of Theology, propositions 11 and 12).

@p
@greek ἀλλὰ μὴν οὐδʼ ἐπὶ τὸ κάτω οἷόν τε εἰς ἄπειρον ἰέναι
Nor can things go on without end downward, when the upward series has a beginning, so that from fire comes water, from water earth, and so always some other kind comes to be. A thing comes to be from another in two ways, leaving aside the sense in which "from" means "after", as we say "from the Isthmian games come the Olympian": either as a man comes from a boy, by the boy's changing, or as air from water. By "as a man from a boy" we mean as what has come to be from what is coming to be, or what is completed from what is being completed. For as becoming is always between being and not being, so what is becoming is always between what is and what is not: the learner is a knower in the making, and that is what we mean when we say that from a learner comes a knower. But coming from another as air from water means the other is destroyed. That is why the first kind of change does not turn back, and the boy does not come from the man (for what comes to be does not come from the coming to be, but is after it; so the day comes from the dawn, in that it comes after it, and that is why the dawn does not come from the day); but the other kind does turn back. In both ways it is impossible to go on without end. Of the first kind, being intermediates, there must be an end; and those of the second change into one another, for the perishing of the one is the coming to be of the other.

@p
@greek ἅμα δὲ καὶ ἀδύνατον τὸ πρῶτον ἀΐδιον ὂν φθαρῆναι
At the same time, the first, being eternal, cannot be destroyed. For since coming to be is not infinite upward, the first from whose destruction something came to be cannot be eternal. Further, that for the sake of which is an end, and of such a kind that it is not for the sake of another, but the others are for its sake. So if there is such a last term, there will be no infinite series; and if there is none, there will be no that for the sake of which. Those who make an infinite series do not notice that they are doing away with the nature of the good; yet no one would try to do anything if he were not going to reach a limit. Nor would there be intellect in the things that are; for one who has intellect always acts for the sake of something, and that is a limit, since the end is a limit.
!flag Nor would there be reason in the world | Ross's "reason" is νοῦς, "intellect", as everywhere in this work; and the Greek says "in the things that are" (ἐν τοῖς οὖσιν), not "in the world".
!note Ross found this paragraph's first sentences very obscure: Aristotle seems to mean that since the upward series must stop, there is an eternal first cause, and an eternal cause cannot be one whose destruction produces something.

@p
@greek ἀλλὰ μὴν οὐδὲ τὸ τί ἦν εἶναι ἐνδέχεται ἀνάγεσθαι
Nor can what it is to be be traced back to another definition fuller in its account. For the earlier definition is always more a definition, and the later is not; and where the first is not, neither is what follows. Further, those who speak so do away with knowing: one cannot know before coming to things that cannot be divided. And there is no knowing at all; for how can one think of things infinite in this way? It is not like the line, which does not stop being divisible, but which cannot be thought without stopping (that is why one who goes through the infinite line will not count its divisions); but the matter too must be thought in something that does not move. And nothing infinite can be; or if it can, at least what it is to be infinite is not infinite.
!note The last sentences are hard in the Greek. Ross took "the matter too must be thought in something that does not move" to mean that even the matter of a changing thing must be grasped by a thought that comes to rest. Taylor's note says that matter, ever flowing, is known according to Plato by a bastard reasoning (Timaeus 52b), and according to Aristotle by analogy.

@p
@greek ἀλλὰ μὴν καὶ εἰ ἄπειρά γʼ ἦσαν πλήθει
And again, if the kinds of causes were infinite in number, knowing would not be possible that way either. For we think we know when we have come to know the causes; and what is infinite by addition cannot be gone through in a finite time.

@ch Metaph.II.3 | How to listen, and the right exactness
What we accept from a speaker depends on our habits: some want mathematics, some examples, some poets as witnesses, some exactness and some none. One must be trained beforehand in what to accept from each kind of argument. Mathematical exactness is not to be asked in everything, but only in what has no matter; so the method of the study of nature is not mathematical, and we must first ask what nature is.

@p
How lectures affect us depends on our habits. We expect things to be said as we are used to, and what is beside this does not seem the same, but less familiar and more foreign because we are not used to it; for the customary is more familiar. How strong the customary is, the laws show, where the mythical and childish elements have more force, from habit, than knowledge of them. Some do not accept what is said unless it is said mathematically, others unless it is shown by examples, as by patterns, while others expect a poet to be brought in as witness. Some want everything exact, while exactness annoys others, either because they cannot follow the connections or because of the pettiness of it; for exactness has something of this, so that, as in contracts, so in arguments it seems to some ungentlemanly. So one must be trained beforehand in how to take each kind of argument, since it is absurd to seek knowledge and the way of knowledge at the same time; and neither is easy to get.
!flag because they regard it as pettifoggery | Ross's "pettifoggery" is a lawyer's word for μικρολογία, "pettiness", the hair-splitting of the small-minded; the comparison that follows is with contracts, where haggling over trifles seems mean.

@p
@greek τὴν δʼ ἀκριβολογίαν τὴν μαθηματικὴν
The exactness of mathematics is not to be asked for in everything, but only in things that have no matter. That is why its method is not that of the study of nature, for presumably all nature has matter. So we must first consider what nature is; for so it will also be plain what natural science is about, and whether it belongs to one science or to more to study the causes and the principles.
!note Ross's Greek of 1924 keeps the last clause; his translation of 1908 brackets it, as not fitting here. The question whether one science studies all the causes is the first of the puzzles of Book Β.

@treatise Metaph.III | Metaphysics, Book Β (III)
Book Β is the book of puzzles (aporiai). Before looking for the science of the first causes, Aristotle says, one must go through the difficulties, as one must see a knot before untying it. He lists some fourteen of them (chapter 1) and argues each both ways (2 to 6): whether one science studies all four causes, the starting-points of proof, and all substances, with their attributes; whether there are substances besides sensible things, such as Forms and the objects of mathematics between them; whether the principles are the genera or the parts a thing is made of, and, if genera, the highest or the lowest; whether anything is apart from individual things; whether the principles are one in kind or in number, and whether perishable and imperishable things have the same principles; whether being and the One are the substance of things, as Plato and the Pythagoreans said; whether numbers, solids, surfaces and points are substances; and whether the principles are universal or individual, and in potency or actual. The book answers none of them; the later books do (Γ, Ζ to Θ, Λ, Μ and Ν). For the Platonists it is the book where Aristotle's quarrel with Plato is set out as a set of fair questions, several of which Syrianus answers in his commentary on Books Β and Γ.

@ch Metaph.III.1 | Why one must go through the puzzles first, and the list of them
To find a way through, one must first know where the way is blocked: a puzzle in thought is a knot in the thing. So the difficulties are listed first. Does one science study all the kinds of cause, and the starting-points of proof as well as substance? Are there substances besides sensible ones? Are the principles the genera or the parts, one in kind or in number, the same for perishable and imperishable things? Are being and the One substances? Are the principles universal or individual, potential or actual? Are numbers, lines and points substances?

@p
For the sake of the science we are seeking, we must first go through the things we need to be puzzled about first. These are both the views that some others have held about them, and anything besides that has happened to be overlooked. For those who want to find their way through, it pays to go well through the difficulties: the later ease of passage is the undoing of the earlier puzzles, and one cannot untie a knot one does not know. A difficulty in our thinking shows a knot in the thing: in so far as our thought is puzzled, it is in the same state as those who are tied up, since in either case one cannot go forward. That is why one must have looked at all the difficulties beforehand, both for these reasons and because those who search without first going through the puzzles are like people who do not know where they have to walk; and besides, such a person does not even know whether he has found what he was looking for, since the end is not clear to him, while to one who has gone through the puzzles first it is clear. Further, one who has heard all the opposing arguments, like the parties in a lawsuit, must be in a better position to judge.
!note The word for a puzzle, ἀπορία, means "no way through", and its solution, εὐπορία, "a good way through"; Aristotle's figure of the knot plays on both. Plato's dialogues often end in such a puzzle; the Platonists read this chapter as Aristotle's own account of why the dialogues do.

@p
@greek ἔστι δʼ ἀπορία πρώτη μὲν περὶ ὧν
The first puzzle is about the things we raised in our opening remarks: whether it belongs to one science or to several to study the causes. And whether the science should survey only the first principles of substance, or also the principles from which everyone proves things, such as whether it is possible to assert and deny one and the same thing at the same time, and all other such questions. And if the science is about substance, whether one science deals with all substances or several do; and if several, whether all are akin, or some of them should be called kinds of wisdom and the others something else. This too is one of the things that must be looked into: whether we should say that only sensible substances exist, or others besides them; and whether these are of one kind, or there are several kinds of substances, as those say who posit both the Forms and the objects of mathematics between these and sensible things. These questions, then, as we say, must be examined; and also whether the study is only of substances or also of the attributes that belong to substances in themselves. Further, about same and other, like and unlike and contrariety, and about prior and posterior and all the others of this kind, which the dialecticians try to examine, starting their inquiry only from reputable opinions: whose task is it to study all these? Further, we must study whatever belongs in themselves to these very things, and not only what each of them is, but also whether one thing always has one contrary. Again, whether the principles and elements are the genera, or the parts present in each thing into which it is divided; and if the genera, whether those predicated last of the individuals, or the first, for instance whether animal or man is the principle, and more apart from the individual.
!flag starting their investigation from probable premises only | Ross's "probable premises" are ἔνδοξα, the "reputable opinions" held by everyone, or by most, or by the wise, from which dialectic argues (Topics I.1). The modern version says "reputable opinions": the point is not that they are only probable but that they are held.
!note "Our opening remarks": Book Α, chapters 1 and 2, on wisdom as the science of the first causes. The answers to the puzzles come later: whether one science studies being and the axioms, in Book Γ; the kinds of substance, in Books Ζ, Λ, Μ and Ν.

@p
@greek μάλιστα δὲ ζητητέον καὶ πραγματευτέον πότερον ἔστι
Above all we must seek and work out whether there is any cause by itself besides matter or not, and whether it is separable or not, and whether it is one or more in number; and whether there is something apart from the composite thing (I call it composite when something is predicated of the matter), or nothing, or something in some cases and not in others, and in what sort of cases. Again, whether the principles are limited in number or in kind, both those in the accounts and those in the underlying thing; and whether the principles of perishable and of imperishable things are the same or different, and whether they are all imperishable, or those of perishable things perishable. Further, there is the hardest question of all, and the most perplexing: whether the One and being, as the Pythagoreans and Plato said, are not something else but are the substance of the things that are; or this is not so, but the underlying thing is something else, as Empedocles says love, another fire, another water or air. And whether the principles are universal or like individual things; and whether they are in potency or in actuality; and further, whether in any other way than with reference to motion, for these questions too would give much difficulty. Besides these, whether numbers, lengths, figures and points are a kind of substance or not, and if they are substances, whether they are separate from sensible things or present in them. About all these things it is not only hard to get through to the truth: it is not easy even to go well through the difficulties in argument.
!flag whether unity and being, as the Pythagoreans and Plato said | Ross's "unity" renders τὸ ἕν, "the One", the first principle of Plato and the Pythagoreans. The modern version says "the One" where it is their principle, and "one" otherwise, as in Book Α.
!flag whether they subsist in capacity or energy | Taylor's "capacity" and "energy" render δύναμις and ἐνέργεια, which the modern version says "potency" and "actuality", as everywhere in this work. "Energy" is the Greek word carried over; it means being at work, being actual, not force.

@ch Metaph.III.2 | The first puzzles: one science or many; the axioms; substance; Forms and intermediates
Can one science study all four causes, when some things, such as the objects of mathematics, have no end or good? But if there are several, which is wisdom? Does the same science study the axioms of all proof? Is there one science of all substances, and of their attributes too? And are there substances besides sensible things: Forms, which are only eternal sensibles, as the gods were eternal men; or intermediates, which would give a second heaven and sun, and a second medicine; or intermediates inside sensible things?

@p
First, then, about what we spoke of first: whether it belongs to one science or to more to study all the kinds of cause. How could it belong to one science to know the principles, when they are not contrary to one another? Further, there are many things in which not all of them are present. How can a source of motion, or the nature of the good, be present in unchangeable things? For everything that is good in itself and by its own nature is an end, and so a cause, since it is for its sake that the other things come to be and are; and an end, that for the sake of which, is the end of some action, and all actions involve motion. So in unchangeable things this principle could not be, nor any good itself. That is why in mathematics nothing is proved by this kind of cause, nor is there any demonstration "because it is better, or worse"; no one even mentions anything of the kind. And so some of the sophists, such as Aristippus, scorned mathematics: in the other arts, even the manual ones, such as carpentry and shoemaking, every reason given is "because it is better, or worse", but mathematics gives no account of goods and evils.
!flag for he is above being to which energy is subordinate | These words are not Aristotle's: they end a note of Taylor's, from Syrianus, that the first principle is above potency and actuality alike, which the scan of his page reads into the opening of the chapter.
!note Aristippus of Cyrene, a companion of Socrates and the founder of the Cyrenaic school, which held pleasure the good. The Platonists answered that mathematics does show the good, as order, symmetry and limit (Proclus, on Euclid, Prologue I, quoting this passage).

@greek ἀλλὰ μὴν εἴ γε πλείους ἐπιστῆμαι τῶν αἰτίων
But if there are several sciences of the causes, and a different one for each principle, which of these should we say is the one we are seeking, or which of those who have them knows best the thing we are looking for? The same thing may have all the kinds of cause: of a house, the source of motion is the art and the builder, that for the sake of which is its work, the matter is earth and stones, and the form is its account. From what we decided earlier about which of the sciences should be called wisdom, there is reason to call each of them so. In so far as it is the most ruling and commanding, and the others, like slave women, may not even speak against it, the science of the end and of the good is of the nature of wisdom, since the other things are for the sake of the end. But in so far as wisdom was defined as the science of the first causes and of what is most knowable, the science of substance would be such. For people know the same thing in many ways, and we say that one who knows what a thing is, by its being something, knows it more than one who knows it by its not being something; and among those, one knows more than another, and most of all the one who knows what it is, not how much or what sort it is, or what it is by nature to do or undergo. Further, in the other cases too, where there are demonstrations, we think we know each thing when we know what it is: for instance, what squaring a rectangle is, that it is finding a mean proportional; and likewise in the others. But about comings to be and actions and every change, we think we know when we know the source of motion; and this is other than the end, and opposite to it. So it would seem to belong to different sciences to study each of these causes.
!note "What we decided earlier": Book Α, chapter 2 (982a-b), where wisdom is said to be the ruling science, the science of the end and the good, and the science of the first causes. To square a rectangle is to find the side of a square equal to it: the mean proportional between its two sides.

@p
@greek ἀλλὰ μὴν καὶ περὶ τῶν ἀποδεικτικῶν ἀρχῶν
But again, about the starting-points of demonstration too, it is disputed whether they belong to one science or to more. By starting-points of demonstration I mean the common opinions from which everyone proves, such as that everything must be either affirmed or denied, and that it is impossible at the same time to be and not to be, and all other such premises. Does one science deal with these and with substance, or a different one; and if not one, which of the two should we call the one we are now seeking? It is not reasonable that they belong to one science: why should it belong to geometry, or to any other science you please, more than to the rest to understand them? If then it belongs likewise to any one of them, and cannot belong to all, then knowing about them is no more the business of the science that knows substances than of any other. At the same time, in what way can there be a science of them? We already know what each of them is; at any rate the other arts use them as known. And if there is a demonstrative science of them, there will have to be some underlying kind, and some of them will be attributes proved and others axioms (for there cannot be demonstration of everything), since demonstration must start from something, be about something, and prove something. So it follows that there is one kind for all the things that are proved, since all the demonstrative sciences use the axioms. But if the science of substance and the science of the axioms are different, which of them is by nature more authoritative and prior? The axioms are the most universal, and principles of all things; and if it is not the philosopher's task, whose else will it be to study what is true and false about them?
!note Book Γ answers this puzzle: the philosopher, who studies being as being, studies the axioms too, above all the principle of non-contradiction (chapters 3 to 8).

@p
@greek ὅλως τε τῶν οὐσιῶν πότερον μία πασῶν ἐστὶν
And in general, is there one science of all substances or more than one? If not one, what sort of substance should we set down as the subject of this science? On the other hand, it is not reasonable that there is one science of all of them, for then there would be one demonstrative science of all the attributes, since every demonstrative science studies, about some underlying thing, the attributes that belong to it in itself, starting from the common opinions. So it belongs to one science to study the attributes in themselves of one kind, starting from one set of opinions. For the subject belongs to one science, and the starting-points to one, whether the same or another; so that the attributes too are studied either by these sciences or by one made from both.
!note Ross found no really good sense in the Greek of the last sentence, and corrected it; the modern version gives the sense he gives it.

@p
@greek ἔτι δὲ πότερον περὶ τὰς οὐσίας μόνον ἡ θεωρία
Further, is the study only of substances, or also of their attributes? I mean, for instance: if the solid is a substance, and lines and planes, does it belong to the same science to know these and the attributes of each kind, those that the mathematical sciences prove, or to another? If to the same, then the science of substance too would be a demonstrative science; but it is thought that there is no demonstration of what a thing is. If to another, what will the science be that studies the attributes of substance? This is very hard to answer.
!flag But demonstration does not appear to be employed about the formal cause | The Greek says "there is no demonstration of what a thing is" (τοῦ τί ἐστιν). Taylor's "formal cause" is his own gloss; it is near the sense, since what a thing is is its form, but the argument turns on definition: what a thing is is defined, not proved.

@p
@greek ἔτι δὲ πότερον τὰς αἰσθητὰς οὐσίας μόνας εἶναι
Further, should we say that only sensible substances exist, or others besides them? And are substances of one kind, or of several, as those say who posit both the Forms and the intermediates, with which they say the mathematical sciences deal? In what sense we say the Forms are causes and substances by themselves has been said in our first discussion of them. But while they are hard in many ways, nothing is stranger than to say that there are certain natures besides those in the heaven, and that these are the same as the sensible ones, except that the one are eternal and the others perishable. They say there is a man himself, a horse itself and health itself, and nothing more, doing much the same as those who said there are gods, but in human form: those made the gods nothing but eternal men, and these make the Forms nothing but eternal sensibles.
!note "Our first discussion": Book Α, chapters 6 and 9. Aristotle again says "we" of the Platonists. "Man himself" (αὐτοάνθρωπος) and "horse itself" are the Academy's names for Forms. The Platonists answered that a Form is not a sensible thing made eternal but its cause, of another order altogether: the Form of man is not a man (Syrianus, on Book Β).

@p
@greek ἔτι δὲ εἴ τις παρὰ τὰ εἴδη καὶ τὰ αἰσθητὰ
Further, if besides the Forms and the sensible things one posits the intermediates between them, there will be many difficulties. Plainly, by the same reasoning there will be lines besides the lines themselves and the sensible ones, and so with each of the other kinds. So, since astronomy is one of these mathematical sciences, there will be a heaven besides the sensible heaven, and a sun and a moon, and the rest of the heavenly bodies likewise. Yet how are we to believe this? It is not reasonable that this heaven be unmoved, and that it moves is altogether impossible. The same holds of the things optics and mathematical harmonics deal with: these too cannot be besides the sensible things, for the same reasons. For if there are sensible things and sensations in between, plainly there will also be animals between the animals themselves and the perishable ones.
!note The intermediates (τὰ μεταξύ): the objects of mathematics, which Plato, as Aristotle reports him (Book Α, 987b14-18), set between the Forms and sensible things, eternal and unchanging like the Forms but many of each kind like sensibles. The Platonists put them in the soul, as the reasons it unfolds in thinking (Proclus, on Euclid, Prologue I).

@p
@greek ἀπορήσειε δʼ ἄν τις καὶ περὶ ποῖα τῶν ὄντων
One might also be puzzled about which kinds of things we should look for these sciences of. If geometry differs from land-measuring only in this, that the one is about things we perceive and the other about things that are not perceptible, plainly there will also be a science besides medicine, between medical science itself and this medical science; and so with each of the others. Yet how is this possible? There would also have to be healthy things besides the sensible healthy things and the healthy itself. At the same time, it is not even true that land-measuring is about perceptible and perishable magnitudes, for then it would perish when they perish. Nor would astronomy be about perceptible magnitudes, nor about this heaven. Perceptible lines are not such lines as the geometer speaks of, since nothing perceptible is straight or round in that way: the hoop touches the ruler not at a point, but as Protagoras used to say, refuting the geometers. Nor are the motions and spirals of the heaven like those astronomy reasons about, nor have points the same nature as the stars.
!flag If geometry is to differ from mensuration only in this | Ross's "mensuration" is γεωδαισία, "land-measuring", the practical art of surveying fields, as against geometry, which was once the same art and gave the science its name ("earth-measuring").
!note Protagoras' point is that a sensible circle touches a sensible straight edge along a stretch, not at a point, so geometry is not true of the things we see. Plato's Republic (527a-b) and Philebus (56e-57a) make the same distinction between the geometry of the builders and that of the philosophers.

@p
@greek εἰσὶ δέ τινες οἵ φασιν εἶναι μὲν τὰ μεταξὺ
There are some who say that these so-called intermediates between the Forms and sensible things exist, not apart from the sensible things, however, but in them. The impossible results for them would take too long to go through in full, but it is enough to look at such as these. It is not reasonable that this should hold only for the intermediates: plainly the Forms too could be in the sensible things, since the same argument applies to both. Further, there would have to be two solids in the same place, and the intermediates could not be unmoved, since they are in moving sensible things. And in general, for the sake of what would one posit that they are, but are in the sensibles? The same strange results will follow as those just stated: there will be a heaven besides the heaven, only not apart but in the same place, which is still more impossible.

@ch Metaph.III.3 | Are the principles the genera, or the parts things are made of?
The elements of speech are its sounds, of a diagram its first proofs, of a bed its parts: so the principles seem to be the constituents. But we know things by definitions, which go by genera: so the genera seem principles. And if genera, the highest or the lowest? Being and one are the highest, but they cannot be genera, since a genus is not predicated of its differences. The lowest species seem more one, and where there is prior and posterior there is no genus apart; yet only the universal could be apart, and then the highest genera would be the principles.

@p
About these things, then, there is much difficulty in how one should set them down to get at the truth; and about the principles too: whether we should take the genera to be elements and principles, or rather the first things present in each thing, from which it is made. The elements and principles of speech, for instance, are thought to be those first things of which all speech is made, not the common genus, speech; and in geometry we call elements those propositions whose proofs are present in the proofs of the others, either of all or of most. Further, those who say there are several elements of bodies, and those who say there is one, say that the things of which bodies are made and composed are the principles: Empedocles, for instance, says that fire and water and the rest with them are the elements present in things, from which they are, and does not call them genera of the things that are. Besides, if one wants to look into the nature of anything else, of a bed, for instance, one knows it when one knows of what parts it is made and how they are put together. From these arguments, then, the genera would not be the principles of things. But in so far as we know each thing through its definitions, and the genera are the principles of definitions, the genera must also be the principles of what is defined. And if to get knowledge of things is to get knowledge of the species by which they are called, the genera are at least principles of the species. And some of those who say that the elements of things are the One and being, or the great and the small, seem to use them as genera. But one cannot speak of the principles in both ways: the account of a thing's substance is one, but the definition through the genera will be different from the one that says what parts a thing is made of.
!note "Elements" (στοιχεῖα) was the name for the letters of speech, and for the first propositions of geometry, as in Euclid's Elements. Those who make the One and the great and the small the elements are Plato and his school (Book Α, chapter 6).

@p
@greek πρὸς δὲ τούτοις εἰ καὶ ὅτι μάλιστα ἀρχαὶ
Besides, even if the genera are principles above all, should we think the first of the genera are principles, or those predicated last of the individuals? This too is disputed. If the universals are always more principles, plainly the highest of the genera will be, since these are said of all things. Then there will be as many principles of the things that are as there are first genera, so that being and one will be principles and substances, since these above all are said of all things. But neither one nor being can be a single genus of the things that are. For the differences of each genus must each both be and be one; but neither the species of a genus can be predicated of its own differences, nor the genus without its species. So if one or being is a genus, no difference will either be or be one. But if they are not genera, neither will they be principles, if the genera are the principles. Further, the intermediate kinds, taken together with their differences, will be genera down to the individuals; but as it is, some are thought to be genera and others not. Besides, the differences are principles even more than the genera; and if these too are principles, the principles become, so to speak, infinite, especially if one takes the first genus as a principle.
!note The argument that being is not a genus: a genus is not said of its own differences (animal is not said of "two-footed"), but being and one are said of everything, the differences included. Plato's Sophist (254b-259b) makes being, same and other "greatest kinds" that run through all the rest; the Platonists read Aristotle's point as agreeing that being is not one genus among others.

@p
@greek ἀλλὰ μὴν καὶ εἰ μᾶλλόν γε ἀρχοειδὲς τὸ ἕν
But again, if what is one is more of the nature of a principle, and the indivisible is one, and everything indivisible is so either in quantity or in species, and what is indivisible in species is prior, and the genera are divided into species, then what is predicated last will be more one; for man is not the genus of individual men. Further, in things where there is a prior and a posterior, what is said of them cannot be something besides them. If two is the first of the numbers, for instance, there will not be a number besides the kinds of numbers; and likewise there will not be a figure besides the kinds of figures. And if not of these, the genera of other things will hardly be besides their species, since it is of these that there are thought most of all to be genera. But in the indivisible species there is no prior and posterior. Further, where one is better and another worse, the better is always prior; so of these too there would be no genus.
!note The rule that there is no common genus apart from a series with a first and a second (the numbers, the figures) Aristotle also gives in the Nicomachean Ethics (I.6, 1096a17-23), against the Form of the Good: "those who brought in this view did not set up Forms of things in which they spoke of prior and posterior, which is why they did not set up a Form of the numbers".

@p
@greek ἐκ μὲν οὖν τούτων μᾶλλον φαίνεται τὰ ἐπὶ
From these arguments, then, the species predicated of the individuals seem to be principles rather than the genera. But again, it is not easy to say in what way one should take these to be principles. For the principle and the cause must be besides the things whose principle it is, and must be able to be separate from them. And why should one suppose that any such thing is besides the individual, except that it is predicated universally and of all? But if for this reason, the more universal must be taken to be more of a principle, so that the first genera would be the principles.

@ch Metaph.III.4 | Is there anything apart from individuals? One in kind or one in number? The perishable and the imperishable; being and the One
The hardest puzzle: if there is nothing besides individuals, there is no knowledge and nothing eternal, not even matter or form; if there is, it cannot be for everything, nor one substance for all men. If the principles are one only in kind, nothing is one in number; if one in number, there is nothing besides the elements. Why are some things perishable and others not, if their principles are the same, as Hesiod and Empedocles fail to say? And are being and the One the substance of things, as Plato and the Pythagoreans held? If not, nothing universal is a substance, nor is number; if so, all things are one, as Parmenides said, and the One is nothing, as Zeno argued.

@p
Connected with these there is a puzzle that is the hardest of all and the most necessary to study, and our argument has now come to it. If there is nothing besides the individual things, and the individuals are infinite, how is it possible to get knowledge of the infinite? For we know all things in so far as there is something one and the same about them, and in so far as something belongs to them universally. But if this is necessary, and there must be something besides the individuals, the genera must be besides the individuals, either the last or the first; and we have just argued through that this is impossible. Further, if it is granted fully that there is something besides the composite thing, whenever something is predicated of the matter: if there is, must there be something besides all of them, or besides some and not others, or besides none?
!note Ross notes that the last sentence breaks off and changes its form: the question which individuals have something apart leads Aristotle to ask whether any have. The answer comes in Book Ζ, chapters 8 and 13 to 16, and in Book Λ.

@p
@greek εἰ μὲν οὖν μηδέν ἐστι παρὰ τὰ καθʼ ἕκαστα
If there is nothing besides the individuals, nothing will be intelligible, an object of thought, but all things will be objects of sense, and there will be knowledge of nothing, unless one says that sensation is knowledge. Further, nothing will be eternal or unmoved, since all sensible things perish and are in motion. But if nothing is eternal, there cannot be coming to be either. For there must be something that comes to be, and something from which it comes to be, and the last of these must be ungenerated, if the series stops, and since it cannot come to be from what is not. Further, if there is coming to be and motion, there must also be a limit: no motion is infinite, but every motion has an end; and what cannot finish coming to be cannot be coming to be, and what has come to be must be as soon as it first has come to be. Further, if the matter is because it is ungenerated, it is much more reasonable that the substance be, which the matter at any time comes to be; for if neither this nor the matter is to be, nothing at all will be. And if this is impossible, there must be something besides the composite, the shape and the form.
!note "Unless one says that sensation is knowledge": the thesis of Protagoras that Plato examines in the Theaetetus (151e-186e). Aristotle returns to it at length in Book Γ, chapters 5 and 6.

@p
@greek εἰ δʼ αὖ τις τοῦτο θήσει
But if one posits this, it is a puzzle in which cases one should posit it and in which not. That it cannot be posited in all is plain: we would not suppose that there is a house besides the particular houses. Besides, will the substance of all of them, of all men, for instance, be one? That is strange, for all the things whose substance is one are one. Then many and different? This too is unreasonable. At the same time, how does the matter become each of them, and how is the composite both of these?
!note "A house besides the houses": Aristotle says the same of the Forms of artefacts in Book Λ (1070a13-20), and the Platonists agreed that there are no Forms of things made by art (Syrianus, on Book Μ, and Proclus, on the Parmenides, Book III).

@p
@greek ἔτι δὲ περὶ τῶν ἀρχῶν καὶ τόδε ἀπορήσειεν ἄν τις
Again, one might be puzzled about the principles in this way too. If they are one in kind, nothing will be one in number, not even the One itself and being itself; and how will there be knowing, if there is not something one over all? But if the principles are each one in number, and not, as with sensible things, different for different things (for instance, since this syllable is the same in kind wherever it is, its principles too are the same in kind, for these too are different in number), if they are not so but are one in number, there will be nothing else besides the elements. For "one in number" and "individual" make no difference: this is what we call the individual, the one in number, and the universal what is said of these. So it would be as if the elements of speech were limited in number: all the letters would have to be as many as the elements, since there could not be two or more of the same.
!flag all the literature in the world would be confined to the ABC | Ross's "literature" and "the ABC" are a free rendering: the Greek says that all the letters (γράμματα) written anywhere would be as many in number as the elements, since no two could be the same in kind. The point is about letters, not books.

@p
@greek οὐθενὸς δʼ ἐλάττων ἀπορία παραλέλειπται
A puzzle as great as any has been passed over both by those of today and by those before them: whether the principles of perishable and of imperishable things are the same or different. If they are the same, how are some things perishable and others imperishable, and for what cause? Hesiod and all the theologians thought only of what was convincing to themselves, and took no thought for us. Making the principles gods and born from gods, they say that those which did not taste nectar and ambrosia became mortal. Plainly these words are familiar to them; but what they have said about the very applying of these causes is above us. If the gods take them for pleasure, nectar and ambrosia are in no way the cause of their being; and if for their being, how could gods who need food be eternal? But it is not worth looking seriously into those who are clever in the manner of myth. We must ask those who speak by demonstration why, if things are made of the same principles, some of them are eternal by nature and others perish. Since they give no cause, and it is not reasonable that it should be so, plainly the principles and the causes of these things are not the same.
!flag The school of Hesiod and all the mythologists | Ross's "mythologists" renders θεολόγοι, "theologians", those who spoke of the gods in poems, such as Hesiod and Orpheus. For the Platonists the word is a title of honour: Proclus calls Orpheus, Homer and Hesiod the "theologians" whose myths hide the truths that Plato teaches openly.
!note Aristotle's contrast is between those who speak "mythically" (μυθικῶς) and those who speak "through demonstration" (διʼ ἀποδείξεως). Plato's Republic (377d-383c) criticizes Hesiod's stories of the gods; the Platonists read them, as Proclus does in his essays on the Republic, as symbols to be interpreted.

@p
@greek καὶ γὰρ ὅνπερ οἰηθείη λέγειν ἄν τις
Even the one whom one might think speaks most consistently, Empedocles, has made the same mistake. He posits strife as a principle and cause of destruction; but strife would seem none the less to produce everything except the One, for all things except God come from it. At least he says: "From which grew all that was and is and will be hereafter: trees, and men and women, and beasts and birds, and water-nourished fish, and long-lived gods." And it is plain even apart from these words: if strife had not been in things, all things would have been one, as he says, for when they came together, "then strife stood outermost". That is why it follows for him that the most blessed god is less wise than the others, since he does not know all the elements: he has no strife in him, and knowing is of like by like. For, he says, "By earth we see earth, by water water, by ether godlike ether, by fire consuming fire, love by love, and strife by grievous strife." But, to return to where the argument began, this much is plain: for him strife turns out to be no more a cause of destruction than of being. Likewise love is not the cause of being, for in bringing things together into the One it destroys the rest. At the same time he gives no cause of the change itself, except that it is so by nature: "But when strife had grown great in the limbs, and rose to its honours as the time was fulfilled, which is fixed for them in turn by a mighty oath." This says that change is necessary, but he shows no cause of the necessity. Still, so far he alone speaks consistently: he does not make some things perishable and others imperishable, but makes all perishable except the elements. The puzzle now is why some things are perishable and others not, if they are made of the same principles.
!note The lines are Empedocles' fragments 21, 36, 109 and 30 in Diels. "The most blessed god" is the Sphere, the whole when love has gathered everything into one. That like is known by like is an old principle; Plato's Timaeus (37a-c) gives the soul a share of the same and the other so that it can know both.

@p
@greek ὅτι μὲν οὖν οὐκ ἂν εἴησαν αἱ αὐταὶ ἀρχαί
Let this suffice to show that the principles cannot be the same. But if the principles are different, one puzzle is whether they too will be imperishable or perishable. If they are perishable, plainly they too must be made of something, since everything that perishes perishes into the things it is made of; so it follows that there are other principles before the principles. But this is impossible, whether the series stops or goes on without end. Further, how will there be perishable things, if their principles are destroyed? But if they are imperishable, why will things made of some imperishable principles perish, while those made of others do not? This is not reasonable, but is either impossible or needs much argument. Further, no one has even tried to say that the principles are different; they say the same principles hold for all things. But they nibble at the first puzzle as if they took it for something small.
!note Ross's footnote gives the answer to this puzzle in Book Ζ (chapters 7 to 9) and Book Λ (chapters 4 and 5): the principles of perishable and of eternal things are the same only by analogy.

@p
@greek πάντων δὲ καὶ θεωρῆσαι χαλεπώτατον
The hardest of all to study, and the most necessary for knowing the truth, is whether being and the One are the substances of the things that are, each of them being nothing else but simply one or being; or we must ask what being and one are, as if some other nature underlay them. Some think the one way and some the other. Plato and the Pythagoreans thought that being and the One are nothing else, but that this is their nature, their substance being just to be one and being. But the natural philosophers think otherwise: Empedocles, for instance, as if bringing it back to something more knowable, says what the one is; for he would seem to say it is love, since love is the cause to all things of their being one. Others say that this one and being, from which things are and have come to be, is fire, and others air. Those who make the elements more than one say the same, since they too must say that being and one are just as many as the principles they name.
!note This is the eleventh puzzle in Ross's count, and the one Aristotle called the hardest in chapter 1. The answer is in Book Ι, chapter 2, and Book Ζ, chapter 16: being and one are not substances but are said of every substance. For the Platonists, Plato's One is not "a substance" either, but above substance and being (Republic 509b, Parmenides 141e).

@p
@greek συμβαίνει δέ, εἰ μέν τις μὴ θήσεται
If one does not posit that the One and being are a substance, it follows that none of the other universals is either, since these are the most universal of all. If there is not a One itself or a being itself, there will hardly be any of the others besides what are called the individuals. Further, if the One is not a substance, plainly number will not be a nature separate from the things that are either: for number is units, and the unit is just what is one. But if there is a One itself and a being itself, their substance must be the One and being, since nothing else is predicated universally of them, only these themselves. But if there is to be a being itself and a One itself, there is much difficulty in how there will be anything else besides them, I mean how the things that are will be more than one. What is other than being is not; so it follows of necessity, by the argument of Parmenides, that all the things that are are one, and that this is being.
!flag unity-itself or being-itself, there will | Ross's "unity-itself" renders αὐτὸ τὸ ἕν, "the One itself", the Platonists' own name for their first principle; the modern version keeps it, as in Book Α.
!note Parmenides' argument, as Aristotle states it in Physics I.3: what is other than being is not; so there is nothing besides being, and being is one. Plato's Sophist (241d, 256d-259b) answers by showing in what sense the other than being is: as difference, not as the opposite of being.

@p
@greek ἀμφοτέρως δὲ δύσκολον
Both ways are hard. Whether the One is not a substance or there is a One itself, number cannot be a substance. Why, if the One is not a substance, has been said already; and if it is, the same puzzle arises as about being. For from what, besides the One, will there be another one? It must be not one; but all things that are are either one or many, and each of the many is one.

@p
@greek ἔτι εἰ ἀδιαίρετον αὐτὸ τὸ ἕν
Further, if the One itself is indivisible, by Zeno's postulate it will be nothing. For what makes a thing neither greater when added nor less when taken away, he says is not one of the things that are, evidently taking it that what is is a magnitude. And if a magnitude, bodily, since the bodily is in every dimension; the others, a plane or a line, will make a thing greater if added one way, but not another, and the point and the unit in no way. But since he studies things crudely, and something indivisible can be, so that in this way too there is an answer to him (for such a thing when added will make a thing more in number, though not greater), still, how will a magnitude come from one such thing, or from many? It would be like saying that the line is made of points. But even if one supposes, as some say, that number comes from the One itself and something else that is not one, one must still ask why and how what comes to be will sometimes be a number and sometimes a magnitude, if the not-one was inequality and was the same nature. For it is not plain how magnitudes would come either from the One and this nature, or from some number and this nature.

!note Zeno of Elea, Parmenides' student, whom Plato's Parmenides (127a-128e) shows defending his master's one against the many. @ch Metaph.III.5 | Are numbers, solids, surfaces and points substances?
If they are not, nothing seems to be substance: attributes are not, and bodies seem less substance than the surfaces, lines and points that bound them, as the later and wiser thinkers held who made numbers the principles. If they are, they are only divisions of bodies, no more in a solid than any shape is in a block of stone, and they come and go without coming to be or perishing, like the now.

@p
A puzzle connected with these is whether numbers, bodies, planes and points are substances or not. If they are not, it escapes us what being is and what the substances of the things that are. For affections and motions and relations and dispositions and ratios do not seem to signify the substance of anything, since they are all said of some underlying thing and none of them is a this. And the things that would most seem to signify substance, water, earth, fire and air, of which composite bodies are made, have hot and cold and such as affections, not substances; and only the body that undergoes these remains as something that is and a substance. But the body is less a substance than the surface, the surface than the line, and the line than the unit and the point. For the body is bounded by these, and they seem able to be without the body, but the body cannot be without them. That is why most thinkers, and the earlier ones, thought that substance and being is body, and the rest affections of it, so that the principles of bodies are principles of the things that are; but the later ones, thought wiser than these, thought the principles were numbers.
!note "The later ones, thought wiser": the Pythagoreans and the Platonists, for whom the point, the line, the surface and the solid are the limits that make body what it is, and come from number. Plato's Timaeus (53c-55c) builds the elements from triangles; the Platonists read the whole series as the soul's unfolding of the Forms (Proclus, on Euclid).

@p
@greek καθάπερ οὖν εἴπομεν, εἰ μὴ ἔστιν οὐσία ταῦτα
As we said, then, if these are not substance, there is no substance at all and nothing that is: for it is surely not right to call the accidents of these things beings. But if it is agreed that lengths and points are substance more than bodies are, and we do not see to what sort of bodies they would belong (for they cannot be in the sensible ones), there would be no substance. Further, these all seem to be divisions of body, in breadth, in depth or in length. Besides, every shape is likewise in the solid, or none is; so if the Hermes is not in the stone, neither is the half of the cube in the cube as something marked off. So neither is the surface: if any surface whatever were in it, the one marking off the half would be too. The same argument holds of the line, the point and the unit. So if body is substance above all, and these are more so than it, but these are not even any substances, it escapes us what being is and what the substance of the things that are.
!note The Hermes in the stone: a statue of Hermes is in the block only in potency, until the sculptor cuts it out; so with the half-cube in the cube, and so, Aristotle argues, with every surface, line and point within a body. The example returns in Book Δ (1017b6) and Book Θ (1048a32).

@p
@greek πρὸς γὰρ τοῖς εἰρημένοις καὶ τὰ περὶ τὴν γένεσιν
For besides what has been said, the questions about coming to be and perishing bring strange results. A substance, if it now is when it was not before, or later is not when it was before, is thought to undergo this by coming to be and perishing. But points and lines and surfaces cannot come to be or perish, though they sometimes are and sometimes are not. When bodies touch or are divided, at one time, when they touch, the boundaries become one, and at another, when they are divided, two. So when bodies have been put together, the one boundary is not but has perished; and when divided, boundaries are that were not before (for the point, which is indivisible, was surely not divided into two). And if they come to be and perish, from what do they come to be? Much the same holds of the now in time: it too cannot come to be and perish, yet it seems always to be different, not being a substance. Likewise, plainly, the same holds of points, lines and planes, since the same argument applies: they are all likewise either limits or divisions.
!note The "now" (τὸ νῦν), the instant, is studied in the Physics (IV.10-13). Plato's Parmenides (156c-e) has its own strange instant, "the sudden" (τὸ ἐξαίφνης), in which the one changes without being in time.

@ch Metaph.III.6 | Why posit Forms? Potency or actuality? Universal or individual?
The Forms seem needed if any principle is to be one in number and not only in kind, since the objects of mathematics, like letters, are many of each kind; yet principles one in number bring back the earlier difficulties. Are the elements potential or actual? Potency is prior, but what is potential may never be. And are the principles universal or individual? Universals are not substances, but individuals cannot be known.

@p
In general one might be puzzled why we must look for other things besides sensible things and the intermediates, such as the Forms we posit. If it is for this reason, that the objects of mathematics differ from the things here in some other respect but not at all in being many of the same kind, so that their principles will not be limited in number (as the principles of all the letters here are not limited in number but in kind, unless one takes those of this syllable or this sound, which will be limited in number too, and likewise with the intermediates, since there too the members of one kind are infinite), so that if there are not, besides the sensible things and the objects of mathematics, others such as some call the Forms, there will be no substance one in number and in kind, nor will the principles of the things that are be so many in number, but only in kind: if then this is necessary, it is necessary for this reason too to posit Forms. For even if those who say so do not articulate it well, this is what they mean, and they must say that each of the Forms is a substance and none is by accident. But if we posit that the Forms are, and that the principles are one in number and not in kind, we have said what impossible results must follow.

!note Each Form is one in number, a single "this", and so is the principle needed if anything is to be known as one; this is why, for the Platonists, there must be Forms over the many mathematical objects. Aristotle grants the reason but holds the result impossible, for the reasons of chapter 4 (999b24-1000a4). The answer comes in Books Μ and Ν, especially Μ.10 (1086b14-1087a25).

Close to these is the puzzle whether the elements are in potency or in some other way. If in some other way, there will be something else prior to the principles; for the potency is prior to that cause, and what is potential need not all be in that way. But if the elements are in potency, it is possible that nothing that is should be: for even what is not yet is able to be. What is not comes to be, but nothing comes to be that is not able to be.
!note The answer is in Book Θ, chapter 8, and Book Λ, chapter 6: actuality is prior to potency, and the first principle must be pure actuality, with no potency in it, for otherwise it might not be.

@p
@greek ταύτας τε οὖν τὰς ἀπορίας ἀναγκαῖον ἀπορῆσαι
We must not only go through these puzzles about the principles, but also whether they are universal or as we say the individuals are. If universal, they will not be substances: for nothing common signifies a this, but a such, while substance is a this. And if it is possible to set out what is predicated in common as a this and one, Socrates will be many animals: himself, and man, and animal, if each of these signifies a this and one. If the principles are universal, then, these results follow; but if they are not universal but like individuals, they will not be knowable, since the knowledge of everything is universal. So there will have to be other principles before the principles, predicated universally of them, if there is to be knowledge of them.

@treatise Metaph.IV | Metaphysics, Book Γ (IV)
Book Γ answers the first puzzles of Book Β. There is a science of being as being, and of what belongs to it as such (chapter 1). "Being" is said in many ways, but all of them with reference to one thing, substance, as everything healthy is called so with reference to health; so one science studies all that is, and above all substance, and with it the one and the many, same and other, the contraries, and the rest (2). The same science, first philosophy, studies the axioms of all proof, and the firmest of them: that the same thing cannot at the same time belong and not belong to the same thing in the same respect, the principle of non-contradiction (3). It cannot be proved, since it is the starting-point of all proof, but it can be defended by refutation against anyone who will say something that means something (4). Aristotle then answers Protagoras' "man is the measure", the Heracliteans' flux and those who think everything that appears is true (5 and 6), and shows that there is nothing between a contradiction's two sides, the principle of the excluded middle (7), and that neither everything is true nor everything false, nor are all things at rest or all in motion: there is a first mover, itself unmoved (8). For the Platonists the defence of the axioms against Protagoras and the flux follows Plato's Theaetetus, which Aristotle often echoes.

@ch Metaph.IV.1 | The science of being as being
There is a science of being as being and of what belongs to it in itself. It is not one of the special sciences, which cut off a part of being. Since we seek the first causes, they must be causes of being as being.
@taylor 1

@p
There is a science that studies being as being, and the things that belong to it in itself. It is not the same as any of the sciences that are called partial, since none of them looks in general at being as being: they cut off some part of it and study what belongs to that part, as the mathematical sciences, for instance, do. Since we are seeking the principles and the highest causes, plainly these must be the causes of some nature in itself. If, then, those who sought the elements of the things that are were seeking these same principles, the elements too must be elements of being, not by accident but as being. That is why we too must grasp the first causes of being as being.
!note "Being as being" (τὸ ὂν ᾗ ὄν): what is, looked at only in so far as it is, not as number, body or living thing. This is the subject Aristotle gives first philosophy; in Book Ε (chapter 1) he also calls it theology, since its first object is the divine, unchanging substance.

@ch Metaph.IV.2 | Being is said with reference to one thing; one science of being, the one, and the opposites
"Being" is said in many ways, but with reference to one nature, as "healthy" is said with reference to health: some things are because they are substances, others because they are affections, ways to or destructions of substance. So one science studies all that is, and above all substance. Being and one go together, so the same science studies the kinds of one, same, like and equal and their opposites, and contrariety. It is the philosopher's task to ask whether Socrates and Socrates seated are the same; the dialectician only tests and the sophist only seems to know. All contraries go back to the one and the many.
@taylor 1

@p
"Being" is said in many ways, but with reference to one thing and one nature, and not homonymously. Everything healthy is said with reference to health: one thing by preserving it, another by producing it, another by being a sign of it, another by being able to receive it. And the medical is said with reference to the art of medicine: one thing is called medical by having it, another by being naturally suited to it, another by being a work of medicine; and we shall find other words used in the same way. So too "being" is said in many ways, but all with reference to one principle. Some things are said to be because they are substances, others because they are affections of substance, others because they are a way towards substance, or destructions or privations or qualities of substance, or productive or generative of substance or of things said with reference to substance, or negations of some of these or of substance. That is why we say that even what is not is not-being. As, then, there is one science of all healthy things, so it is likewise in the other cases too. For it belongs to one science to study not only the things said with reference to one common account, but also those said with reference to one nature, since these too are in a way said with reference to one common account. Plainly, then, it belongs to one science to study the things that are, as things that are. But everywhere a science is mainly of what is first, on which the others depend and through which they are named. If, then, this is substance, it is of substances that the philosopher must grasp the principles and the causes.
!note "With reference to one thing" (πρὸς ἕν): Aristotle's answer to the puzzle of Book Β whether there can be one science of being, when being is not a genus. The word is neither said in one sense of all (synonymous) nor in unrelated senses (homonymous), but of all with reference to substance, as "healthy" is said of a diet, a complexion and a body with reference to health.

@greek ἅπαντος δὲ γένους καὶ αἴσθησις μία ἑνὸς
Of every kind of thing that is one, there is one sense and one science: grammar, for instance, being one science, studies all spoken sounds. So it belongs to a science one in kind to study all the kinds of being as being, and to its kinds to study the kinds of being. Now being and one are the same and one nature, in that they follow each other, as principle and cause do, though not in that they are shown by one account (and it makes no difference if we take them as alike; it even helps). For "one man" and "man" are the same, and "a man that is" and "a man"; and doubling the words in "he is one man" and "he is a man that is" shows nothing different (plainly they are not separated in coming to be or in perishing; and likewise with one), so that it is plain that the addition shows the same thing in these cases, and the one is nothing other besides being. Further, the substance of each thing is one not by accident, and likewise it is just what something is. So there are as many kinds of being as of one. And to study what these are belongs to a science the same in kind: I mean, for instance, about the same and the like and the others of this kind. Almost all contraries go back to this principle; let them be taken as studied by us in the Selection of Contraries.

@ch Metaph.IV.3 | The axioms belong to the philosopher; the firmest principle of all
The axioms are true of everything that is, so the one who studies being as being studies them too, not the geometer or the student of nature. The firmest principle, about which no one can be mistaken, which is neither a hypothesis nor learned, is this: the same thing cannot at the same time belong and not belong to the same thing in the same respect. No one can believe that the same thing is and is not, whatever Heraclitus is said to have said; all proofs go back to it.

@p
We must say whether it belongs to one science or to different ones to study what in mathematics are called axioms, and substance. Plainly the inquiry into these too belongs to one science, the philosopher's. For they belong to all things that are, not to some one kind apart from the rest. And everyone uses them, because they are of being as being, and each kind is a being; but they use them so far as is enough for them, that is, as far as the kind extends about which they bring their proofs. So, since it is plain that they belong to all things as beings (for this is what is common to them), it is the task of the one who knows about being as being to study them too. That is why none of those who study a part tries to say anything about them, whether they are true or not, neither the geometer nor the arithmetician; some of the students of nature did, and understandably, since they alone thought they were studying the whole of nature and being. But since there is someone still above the student of nature (for nature is only one kind of being), the inquiry into these too will belong to the one who studies the universal and the first substance. The study of nature too is a kind of wisdom, but not the first. And what some of those who speak of truth try to say, about how one should accept it, they do from lack of training in the Analytics: they should come to it knowing these things already, and not seek them while they are listening. Plainly, then, it is the philosopher's task, the one who studies all substance as it is by nature, to examine the principles of reasoning too.

@greek προσήκει δὲ τὸν μάλιστα γνωρίζοντα περὶ ἕκαστον
It is fitting that the one who knows most about each kind should be able to state the firmest principles of his subject, so that the one who knows about the things that are as things that are should be able to state the firmest principles of all things. This is the philosopher. And the firmest principle of all is the one about which it is impossible to be mistaken. Such a principle must be the best known (for people are always mistaken about things they do not know) and not a hypothesis. For a principle that anyone must have who understands anything that is, is not a hypothesis; and what anyone must know who knows anything, he must already have when he comes to his study. Plainly, then, such a principle is the firmest of all; which it is, let us say next. It is this: the same thing cannot at the same time belong and not belong to the same thing in the same respect; and let whatever further qualifications we might add be added, against the objections of argument. This, then, is the firmest of all principles, since it has the mark we stated. For no one can suppose that the same thing is and is not, as some think Heraclitus says: what one says, one need not also suppose. And if contraries cannot belong at the same time to the same thing (let the usual qualifications be added to this premise too), and the opinion contrary to an opinion is the one that contradicts it, plainly the same person cannot at the same time suppose that the same thing is and is not: one who was mistaken on this would hold contrary opinions at the same time. That is why all who prove things go back to this as the last opinion; for it is by nature the starting-point of all the other axioms too.

@ch Metaph.IV.4 | Non-contradiction defended by refutation
The principle cannot be proved, since not everything can be proved, but it can be defended by refutation if the opponent will only say something that means something to himself and to another. A word that means one thing cannot mean also its contradictory; one who denies this does away with substance, since all predicates would be accidents without end; makes all things one, like Anaxagoras' mixture, speaking of the indefinite; and cannot speak at all. No one lives by it: no one walks into a well. And there are degrees of error, so some truth is nearer.
@taylor 3

@p
There are some who, as we said, both say themselves that it is possible for the same thing to be and not to be, and that one can suppose this; and many of the writers on nature use this argument. But we have now taken it that it is impossible at the same time to be and not to be, and through this have shown that it is the firmest of all principles. Some, through lack of education, think it right to prove even this; for it is lack of education not to know of what things one should seek proof and of what not. It is altogether impossible that there be proof of everything: it would go on without end, so that even so there would be no proof. And if there are some things of which one should not seek proof, they could not say what principle they think needs it less than this one.
!note "Lack of education" (ἀπαιδευσία): not want of schooling, but of the trained judgement that knows what kind of proof each thing admits, as at the end of Book α (995a12-16).

@p
@greek ἔστι δʼ ἀποδεῖξαι ἐλεγκτικῶς καὶ περὶ τούτου
But one can show by refutation, even of this, that it is impossible, if only the one who disputes it says something; and if he says nothing, it is ridiculous to seek an argument with one who has no argument, in so far as he has none: such a person, as such, is already like a plant. I say that showing by refutation differs from proving, because one who proved it would seem to beg the question at the start, but when the other is the cause of it, there will be refutation, not proof. The starting-point for all such arguments is not to ask him to say that something is or is not (for one might think that this was begging the question), but to say something that means something both to himself and to another; this he must do, if he is to say anything. If he does not, such a person would have no argument, either with himself or with another. But if one grants this, there will be proof, since there will already be something definite. The one responsible is not the one who proves but the one who submits to it: doing away with argument, he submits to argument. Further, one who grants this has granted that something is true without proof.
!flag We can, however, demonstrate negatively even that this view is impossible | Ross's "negatively" renders ἐλεγκτικῶς, "by refutation", by the elenchus: the opponent is led to show the principle himself, in saying anything at all. It is Socrates' way of refuting, which Plato's dialogues show throughout.
!flag For such a man, as such, is seen already to be no better than a mere plant | Ross's words are right; the comparison is the Greek's: a plant has life and nourishment, but not speech. Aristotle uses it of one who will not say anything, since speech is what sets a human being apart.

@p
@greek πρῶτον μὲν οὖν δῆλον ὡς τοῦτό γʼ αὐτὸ ἀληθές
First, then, this much is plain and true: that the word "to be" or "not to be" means something definite, so that not everything is "so and not so". Again, if "man" means one thing, let it be "two-footed animal". By meaning one thing I mean this: if "man" is this, then, if anything is a man, this will be what it is for it to be a man. It makes no difference even if one were to say that a word means several things, so long as they are limited in number, since a different name might be given to each account. I mean, for instance, if one were to say that "man" means not one thing but several, of which one has the account "two-footed animal", while there are also several others, but limited in number: a name of its own might be given to each account. But if a name were not given, and one were to say that the word means infinitely many things, plainly there would be no speech. Not to mean one thing is to mean nothing, and when words mean nothing, conversation with one another is done away with, and in truth with oneself too; for one cannot think anything without thinking one thing; and if one can, one name might be given to this thing. Let it be granted, then, as was said at the start, that the name means something, and means one thing. It is not possible, then, that being a man means just what not being a man means, if "man" not only is said of one thing but also means one thing (for we do not count meaning one thing as being said of one thing, since then "musical", "white" and "man" would mean one thing, so that all things would be one, for they would all be synonymous). And it will not be possible to be and not to be the same thing, except by homonymy, as if what we call "man" others were to call "not-man". But the puzzle is not this, whether the same thing can at the same time be and not be a man in name, but in fact. If "man" and "not-man" mean nothing different, plainly not being a man will mean nothing different from being a man, so that being a man will be not being a man: they will be one. For this is what being one means, as with "cloak" and "garment", that the account is one. And if they are one, "being a man" and "not being a man" will mean one thing. But it has been shown that they mean different things. So if it is true to say of anything that it is a man, it must be a two-footed animal, since this was what "man" meant; and if this is necessary, it is not possible for that same thing not to be a two-footed animal, since this is what being necessary means, that it is impossible for it not to be. It is not possible, then, that it be true at the same time to say that the same thing is a man and is not a man.
!note "Not everything is 'so and not so'": the phrase of Plato's Theaetetus (183a), where Socrates shows that if everything is always changing, no answer is more right than another, "so" no more than "not so". Ross notes that the sentence before it is probably out of place.

@p
@greek ὁ δʼ αὐτὸς λόγος καὶ ἐπὶ τοῦ μὴ εἶναι ἄνθρωπον
The same argument holds of not being a man. Being a man and being not-man mean different things, since even being white and being a man are different; and the former are much more opposed, so that they must mean different things. And if he says that "white" too means one and the same thing, we shall again say what was said before, that all things, and not only the opposites, will be one. But if this is not possible, what we said follows, if he answers the question. But if, when asked simply, he adds the negations too, he is not answering the question. Nothing prevents the same thing from being a man, white, and thousands of other things; but still, if asked whether it is true to say that this is a man or not, he must answer what means one thing, and not add that it is also white and large. It is impossible to go through the accidents, which are infinite; so let him go through either all or none. Likewise, then, even if the same thing is ten thousand times a man and not a man, one must not add, in answer to whether it is a man, that it is at the same time also not a man, unless one must also add all the other accidents, all that it is and is not; and if he does this, he is not arguing.

@p
@greek ὅλως δʼ ἀναιροῦσιν οἱ τοῦτο λέγοντες οὐσίαν
In general, those who say this do away with substance and what it is to be. They must say that everything is an accident, and that there is no such thing as just what it is to be a man or to be an animal. For if there is to be something that is just what it is to be a man, this will not be being not-man or not being a man (yet these are its negations); for there was one thing it meant, and this was the substance of something. And to mean the substance of a thing is to mean that its being is nothing else. But if just what it is to be a man is to be just what it is to be not-man, or just what it is not to be a man, it will be something else. So they must say that there will be no such account of anything, but that all things are by accident. For this is how substance and accident are told apart: white is an accident of man because he is white, but is not just what white is. But if everything is said by accident, there will be nothing first of which it is said, if the accident always means a predicate of some underlying thing. It must go on, then, without end. But this is impossible, since not even more than two are joined together. For an accident is not an accident of an accident, unless both are accidents of the same thing: I mean, for instance, the white is musical and the musical white because both are accidents of the man. But Socrates is not musical in this way, that both are accidents of something else. Since, then, some things are said in this way and some in that, those said in the way that white is of Socrates cannot be infinite upwards: Socrates the white, for instance, cannot have another accident besides, since no one thing comes from all of them. Nor will white have another accident, such as musical: this is no more an accident of that than that of this; and at the same time we have distinguished that some are accidents in this way and others as musical is of Socrates; and in the second kind the accident is not an accident of an accident, but only in the first, so that not all things will be said by accident. There will be something, then, that means substance even so. And if so, it has been shown that contradictories cannot be said at the same time.
!flag And in general those who use this argument do away with substance and essence | Ross's "essence" is τὸ τί ἦν εἶναι, "what it is to be", as everywhere in this work; and his "being essentially man" is τὸ ὅπερ ἀνθρώπῳ εἶναι, "just what it is to be a man".

@p
@greek ἔτι εἰ ἀληθεῖς αἱ ἀντιφάσεις ἅμα κατὰ τοῦ αὐτοῦ
Again, if contradictories are all true of the same thing at the same time, plainly all things will be one. The same thing will be a warship, a wall and a man, if it is possible to affirm or deny anything of everything, as it must be for those who state the argument of Protagoras. If a man seems to someone not to be a warship, plainly he is not a warship; so he also is one, if the contradiction is true. And we get the saying of Anaxagoras, "all things together", so that nothing truly is. They seem, then, to be speaking of the indefinite, and while they think they are speaking of being, they speak of what is not; for what is in potency and not in actuality is the indefinite. But they must say of everything the affirmation or the negation of everything. It is strange if of each thing its own negation is to belong, while the negation of something else, which does not belong to it, is not to belong. I mean, for instance, if it is true to say of a man that he is not a man, plainly it is also true that he is not a warship. If, then, the affirmation belongs, the negation must too; and if the affirmation does not belong, the negation will belong even more than its own negation. If, then, that one belongs, the negation of warship will belong too; and if this, the affirmation too. These results, then, follow for those who state this argument, and also that it is not necessary either to affirm or to deny. For if it is true that something is a man and not a man, plainly it will also be neither a man nor not a man: to the two there are two negations; and if the first is taken as one made of both, the second too would be one, opposite to it.
!flag for that which is being in capacity, and not in energy, is the indefinite | Taylor's "capacity" and "energy" are δύναμις and ἐντελέχεια, "potency" and "actuality". Here the Greek has ἐντελέχεια, Aristotle's other word for actuality, "being at the end", which Taylor renders "energy" too.
!note "All things together" is the opening of Anaxagoras' book (fragment 1 in Diels): before intellect set them apart, all things were mixed. Protagoras' argument is "man is the measure of all things", examined in chapter 5 and in Plato's Theaetetus (152a). A trireme, a ship with three banks of oars, was the Athenian warship.

@p
@greek ἔτι ἤτοι περὶ ἅπαντα οὕτως ἔχει
Again, either it is so in every case, and something is white and not white, and being and not being, and likewise with the other assertions and negations; or it is so in some cases and not in others. If not in all, these would be agreed. But if in all, again either the negation holds wherever the affirmation does, and the affirmation wherever the negation does; or the negation holds wherever the affirmation does, but the affirmation not always where the negation does. And if it is the latter, there would be something firmly not, and this would be a firm opinion; and if not being is something firm and known, the opposite affirmation would be more known. But if what one denies one must likewise affirm, then either it is true to say them apart, for instance that it is white and again that it is not white, or not. If it is not true to say them apart, he does not say them, and nothing is; but how could what is not speak or walk? And all things would be one, as was said before, and the same would be a man, a god and a warship, and their contradictories. For if it is likewise for each, nothing will differ from another; for if it differs, this will be true and proper to it. And likewise, if it is possible to speak truly saying them apart, what was said follows.

@p
@greek ἔτι ἆρα ὁ μὲν ἢ ἔχειν πως
Further, it follows that all would speak truly and all falsely, and he admits himself to be wrong. At the same time it is plain that with him the inquiry is about nothing, for he says nothing. He says neither "so" nor "not so", but both "so" and "not so"; and again he denies both of these, "neither so nor not so"; for otherwise there would already be something definite. Again, if when the affirmation is true the negation is false, and when this is true the affirmation is false, it would not be possible to affirm and deny the same thing truly at the same time; but perhaps they might say that this is the very thing at issue. Again, is the one wrong who supposes that a thing is so, or that it is not so, and the one who supposes both right? If he is right, what could it mean that the nature of things is such? And if he is not right, but more right than the one who supposes the other way, the things that are would already be in a certain way, and this would be true, and not at the same time also not true. But if all likewise are both wrong and right, one who is so can neither speak nor say anything, since he says at the same time both this and not this. And if he supposes nothing, but alike thinks and does not think, how would he differ from a plant? From this it is most plain that no one, neither of those who state this argument nor anyone else, is in this state. Why does he walk to Megara and not stay at home, thinking he is walking? Why does he not walk straight into a well or over a cliff early one morning, if he happens on one, but plainly takes care, as not thinking that falling in is alike good and not good? Plainly he supposes the one better and the other not better. And if so, he must also suppose that one thing is a man and another not a man, one thing sweet and another not sweet. For he does not seek and suppose all things alike, when, thinking it better to drink water or to see a man, he then seeks these; yet he should, if the same thing were alike a man and not a man. But, as was said, there is no one who does not plainly avoid some things and not others. So, it seems, all suppose that things are without qualification, if not about all things, at least about the better and the worse. And if they do this not knowing but believing, they should care all the more for the truth, as one who is sick cares more for health than one who is healthy; for one who believes, compared with one who knows, is not in a healthy state with regard to the truth.
!note Megara is a day's walk from Athens; Euclides of Megara, Socrates' companion, founded a school there known for its arguments. The comparison of belief with sickness and knowledge with health is Plato's too (Republic 444c-e, of the soul's health).

@p
@greek ἔτι εἰ ὅτι μάλιστα πάντα οὕτως ἔχει
Again, however much all things are "so and not so", still the more and the less are in the nature of things. We should not say that two and three are likewise even, nor is one who thinks four things are five as wrong as one who thinks they are a thousand. If they are not wrong alike, plainly the one is less wrong, and so more right. If, then, what is more is nearer, there must be some truth to which the more true is nearer. And even if there is not, still there is already something firmer and truer, and we shall be rid of the unmixed argument, which prevents us from marking off anything in thought.

@ch Metaph.IV.5 | Protagoras and the truth of appearances; the flux
Protagoras' saying, that all that seems is true, comes from the same opinion: if contrary opinions are both true, the same thing is and is not. Some are led to it by puzzles about sensible things: contraries come from the same thing, and things appear differently to different people, animals and states. They took being to be only the sensible, and knowledge sensation; and seeing everything change, Cratylus would only move his finger. The answers: being is said in two ways, potentially and actually; a thing is known by its form; the changing region round us is a small part of the whole; there is an unchanging nature; appearance is not sensation; and the cause of sensation is prior to it.
@taylor 4

@p
The saying of Protagoras too comes from the same opinion, and both must alike be or not be. If all that is believed and all that appears is true, everything must be at the same time true and false. Many people suppose contrary things to one another, and think that those who do not believe what they believe are wrong, so that the same thing must both be and not be. And if this is so, all that is believed must be true: those who are wrong and those who are right believe opposite things to each other; so if things are like this, all will be right. Plainly, then, both come from the same thinking.
!note Protagoras' saying, "man is the measure of all things, of things that are, that they are, and of things that are not, that they are not", is examined at length in Plato's Theaetetus (152a-179b), which Aristotle follows closely in this chapter and the next.

@p
@greek ἔστι δʼ οὐχ ὁ αὐτὸς τρόπος πρὸς ἅπαντας
But the same way of meeting them does not fit all: some need persuasion and some force. Those who have come to suppose this from being puzzled have ignorance that is easily cured, since one meets not their words but their thought. But those who say it for argument's sake can be cured only by refuting the argument in its sounds and its words. Those who are puzzled have come to this opinion from sensible things. They think that contradictories and contraries hold at the same time, because they see contraries coming to be from the same thing. If, then, what is not cannot come to be, the thing was there before as both alike, as Anaxagoras says that everything is mixed in everything, and Democritus too: for he says the void and the full are likewise in every part, though one of them is being and the other not-being. To those who suppose it from these reasons, we shall say that in a way they speak rightly and in a way they do not know. For "being" is said in two ways, so that in a way something can come to be from what is not, and in a way not; and the same thing can at the same time be and not be, but not in the same respect. The same thing can in potency be both contraries at the same time, but not in actuality. And further we shall ask them to suppose that among the things that are there is also another kind of substance, to which neither motion nor perishing nor coming to be belongs at all.
!flag but to their dianoetic part | Taylor's "dianoetic part" renders διάνοια, "thought", the soul's power of reasoning; the modern version says "thought". Ross's page has lost these lines (1009a18-22), and the modern version follows the Greek.
!note "Another kind of substance" with no motion, perishing or coming to be: the unmoved substance of Book Λ, and for the Platonists the intelligible world of Plato's Timaeus (27d-28a), "what always is and has no becoming".

@p
@greek δὲ καὶ ἡ περὶ τὰ φαινόμενα ἀλήθεια
Likewise the truth of appearances has come to some from sensible things. They think that the truth should not be judged by the many or the few, and that the same thing seems sweet to some who taste it and bitter to others, so that if all were sick or all mad, and two or three healthy or sane, these would seem the sick and the mad, and not the others. Further, many of the other animals receive contrary appearances to ours about the same things, and to each person himself things do not always seem the same to his senses. Which of these, then, are true or false is unclear: these are no more true than those, but alike. That is why Democritus says that either nothing is true, or it is unclear to us at least. And in general, because they suppose that wisdom, that is, thought, is sensation, and sensation an alteration, they say that what appears to the senses must be true. For these reasons Empedocles and Democritus, and, one might say, each of the others, have been caught by opinions of this kind. Empedocles says that when people change their state they change their thought: "for thought grows in human beings according to what is present". And elsewhere he says, "so far as they change, so far it comes to them always to think other things". And Parmenides speaks in the same way: "as each has the mixture of his much-bending limbs, so is intellect present to men; for it is the same thing that thinks, the nature of the limbs, in each and all men; for the more is thought". A saying of Anaxagoras to some of his companions is remembered too, that things would be for them such as they supposed them. And they say that Homer too plainly held this opinion, because he made Hector, when he was lying senseless from the blow, think other thoughts, as if those out of their mind also think, but not the same things. Plainly, then, if both are thinking, things too are at the same time so and not so. And it is here that the result is hardest. If those who have seen most of the truth possible for us (and these are those who seek it and love it most) hold such opinions and say these things about the truth, how should those trying to do philosophy not lose heart? To seek the truth would be to chase birds in flight.
!flag when he was insane through a wound | Taylor's "insane" is too strong: the Greek says Hector lay "thinking other things" (ἀλλοφρονέοντα), out of his senses from the blow. Our text of Homer has the word not of Hector but of Euryalus, struck down in the boxing (Iliad XXIII.698).
!note Empedocles' lines are fragments 106 and 108 in Diels; Parmenides' are fragment 16. "Thought" here is φρόνησις, which in these early writers means thinking of any kind, not practical wisdom.

@p
@greek αἴτιον δὲ τῆς δόξης τούτοις ὅτι περὶ
The cause of their opinion is that while they were seeking the truth about the things that are, they supposed that the things that are are only the sensible ones; and in these the nature of the indefinite is very much present, and the nature of being in the sense we have said. That is why what they say is likely, but not true; it is fitting to put it so, rather than as Epicharmus did against Xenophanes. Further, seeing that the whole of this nature is in motion, and that nothing true is said of what changes, they thought that nothing can be said truly about what changes everywhere and in every way. From this supposition grew the most extreme of the opinions mentioned, that of those who say they follow Heraclitus, as Cratylus held, who in the end thought one should say nothing, and only moved his finger, and blamed Heraclitus for saying that one cannot step twice into the same river: he thought one could not do it even once.
!flag that of the professed Heracliteans | Ross's words are right; the Greek has a verb, τῶν φασκόντων ἡρακλειτίζειν, "those who say they Heraclitize", Taylor's "profess to Heraclitize". Cratylus is the speaker of Plato's dialogue named after him, and, Aristotle says in Book Α (987a32), Plato's first teacher in philosophy.
!note Epicharmus, the comic poet of Sicily, said something of Xenophanes' views that is lost; Ross guesses it was that they were "neither true nor likely", or "true but not likely". Heraclitus' river is his fragment 91 in Diels; Plato's Cratylus (402a) quotes it.

@p
@greek ἡμεῖς δὲ καὶ πρὸς τοῦτον τὸν λόγον
But we shall say in answer to this argument too that there is some reason for their thinking that what changes, when it is changing, is not. Yet it is disputable: for what is losing something has something of what is being lost, and something of what is coming to be must already be. And in general, if a thing is perishing, something will be there that is; and if it is coming to be, there must be that from which it comes to be and that by which it is generated, and this cannot go on without end. But leaving these aside, let us say this: it is not the same to change in quantity and in quality. Let it be granted that in quantity a thing does not stay the same; but it is by its form that we know each thing. Further, it would be fair to blame those who suppose this, because they declared about the whole heaven what they saw in only a few even of the sensible things. Only the region of the sensible round us is always perishing and coming to be; but this is, so to speak, not even a part of the whole, so that it would have been more just to acquit these things for the sake of those than to condemn those for the sake of these. Further, plainly we shall likewise say to them what was said before: we must show them and convince them that there is an unmoved nature. And indeed it follows for those who say that things at the same time are and are not, that all things are at rest rather than in motion: there is nothing into which they can change, since everything already belongs to everything.
!note The region round us: the world below the moon, where things come to be and perish; above it the heavens move eternally and unchangingly (On the Heavens I.3). The Platonists drew the same contrast in Plato's Timaeus and Phaedo (109b-111c).

@p
@greek περὶ δὲ τῆς ἀληθείας, ὡς οὐ πᾶν
About the truth, that not everything that appears is true: first, even if sensation, at least of what is proper to it, is not false, appearance is not the same as sensation. Then it is fair to wonder whether they are really puzzled whether magnitudes and colours are such as they appear to those far off or to those near, to the healthy or to the sick; whether the heavier things are those that seem so to the weak or to the strong; and whether the true things are those that appear to the sleeping or to the waking. Plainly they do not think so: no one, at any rate, if he supposes one night that he is at Athens when he is in Libya, sets out for the Odeum. Again, about the future, as Plato too says, the opinion of the physician and that of the ignorant are surely not likewise authoritative, about whether someone will get well or not. Again, among the senses themselves, the sense of what is foreign to it and of what is its own, or of what is near to it and what belongs to it, are not of equal weight: of colour sight judges, not taste, and of flavour taste, not sight; and none of them ever says, at the same time and about the same thing, that it is at the same time so and not so. Not even at another time does a sense disagree about the quality, but only about the thing the quality belongs to. I mean, for instance, the same wine might seem, if it changed or the body changed, at one time sweet and at another not sweet; but the sweet, such as it is when it is, has never yet changed, and one is always right about it, and what is to be sweet is of necessity such. Yet all these arguments do away with this: as there is no substance of anything, so nothing is of necessity; for the necessary cannot be in one way and in another, so that if anything is of necessity, it will not be "so and not so".
!note "As Plato too says": Theaetetus 171e and 178b-179a, where Socrates argues against Protagoras that about what will be, the expert's judgement is better than anyone else's. The Odeum was the concert hall at Athens, built by Pericles.

@p
@greek ὅλως τʼ εἴπερ ἔστι τὸ αἰσθητὸν μόνον
And in general, if only the sensible is, there would be nothing if there were no living things, since there would be no sensation. That there would be neither sensible things nor sensations is perhaps true (for it is an affection of the one who senses); but that the underlying things that produce the sensation should not be, even without sensation, is impossible. For sensation is surely not of itself, but there is something else besides the sensation, which must be prior to the sensation; for what moves is prior by nature to what is moved, and none the less so if they are said with reference to each other.
!note What causes the sensation is prior to it, "for what moves is prior by nature to what is moved". The Platonists took the argument further: the sensible itself depends on what is prior to it, the intelligible (Proclus, on the Timaeus, Book I).

@ch Metaph.IV.6 | Who is the judge? Appearance is relative; contraries too cannot hold together
Those who ask who is to judge, the healthy or the sick, seek a proof of the starting-point of proof. Those who want to be forced by argument ask for the impossible. If what appears is true, it is true only for the one to whom it appears, when and as it appears, and then everything is relative to opinion. Since contradictories cannot be true together, contraries cannot belong together either, since one of two contraries is a privation.
@taylor 5

@p
There are some, both of those who are convinced of these things and of those who only state these arguments, who raise the puzzle of who is to judge the healthy person, and in general who is to judge rightly about each thing. Such puzzles are like being puzzled whether we are now asleep or awake. All such puzzles come to the same: these people think there should be an account of everything. They seek a starting-point, and seek to get it by proof; yet it is plain from their actions that they are not convinced. But, as we said, this is what happens to them: they seek an account of things of which there is no account, for the starting-point of proof is not proof.

@p
@greek οἱ δʼ ἐν τῷ λόγῳ τὴν βίαν μόνον ζητοῦντες
These, then, could easily be persuaded of this, since it is not hard to grasp. But those who seek only to be forced by argument seek what is impossible: they ask to be allowed to contradict themselves, and they contradict themselves from the start. But if not all things are relative, but some things are themselves by themselves, not everything that appears will be true; for what appears appears to someone; so one who says that all that appears is true makes all things relative. That is why those who seek force in argument, and at the same time want to submit to argument, must guard themselves by saying, not that what appears is, but that what appears is for the one to whom it appears, and when, and in the respect, and in the way it appears. And if they submit to argument, but not in this way, they will soon find themselves saying contrary things. For the same thing may appear to be honey to sight but not to taste, and, since we have two eyes, things may not appear the same to each, if their sight is unlike. To those who say, for the reasons given earlier, that what appears is true, and that therefore all things are likewise false and true, since things do not appear the same to everyone, nor always the same to the same person, but often contrary at the same time (for touch says there are two when the fingers are crossed, and sight says one): to these we shall say, "but not to the same sense, in the same part of it, in the same way, and at the same time", so that what appears would be true with these qualifications. But perhaps that is why those who argue not from a puzzle but for argument's sake must say, not that this is true, but that it is true for this person. And, as was said before, they must make everything relative, to opinion and to sensation, so that nothing has come to be or will be unless someone has first had an opinion about it. But if something has come to be or will be, plainly not everything is relative to opinion. Further, if a thing is one, it is relative to one thing or to a definite one; and if the same thing is both half and equal, still the equal is not relative to the double. If, then, in relation to the one who believes, man and what is believed are the same, man will not be the one who believes, but what is believed. And if each thing is to be relative to the one who believes, the one who believes will be relative to things infinite in kind.

@greek ὅτι μὲν οὖν βεβαιοτάτη δόξα πασῶν
Let this be enough to show that the firmest opinion of all is that opposite statements are not true at the same time; what follows for those who say they are; and why they say so. Since it is impossible for contradictories to be true of the same thing at the same time, plainly contraries cannot belong to the same thing at the same time either. For of contraries one is no less a privation, a privation of substance; and privation is the negation from some definite kind. If, then, it is impossible to affirm and deny truly at the same time, it is also impossible for contraries to belong at the same time, unless both belong in some respect, or one in some respect and the other without qualification.

@ch Metaph.IV.7 | Nothing between contradictories: the excluded middle
There cannot be anything between the two sides of a contradiction: of each thing one must either affirm or deny any one thing. This is plain from what true and false are: to say of what is that it is, or of what is not that it is not, is true. An intermediate could not change into either side; thought either affirms or denies; there would be a middle between truth and falsehood, being and not-being, odd and not-odd, without end. Some came to this view because they could not answer contentious arguments, others because they wanted a reason for everything. Heraclitus seems to make everything true, Anaxagoras everything false.
@taylor 6

@p
But neither can there be anything between the two sides of a contradiction: of one thing one must either affirm or deny any one thing. This is plain, first, to those who define what the true and the false are. To say that what is is not, or that what is not is, is false; to say that what is is, and that what is not is not, is true; so that one who says that something is or is not will speak truly or falsely. But neither what is nor what is not is said not to be or to be. Again, what is between the contradiction will be so either as grey is between black and white, or as what is neither man nor horse is between man and horse. If in the second way, it would not change; for change is from not-good to good, or from good to not-good; but as it is, it is always seen to change, since there is no change except into opposites and what is between them. And if it is between in the first way, so too there would be a coming to be into white that is not from not-white; but as it is, this is not seen.
!flag To say of what is that it is not, or of what is not that it is, is false | Ross's words are right and famous; the definition of truth they give is Aristotle's, and Plato's before him: the Sophist (240d-241a, 263b) and the Cratylus (385b) say that a true statement says the things that are as they are.

@p
@greek ἔτι πᾶν τὸ διανοητὸν καὶ νοητὸν
Again, thought either affirms or denies everything that is thought or intelligible; this is plain from the definition, whenever it speaks truly or falsely. When it puts things together in this way, affirming or denying, it speaks truly; when in that way, falsely. Again, there must be something besides all contradictions, if one is not speaking just for argument's sake; so that someone will speak neither truly nor not truly, and there will be something between being and not-being, so that there will also be a kind of change between coming to be and perishing. Again, in all kinds in which the negation brings in the contrary, there will be this too: among numbers, for instance, a number neither odd nor not odd. But this is impossible, as is plain from the definition. Again, it will go on without end, and the things that are will be not only half as many again, but more. For again it will be possible to deny this, both with respect to the affirmation and to the negation, and this will be something, since its substance is something else. Again, when someone, asked whether something is white, says "no", he has denied nothing but that it is; and not being is a negation.

The Platonists set διάνοια, discursive reasoning, below νοῦς, intellect, which sees without reasoning (Republic 511d-e); here Aristotle uses the two words together for whatever is thought.

This opinion has come to some as other paradoxes have: when they cannot answer contentious arguments, they give in to the argument and agree that the conclusion is true. Some say it for this reason, others because they seek an account of everything. The starting-point against all of them is definition; and definition comes from their having to mean something, since the account of which the name is a sign will be a definition. The argument of Heraclitus, saying that all things are and are not, seems to make all things true; that of Anaxagoras, that there is something between the contradiction, all things false: for when things are mixed, the mixture is neither good nor not good, so that nothing can be said truly.
!note Contentious (eristic) arguments: arguments made to win, not to find the truth, such as those of the brothers in Plato's Euthydemus. Aristotle's Sophistical Refutations shows how to answer them.

@ch Metaph.IV.8 | Not everything is true, nor everything false; not all at rest, nor all in motion
With these points settled, the one-sided views are plainly false: that nothing is true, and that everything is true. Both refute themselves. Nor are all things at rest, or all in motion, or all sometimes at rest and sometimes in motion: there is something that always moves the things in motion, and the first mover is itself unmoved.
@taylor 7

@p
With these distinctions drawn, it is plain that the things said in one way only, and of everything, cannot hold as some say: some say that nothing is true (for they say nothing prevents everything from being like the diagonal's being commensurable), and others that everything is true. These arguments are almost the same as that of Heraclitus: for one who says that all things are true and all false also says each of these apart, so that if they are impossible, both together must be impossible too. Again, there are plainly contradictions that cannot be true at the same time; nor all false, though this would seem more possible from what has been said. But against all such arguments one must ask, as was said in the arguments above, not that something is or is not, but that he mean something; so one must argue from a definition, taking what the false or the true means. If to say truly is nothing other than to deny falsely, it is impossible that everything be false: for one side of the contradiction must be true. Again, if one must either affirm or deny everything, both cannot be false: one side of the contradiction is false. And it happens to all such arguments, as is often said, that they destroy themselves. One who says that everything is true makes the statement contrary to his own true too, so that his own is not true (for the contrary says that it is not true); and one who says that everything is false makes himself false too. And if they make exceptions, the one saying that only the statement contrary to his is not true, and the other that only his own is not false, none the less they will be asking for infinitely many true and false statements: for the one saying that the true statement is true is true, and this will go on without end.
!note That the diagonal of a square is not commensurable with its side was the great discovery of early Greek mathematics, and Aristotle's standard example of what is impossible; Plato's Theaetetus (147d-148b) and Meno (82b-85b) are built on it.

@p
@greek φανερὸν δʼ ὅτι οὐδʼ οἱ πάντα ἠρεμεῖν
Plainly, too, neither those who say that all things are at rest speak truly, nor those who say that all things are in motion. If all things are at rest, the same things will always be true and false; but this plainly changes, for the one who speaks was once not and again will not be. And if all things are in motion, nothing will be true: everything, then, will be false; but it has been shown that this is impossible. Again, it must be what is that changes, since change is from something into something. But neither are all things at rest or in motion sometimes, and nothing always: for there is something that always moves the things that are moved, and the first mover is itself unmoved.
!flag and the first mover must itself be unmoved | Ross's words are right; the note is the Platonists'. The first unmoved mover is the subject of Book Λ (chapters 6 to 9) and of Physics VIII. For the Platonists Aristotle's first mover is intellect, which they set below the One, as Plato's maker of the world in the Timaeus looks to what is above him.
