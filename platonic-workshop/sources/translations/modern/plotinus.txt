@treatise I.2 | On the virtues
Plato says we should escape from this world by becoming like God, through virtue. But God has no need of virtues. Plotinus answers that we can become like something that does not itself have what makes us like it, as a house is like the plan of it. He then sets the virtues in two ranks: the civic virtues, which put measure into our desires and feelings, and the greater virtues, which purify the soul and turn it to intellect. The good person lives by the greater, and his aim is to live the life of the gods, not just the life of a good human being.

@ch I.2.1 | Becoming like God: does God have virtues?
We escape evil by becoming like God. But which god, and does he have the virtues? The world-soul, who has nothing to fear and nothing to desire, cannot have courage or self-control. Then we are made like what does not have virtue: as the house we see is like the house in the mind, which has no need of order.

@p
Evils are here, and they haunt this place by necessity, and the soul wants to escape evils. So we must escape from here. What, then, is the escape? "Becoming like God," Plato says. And this happens "if we become just and holy, with wisdom," and, in short, if we live in virtue. If, then, it is by virtue that we become like, are we made like someone who has virtue? And like which god? Is it the one who seems most of all to have these things, the soul of the world and the ruling power in it, which has a wonderful wisdom? It makes sense that we, being here, should become like this one. But first, it is open to question whether this one has all the virtues. Can it be self-controlled, or brave, when there is nothing it need fear, since nothing is outside it? And when nothing pleasant comes to it that it could desire when it is absent, so as to get it or seize it? But if it, too, reaches out towards the intelligible things that our souls reach for, then plainly our good order and our virtues also come to us from there.
!note "Becoming like God" and "just and holy, with wisdom" are Plato's words in the Theaetetus (176a-b).
!flag and to the intellect which is the leader in it | The Greek says only "the ruling power in it"; Taylor names it intellect, as the later Platonists did.

@p
@greek ἆῤ οὖν ἐκεῖνο ταύτας ἔχει
Does that one have these virtues, then? It is surely not reasonable that it should have the virtues we call civic: practical wisdom, which belongs to the reasoning part; courage, which belongs to the spirited part; self-control, which lies in a kind of agreement and harmony of the desiring part with reason; and justice, which is each of these parts doing its own work, in ruling and being ruled. Then are we made like God not by the civic virtues, but by the greater ones that go by the same names? But if by others, are we not made like him at all by the civic ones? It would be unreasonable to say that we are not made like him by these in any way at all. At any rate tradition calls the people who have them godlike, and we must say that they have become like God in some way. But the likeness comes by the greater virtues. Either way, it follows that God has virtues, even if not virtues of this kind.
!note The civic virtues and their parts of the soul are those of Plato's Republic (book 4, 441c-443b).
!flag For rumor also says, that these are divine | The Greek says that tradition calls these men divine, that is, the people who have the civic virtues; Taylor makes it the virtues.

@p
@greek εἰ οὖν τις συγχωρεῖ
Suppose, then, someone grants that we can be made like him, though we stand differently towards different virtues, and are not made like him by the civic ones. Then nothing stops us from becoming, by our own virtues, like one who has no virtue. How can this be? Like this. If something is warmed by the presence of heat, must the source of the heat also be warmed? And if something is hot by the presence of fire, must fire itself be warmed by the presence of fire? To the first, one might reply that there is heat in fire too, but heat that is part of its nature. So the argument, following the comparison, would make virtue something brought into the soul from outside, but natural to that from which the soul has it by imitation. To the argument from fire, one might reply that that one is virtue itself, and we hold it to be greater than virtue. But if what the soul shares in were the same as that from which it comes, we would have to speak in this way. As it is, the one is different from the other. The house we see is not the same as the intelligible house, though it has been made like it. The house we see shares in arrangement and order; but there, in the plan, there is no arrangement, no order, no proportion. In the same way we receive order, arrangement and agreement from there, and these belong to virtue here. But the things there have no need of agreement, order or arrangement, and so they have no need of virtue either. And we are none the less made like the things there by the presence of virtue. So much, then, to show that virtue need not be there because we are made like that world by virtue. But we must add persuasion to the argument, and not rest on force alone.
!flag but that virtue in him is in reality greater than virtue [because it subsists causally.] | Taylor's words in square brackets are his own explanation, from the later Platonists; they are not in the Greek.

@ch I.2.2 | Two kinds of likeness; the civic virtues
Likeness is of two kinds: between two copies of one original, and between a copy and its original. The civic virtues give measure to our desires and feelings, and so carry a trace of the measure above; the soul, nearer to the divine than the body, can seem a god itself.

@p
First, then, we must take up the virtues by which we say we become like God. We want to find the one thing that, in us, is an imitation and is virtue, but there is a kind of original and is not virtue. Note that likeness is of two kinds. One kind requires something the same in the things that are alike: it holds between things made equally like each other from the same source. But where one thing is made like another, and that other comes first, does not stand in the same relation back to it, and is not called like it, likeness must be understood in another way. We do not require the same form in both, but rather a different one, since the likeness came about in this second way.

@p
@greek τί ποτε οὖν ἐστιν ἡ ἀρετὴ
What, then, is virtue, both virtue as a whole and each virtue? The account will be clearer if we take each one, for in this way what they have in common, which makes them all virtues, will easily become plain. The civic virtues, which we spoke of above, really do give order to us and make us better, while we still remain and live here. They do it by setting bounds and measures to our desires, and to our passions in general, and by removing false opinions. They do it by what is altogether better, and by setting bounds, since what has measure stands outside the unmeasured and the unbounded. And the virtues themselves, being bounded, in so far as they are measures in the soul as in matter, have been made like the measure there, and hold a trace of the best that is there. What is wholly without measure is matter, and it is wholly unlike. But in so far as a thing shares in form, it is made like that which is without form. Things nearer share more. Soul is nearer than body and more akin, so it shares more: so much that, appearing as a god, it deceives us into thinking that this is all there is of God. That, then, is how these people are made like God.
!flag and taking away false opinions from a more excellent nature | The Greek says the virtues remove false opinions "by what is altogether better", the means by which they work; it does not say "from a more excellent nature".
!flag Perhaps, too, these measures are in soul as in matter | There is no "perhaps" in the Greek here: the virtues are measures in the soul as in matter.

@ch I.2.3 | The greater virtues are purifications
Plato calls the virtues purifications, so there are two kinds. The soul is bad when it is mixed up with the body; it is good when it thinks on its own, does not share the body's feelings, does not fear to leave it, and lets reason lead. This is likeness to God. But God is not in a state of virtue: virtue belongs to the soul, not to intellect or what is beyond it.

@p
But since Plato hints that the likeness is a different one, belonging to a greater virtue, we must speak of that. In doing so the being of civic virtue will also become clearer, and what the greater virtue is in its being, and in general that there is another virtue besides the civic. When Plato says that likeness to God is an escape from the things here, and does not call the virtues of civic life simply virtues but adds "civic", and elsewhere calls them all purifications, he plainly holds that virtues are of two kinds, and that the likeness does not come by the civic kind.
!note "An escape from the things here" is from the Theaetetus (176a-b); the virtues as purifications are in the Phaedo (69b-c); the "civic" virtues are named so in the Republic (430c) and the Phaedo (82a).

@p
@greek πῶς οὖν λέγομεν ταύτας
In what sense, then, do we call these virtues purifications? And how is it that, once purified, we are made like God most of all? It is like this. The soul is bad when it is mixed up with the body, shares its feelings, and agrees with it in all its opinions. So it would be good, and have virtue, if it did not share the body's opinions but acted alone, and that is to use intellect and to be wise; if it did not share the body's feelings, and that is to be self-controlled; if it did not fear being parted from the body, and that is to be brave; and if reason and intellect led, and the rest did not resist, and that would be justice. Someone who called this state of the soul, in which it thinks and is in this way free from passion, a likeness to God would not be wrong. For the divine is pure, and its activity is such that whatever imitates it has wisdom.

@p
@greek τί οὖν; οὐ κἀκεῖνο
Well then, is not that one also in this state? No: it is not in a state at all; the state belongs to the soul. And the soul thinks in a different way. Of the beings there, one thinks in another way, and the other does not think at all. Then is "thinking" just the same word for two different things? Not at all. One thinks first, and the other thinks in a different way that comes from the first. As speech in the voice is an imitation of the speech in the soul, so the speech in the soul is an imitation of the speech in something else. So, as spoken speech is a copy of the speech in the soul, the speech in the soul, being its interpreter, is a copy of what comes before it. Virtue, then, belongs to the soul. It does not belong to intellect, nor to what is beyond intellect.
!note The Workshop takes "one" and "the other" of the beings there to be intellect, which thinks in its own way, and the One, which does not think, as the last sentence of the chapter suggests.
!flag is divided when compared with it | Taylor has "divided" twice here; Volkmann's Greek has μεμιμημένος, "imitated", and the modern version follows the Greek shown.

@ch I.2.4 | Purification, turning, and the soul's own good
Is virtue the purifying, or the having been purified? Purification only removes what is foreign; the soul's good is to be with what is akin to it, and it reaches it by turning. Its virtue is what comes from the turning: a seeing of the realities, whose imprints the soul held unlit and now lights up.

@p
We must ask whether purification is the same as this kind of virtue, or whether purification comes first and virtue follows. And does virtue lie in being purified, or in having been purified? Virtue in the process of being purified is less complete than virtue in having been purified, for having been purified is already a kind of goal. But having been purified is the removal of everything foreign, and the good is something other than that. Or, if the soul was good before the purification, is purification enough? Purification will be enough; but what is left will be the good, not the purification. And we must ask what it is that is left. Perhaps the nature that is left was not the good itself, or it would not have come to be in evil. Should we call it good-like, then? Yes, but not able to stay in what is truly good, for it is made so that it can go either way. Its good, then, is to be with what is akin to it, and its evil is to be with the opposite. So it must be with what is akin to it, once it is purified, and it will be with it by turning to it. Does it turn, then, after the purification? Rather, after the purification it has turned. Is this, then, its virtue? Rather, its virtue is what comes to be in it from the turning. What is that? A seeing, and an imprint of what is seen, set in it and at work, as sight is at work on what it sees.
!flag if the soul was good prior to her impurity | The Greek says "before the purification", not "before her impurity".

@p
@greek οὐκ ἄρα εἶχεν αὐτὰ
Did the soul not have these things already, then, and does it not recollect them? It had them, but not at work: they lay stored away, unlit. For them to be lit, and for the soul then to know that they are in it, it must come close to what gives light. And it had not the things themselves but imprints of them, so it must fit the imprint to the true things of which the imprints are imprints. Perhaps this is also why the soul is said to have them: intellect is not foreign to it, and least of all foreign when the soul looks towards it. If it does not, intellect is foreign even when it is present. So too our kinds of knowledge are foreign to us if we never act by them at all.
!flag She did not, therefore, possess these, nor recollect them | The Greek is a question ("Did it not have them, then, and does it not recollect?"), which the next sentence answers; Taylor makes it a statement.
!flag when it looks to the illuminating cause | The Greek says "when it looks towards it", that is, when the soul looks to intellect; "the illuminating cause" is Taylor's.

@ch I.2.5 | How far purification goes
The purified soul draws itself together away from the body. It takes bodily pleasures only as cures, bears pain calmly, removes anger and fear as far as it can, and desires nothing base. It wants to make its lower part pure too, so that this part respects reason, as a man respects a wise neighbour.

@p
We must say how far purification goes, for in this way it will be clear to what the likeness is, and with which god the soul becomes the same. The question is chiefly this: how the soul purifies anger and desire and all the rest, pain and its kin, and how far it can be separated from the body. From the body, perhaps, by drawing itself together, as it were, into its own place; and in any case by being free from passion. It makes the necessary pleasures and the perceptions of the senses only cures and reliefs from pain, so that it is not troubled. It takes away pains, and if it cannot, it bears them calmly and makes them smaller by not feeling with the body. It takes away anger as far as it can, entirely if possible; and if not, at least it does not join in the anger itself, but the involuntary movement belongs to something else, and is slight and weak. Fear it takes away entirely, for it will fear nothing; though here too there may be something involuntary, except where fear serves as a warning.

@p
@greek ἐπιθυμία δὲ ὅτι μὲν
As for desire, plainly the soul will desire nothing base. It will not itself desire food and drink for relaxation, nor sex either. If it does at all, I think it will desire what nature needs, with nothing involuntary in it; and if there is something involuntary, it will go no further than a passing image in the mind. In short, the soul itself will be pure of all these things, and it will want to make the unreasoning part pure too, so that this part is not struck by them; or, if it is, not hard, but its blows will be few and quickly undone by the nearness of reason. It is as if a man lived next door to a wise man and gained from being his neighbour, either by becoming like him, or by being so in awe of him that he dared to do nothing the good man would not want.
!flag And as to the desire of meats and drinks for the sake of a remission of pain | The Greek has "for relaxation" (anesis); "of pain" is Taylor's addition.

@p
@greek οὔκουν ἔσται μάχη
So there will be no struggle. The presence of reason is enough. The worse part will respect it, so that the worse part itself will be vexed if it is moved at all, because it did not keep quiet when its master was there, and it will rebuke itself for its weakness.

@ch I.2.6 | To be a god, not only without fault
The aim is not to be free from fault but to be a god. In the purified person wisdom is the seeing of what intellect holds. Each virtue is twofold: in intellect it is not virtue but the pattern; in the soul it is virtue. The greater justice, self-control and courage are the soul's activity towards intellect, its turning inward, and its freedom from passion.

@p
None of these things is a sin; they are right conduct for a human being. But our aim is not to be free from sin; it is to be a god. If something of this kind still happens involuntarily, such a person would be a god and a daemon at once, a double being; or rather he would have with him another, who has a different virtue. If nothing of the kind happens, he is a god alone: a god of those who follow the first.
!note "Those who follow" recalls the gods and daemons who follow Zeus in the Phaedrus (246e-247a).
!flag but a correction of the man | The Greek word (katorthōsis) means right action, doing well; it is not "correction".

@p
@greek αὐτὸς μὲν γάρ ἐστιν
For he himself is the one who came from there, and his own true self, if he became such as he was when he came, is there. Coming here, he was settled together with intellect, and he will make "this" like himself, as far as its power allows, so that, if possible, it is not struck at all, or at least does nothing that does not please its master.
!flag But coming hither, he will associate with intellect ; and will assimilate this to himself | A hard sentence. Volkmann's Greek has "with intellect" (νῷ), as Taylor does; read so, it is not clear what "this" is that the man makes like himself. From what follows ("not struck", "its master") it seems to be the lower part he lives with here. The modern version keeps the Greek shown and marks "this".

@p
@greek τίς οὖν ἑκάστη
What, then, is each virtue for such a man? Wisdom, both theoretical and practical, lies in contemplating what intellect holds; but intellect holds it by direct contact. Each of the two is double: one is in intellect, the other in soul. There it is not virtue; in the soul it is virtue. What is it there, then? Intellect's own activity, and what intellect is. Here, what comes from there and is in another is virtue. For justice itself, and each virtue itself, is not virtue, but a kind of pattern; what comes from it into the soul is virtue. Virtue is the virtue of someone; but each thing itself belongs to itself, and not to anything else.
!flag But he will possess intellect by contact | The Greek says that intellect has what it holds by direct contact; it does not say that the man possesses intellect.

@p
@greek δικαιοσύνη δὲ εἴπερ οἰκειοπραγία
If justice is doing one's own work, is it always found in a number of parts? Or is there one justice in a number of parts, when there are many parts, and another that is simply doing one's own work, even of a single thing? True justice itself, at any rate, is of one thing towards itself, in which there is not one part and another. So for the soul too, the greater justice is to act towards intellect; self-control is the turning inward towards intellect; and courage is freedom from passion, in likeness to that which the soul looks towards, which is by nature free from passion. In the soul, this freedom comes from virtue, so that it does not share the feelings of the worse partner it lives with.
!flag Hence justice in the soul is to energize in a greater degree intellectually | The Greek says that the greater justice of the soul is its activity towards intellect; "greater" belongs to the justice, not to the activity.

@ch I.2.7 | The virtues follow one another; the life of the gods
The virtues go together in the soul, as their patterns do in intellect. Whoever has the greater virtues has the lesser ones in potential. The good person will use the lesser when circumstances call for them, but will live by the higher measures, and choose not the life of a good human being but the life of the gods.

@p
These virtues in the soul, then, follow one another, as, there in intellect, do the patterns that come before virtue. For there, thinking is knowledge and wisdom; being turned towards itself is self-control; doing its own work is its justice; and what is like courage is its sameness, its staying pure by itself. In the soul, then, the seeing directed towards intellect is wisdom, theoretical and practical: these are virtues of the soul, for the soul is not these things itself, as intellect is there. The other virtues follow in the same way. And with purification too: since all the virtues are purifications, in the sense of having been purified, the soul must have all of them, or none will be complete. Whoever has the greater virtues necessarily has the lesser ones too, in potential; but whoever has the lesser ones does not necessarily have the greater.
!flag and that which is as it were fortitude is immateriality | Volkmann's Greek has ταυτότης, "sameness"; Taylor has "immateriality", which is not what the Greek shown says. The modern version follows the Greek.

@p
@greek ὁ μὲν δὴ προηγούμενος
This, then, is the life that is the chief aim of the good man. Whether he has the lesser or the greater virtues in act, or in some other way, must be looked at virtue by virtue: practical wisdom, for example. If it is going to use other principles, how can the lesser one still remain, even if it is not at work? And what if one virtue by nature goes so far and another so far, and the one self-control sets measures to the passions, while the other takes them away altogether? The same holds for the other virtues, once practical wisdom has been moved. Or perhaps he will know them, and how much he will take from them; and perhaps at times, as circumstances call for it, he will act by some of them. But when he reaches higher principles and other measures, he will act by those. He will not, for example, place self-control in that measure, but will separate himself altogether, as far as he can. And he will not live the life of the good human being that civic virtue thinks right; he will leave it behind and choose another, the life of the gods. For his likeness is to be to them, and not to good human beings. Likeness to good human beings is the likeness of one image to another, each made like the same original. Likeness to the other is likeness to the pattern itself.
!flag For if it uses the other virtues | The Greek says "if it is going to use other principles" (archai); Taylor has "the other virtues".

@treatise I.3 | On dialectic
What leads us up to the Good? Plato names three kinds of people who can be led up: the musician, the lover and the philosopher. Each starts from a different place, and Plotinus says how each is to be led. All of them must come at last to dialectic, which is not a tool for arguing but the highest part of philosophy: knowledge of what each thing is, which rests in the intelligible world and hands logic over to others. The lower virtues and wisdom grow together and complete each other.

@ch I.3.1 | Who can be led up, and the musician
The journey has two stages: up to the intelligible world, and then through it to its summit. Plato names three natures fit for the first: the musician, the lover and the philosopher. The musician, stirred by beautiful sounds, must be taught that what moved him was the harmony and beauty of the intelligible world.

@p
What art, method or practice leads us up to where we must go? Where we must go, to the Good and the first principle, let us take as agreed and as shown by many arguments. And indeed the arguments by which it was shown were themselves a kind of leading up.

@p
@greek τίνα δὲ δεῖ εἶναι
What kind of man must he be who is to be led up? Surely the one who, as Plato says, has seen all things, or most things, and who "at his first birth entered into the seed of a man who will be a philosopher, or a musician, or a lover". The philosopher by nature, the musician and the lover are the ones to be led up. What, then, is the way? Is it one and the same for all of them, or is there one for each? For all of them the journey has two stages: one for those going up, and one for those who have arrived above. The first starts from the things below. The second is for those who are already in the intelligible world and have, as it were, set foot there: they must travel on until they reach the furthest point of the region, which is the end of the journey, when one stands on the summit of the intelligible. But let that wait. Let us first try to speak about the leading up.
!note Plato's words are from the Phaedrus (248d): the soul that has seen most of the realities is born as a lover of wisdom, a lover of beauty, a musician or a lover.

@p
@greek πρῶτον δὴ διασταλτέον
First, then, we must tell these men apart, beginning with the musician, and say what he is by nature. We must take him to be easily moved and excited by beauty, but less able to be moved from within himself; he is ready to respond to whatever strikes him, as it were. As timid people start at noises, so he starts at musical sounds and the beauty in them. He always flees what is out of tune and lacks unity in songs, and pursues what has good rhythm and good shape in rhythms. After these sounds and rhythms and shapes that we perceive, then, he must be led like this. We must separate the matter in which the proportions and ratios are found, and lead him to the beauty that is in them. He must be taught that what excited him was the intelligible harmony and the beauty in it, and beauty as a whole, not only one particular beauty. And the arguments of philosophy must be put into him, by which he is to be led to believe in what he has without knowing it. What these arguments are, we shall say later.
!flag yet is not disposed to be moved from himself | The Greek says he is "less able to be moved from within himself", that is, he needs something outside to move him; Taylor's words come near this.

@ch I.3.2 | The lover
The lover remembers beauty but cannot grasp it apart from bodies. He must be led, as in Plato's Symposium, from one beautiful body to all of them, then to beautiful ways of life and laws, arts, sciences and virtues, and from the virtues up to intellect and being.

@p
The lover, whom the musician may turn into, and having turned may either stay as or go beyond, has a kind of memory of beauty. But he cannot grasp it on its own, apart from bodies; struck by the beautiful things he sees, he is excited about them. He must be taught, then, not to fall excited before one body, but must be led by reasoning to all bodies, and shown what is the same in all of them. He must be told that it is other than bodies and comes from elsewhere, and that it is found more in other things: he must be shown beautiful ways of life and beautiful laws (for this already accustoms him to what is lovable in things without body), and that there is beauty in the arts, in the kinds of knowledge and in the virtues. Then all these must be made one, and he must be taught how beauty comes to be in them. From the virtues he must now go up to intellect, to being; and there he must take the upward road.
!note The ascent from one beautiful body to all of them, to ways of life and laws, and to the kinds of knowledge, is Diotima's in Plato's Symposium (210a-d).

@ch I.3.3 | The philosopher
The philosopher by nature is already winged and moving upward, and needs only to be shown the way: first mathematics, to train him in thinking of what has no body; then the completion of his virtues; then dialectic.

@p
The philosopher by nature is ready for it, winged, as it were, and does not need to be separated from bodies as these others do. He is already moving upward, and lacks only someone to show him the way. So he must be shown, and set free, since he wants it himself and has long been free by nature. He must be given mathematics, to accustom him to thinking of what has no body, and to believing in it; and he will take to it easily, since he loves learning. Being virtuous by nature, he must be led on to the completion of his virtues. And after mathematics he must be given the arguments of dialectic, and made, in short, a dialectician.

@ch I.3.4 | What dialectic is
Dialectic is the power to say of each thing what it is, how it differs from others and what it shares with them, and to do this by knowledge, not opinion. It works in the intelligible world, dividing and combining the Forms until it reaches the first principle; then it rests, and leaves logic to another art.

@p
What is dialectic, which must be handed on to the first two as well? It is the state of mind that can say, in reasoned speech, about each thing, what it is, how it differs from other things, and what it has in common with them; among which things it belongs, and where each of these stands; whether it is what it is; how many things there are that are, and again the things that are not, which are other than the things that are. It discusses the good and what is not good, the things that fall under the good and those that fall under its opposite, and what is eternal and what is not; and all of it by knowledge, of course, not by opinion.
!note "The things that are not, which are other than the things that are" is the teaching of Plato's Sophist (257b-259b): what is not is not nothing, but what is other.

@p
@greek παύσασα δὲ τῆς περὶ τὸ αἰσθητὸν
It stops wandering about the world we perceive and settles in the intelligible world, and does its work there. It lets falsehood go, and feeds the soul in what is called "the plain of truth". It uses Plato's method of division to tell the Forms apart, to find what each thing is, and to find the first kinds; it weaves together, by intellect, the things that come from these, until it has gone through the whole intelligible world; and then, the other way, it takes them apart again, until it comes back to the first principle. Then it is still, since it is at peace in so far as it is there; it no longer busies itself with many things, but has become one, and looks. What is called logic, about propositions and syllogisms, it hands over to another art, as it would the knowledge of how to write. Some of these things it thinks necessary, as coming before the art; but it judges them, as it judges everything else, and thinks some of them useful and others superfluous, belonging to the method that wants them.
!note "The plain of truth" is from the Phaedrus (248b). Plato's method of division is shown in the Sophist and the Statesman; the "first kinds" are the greatest kinds of the Sophist (254b-255e).
!flag and again, by an analytic process it arrives at that to which it had proceeded from the first | The Greek says that it takes things apart again "until it comes to the principle" (archē), the first principle; Taylor's words do not say this.

@ch I.3.5 | Where dialectic gets its principles; dialectic and philosophy
Intellect gives dialectic its principles. Dialectic is the most valuable part of philosophy, not a tool: it deals with real things, judges falsehood by the truth it holds, and leaves the fine points of propositions to another art.

@p
But where does this knowledge get its principles? Intellect gives clear principles, if a soul can receive them. Then dialectic puts together, weaves and divides what follows from them, until it arrives at complete intellect. For "it is", as Plato says, "the purest part of intellect and wisdom". Since it is the most valuable of the states of mind in us, it must be concerned with being and with what is most valuable: wisdom with being, and intellect with what is beyond being.
!note Plato's words are from the Philebus (58d), where Burnet's text has "the pure" (τὸ καθαρὸν) part of intellect and wisdom; Plotinus, as Volkmann prints him, has "the purest". Volkmann's Greek, as First1KGreek gives it, prints "τὸ ἂν" where the sense needs τὸ ὄν, "being".

@p
@greek τί οὖν ἡ φιλοσοφία
What, then, is philosophy? The most valuable thing. Are philosophy and dialectic the same? Rather, dialectic is the valuable part of philosophy. We must not think that it is a tool of the philosopher's. It is not bare theorems and rules: it deals with things, and has the things that are as its matter, so to speak. It does go to them by a method, but it holds the things together with the theorems. Falsehood and sophistry it knows only incidentally, when another has made them: it judges falsehood as something foreign, by the truths in itself, and knows, when someone presents it, that it goes against the rule of truth. So it does not know about the proposition as such (that would be like knowing letters); but knowing the truth, it knows what they call a proposition. And in general it knows the movements of the soul: what the soul affirms and what it denies, whether it denies what it affirmed or something else, and whether things are different or the same. It grasps whatever is put before it, as sense-perception grasps things, and leaves exact study of these matters to another art, which enjoys it.

@ch I.3.6 | The other parts of philosophy, and the natural virtues
Philosophy also studies nature and conduct, with help from dialectic. Practical wisdom reasons about the virtues as a whole; dialectic and wisdom serve it from above. The lower virtues are incomplete without wisdom, and wisdom cannot come without them: the two grow together.

@p
It is the valuable part, then, since philosophy has other parts too. It studies nature, taking help from dialectic, as the other arts make use of arithmetic, though philosophy draws on dialectic more closely. And it studies conduct in the same way, contemplating from there, and adding the habits and the exercises from which the habits come. The habits of reason hold what comes from there as already their own; for most of the moral habits come with matter. The other virtues apply reasoning to particular feelings and actions; but practical wisdom is a kind of reasoning above these, more concerned with the universal: whether the virtues go together, and whether to hold back now or later, or whether something else altogether is better. Dialectic and wisdom, more universally still and without matter, bring everything forward for practical wisdom to use.

@p
@greek πότερα δὲ ἔστι τὰ κάτω
Can the lower virtues exist without dialectic and wisdom? Yes, but incompletely, with something lacking. And can one be wise and a dialectician in this way without the lower ones? That could not happen: they must come first, or grow up together. Perhaps a person may have natural virtues, from which the complete virtues come when wisdom is added. Wisdom, then, comes after the natural virtues, and then completes one's character. Or rather, when the natural virtues are there, both grow together and are completed together; or one, coming first, completes the other. For natural virtue in general has an incomplete eye and an incomplete character, and the principles from which we have them both count for most in both.

@treatise II.4 | On matter
Everyone who has thought about matter agrees that it is a substrate, a receiver of forms; they differ about what it is. Plotinus first argues that there is a matter in the intelligible world too: the depth beneath the forms there, unbounded until it turns to the One. He then turns to the matter of bodies. It is needed because the elements change into one another; it has no quality and no size, and is known only by a "bastard reasoning". It is not a quality, nor a privation added to something else: it is the unlimited itself, poverty itself, and so evil.

@ch II.4.1 | What the schools say matter is
All agree that matter is a substrate and a receiver of forms, but not what it is. The Stoics, who hold that only bodies exist, make matter the one substance under the elements, a body without quality, and even make God matter in a certain state. Others call it incorporeal, and some posit a second matter in the intelligible world, under the forms there.

@p
Those who have come to think about the nature called matter all say the same thing about it up to a point: that it is a substrate, something underlying, and a receiver of forms. So far they go along together. But when they go on to ask what this underlying nature is, how it receives, and what it receives, they part ways. Some hold that only bodies are real, and that substance lies in them. They say that matter is one, that it underlies the elements, and that it is itself substance; everything else, the elements included, is a kind of affection of matter, matter in a certain state. They even dare to carry it up as far as the gods, and in the end they make God himself this matter in a certain state. They also give it body, calling it a body without quality, and they give it size. Others say it is incorporeal. And some of these say there is not just one matter: this one underlies bodies, they agree, and it is the matter the first group speaks of; but there is another, prior to it, in the intelligible world, which underlies the forms there and the incorporeal substances.
!note The first group are the Stoics, for whom God is matter "in a certain state" and matter a "body without quality".
!flag They, likewise, give a body to matter, calling it, body mid of quality | Taylor's "body mid of quality" is the scan's misreading of "body void of quality"; the Greek has a body without quality (apoion sōma).

@ch II.4.2 | Objections to a matter in the intelligible world
If matter is unbounded and formless, it cannot be among the best things there; simple things need no matter; only things that come to be need it. And where would it come from? If it was made, something made it; if it is eternal, there are many first principles; and form added to it would make a body there.

@p
So we must first ask about this other matter: whether it exists, what it is, and how it exists. If matter has to be something unbounded and shapeless, and there is nothing unbounded or shapeless among the beings there, which are the best of all, there would be no matter there. And if each thing there is simple, it would have no need of matter, of the kind from which, with something else, a composite is made. Things that come to be, and things that make one thing out of another, need matter; it was from these that the matter of sensible things was first conceived. Things that do not come to be do not need it. Again, where did it come from, and how did it come to exist? If it came to be, something made it. If it is eternal, there are several first principles, and the first things exist by chance. And if form comes to it, the composite will be a body, so that there will be body there too.
!flag and first natures will have a casual subsistence | "Casual" here means by chance (kata syntychian): if matter is eternal beside the first principles, the first things are together by accident.

@ch II.4.3 | The unbounded is not always to be despised
Not everything unbounded and shapeless is to be scorned, if it offers itself to what is better, as soul does to intellect. Composition there is not like that of bodies. The matter of things that come to be takes one form after another; the matter there has all forms at once, and so is never without form.

@p
First, then, we must say that the unbounded is not to be despised everywhere, nor is something that is shapeless in its own concept, if it is going to offer itself to what is prior to it and to the best things. Soul is of this kind in relation to intellect and reason: it is shaped by them and led to a better form. In the intelligible world composition is of another kind, not like that of bodies. For rational principles are composite too, and by their activity they make the nature that is active towards form composite. And if it is active towards something else, and from something else, it is all the more so. The matter of things that come to be always takes one form after another; the matter of eternal things is always the same and has the same form. Perhaps the matter here is the reverse of the matter there. Here everything comes in turn, and only one thing at a time, so nothing stays, each thing pushing out the next; that is why it is never the same. There, it is all things at once, so it has nothing to change into: it has everything already. So the matter there is never shapeless; nor is the matter here, though each is formed in a different way. Whether it is eternal or has come to be will be clear once we have grasped what it is.
!flag through nature which has an energy directed to form | The Greek says the rational principles make composite "the nature that is active towards form"; Taylor's "through nature" makes nature the means.

@ch II.4.4 | Why there must be matter there
If there are many Forms, they have something in common and something of their own; what is their own is their shape, and what is shaped is matter. The world here imitates that world, and this one is made of matter, so that one has matter too. Take away from intellect its variety, shapes and thoughts, and what is left before them is shapeless and unbounded.

@p
Let us assume for now that the Forms exist, for it has been shown elsewhere, and let the argument go on. If, then, the Forms are many, there must be something common to them, and also something of each one's own, by which one differs from another. This something of its own, the difference that sets it apart, is its own shape. But if there is shape, there is also what is shaped, the thing the difference is about. So there is also matter, which receives the shape, and always something underlying. Again, if there is an intelligible world there, and this world is an imitation of that, and this one is composite and made of matter, there must be matter there too. Or how will you call it a world, an ordered whole, without looking at its form? And how will you look at form without taking hold of that on which the form is? For that world is wholly without parts in every way, and yet in some way it has parts. If the parts are torn away from one another, the cutting and the tearing are an affection of matter, for it is matter that has been cut. But if, being many, it is undivided, then the many, being in one, are in a matter, as its shapes. Think of this one, which is many, as varied and of many shapes. Then before it was varied, it was shapeless. For if in your mind you take away the variety, the shapes, the rational principles and the thoughts, what comes before them is shapeless and unbounded, and none of the things on it or in it.
!note "Shown elsewhere": in V.9, On Intellect, the Forms and Being, which Porphyry places later but Plotinus wrote earlier.
!flag Hence, if by intellect you take away its variety | The Greek "τῷ νῷ" can mean "in thought" (in your mind) or "from intellect"; Taylor takes it as "by intellect". The modern version reads it as "in your mind", the usual sense.

@ch II.4.5 | The depth of each thing; the matter there
Intellect divides each thing till it reaches what is simple; the depth of each is matter, and so all matter is dark. The matter there has a defined and thinking life; the matter here becomes a defined but dead thing. The matter there comes from Otherness, the first motion, which is unbounded until it turns to the One and is lit by it.

@p
Someone may say that because the matter there always has these things, and has them all together, the two are one and there is no matter there. But then there will be no matter of bodies here either: it is never without shape, but is always a whole body, though a composite. Intellect finds out that it is double, for intellect divides until it comes to something simple that cannot be broken down further. As long as it can, it goes on into the thing's depth. And the depth of each thing is matter. That is why all matter is dark: the light is the rational principle, and intellect is a rational principle. So intellect, seeing the rational principle on each thing, takes what is beneath to be dark, as being below the light. So an eye, which is like light, turned towards the light and the colours, which are lights, says that what is beneath the colours is dark and material, "hidden by the colours".
!flag But so far as it is able, it proceeds into the profundity of body. say that matter is essence | Taylor's chapter, as the scan gives it, leaves out a passage here: the Greek's "the depth of each thing is matter", the dark beneath the colours, and the two kinds of matter, before "those who say that matter is substance".

@p
@greek διάφορόν γε μὴν τὸ σκοτεινὸν
But the darkness in the intelligible world and the darkness in the sensible world are different. And the matter is different too, as much as the form laid on each is different. For the divine matter, when it receives what defines it, has a life that is defined and thinking. The other matter becomes something defined, but not living or thinking: a decorated corpse. And its shape is an image, so what underlies it is an image too. There the shape is true, and so is what underlies it. That is why those who say that matter is substance would have to be taken to speak rightly, if they were speaking of that matter. For what underlies there is substance; or rather, taken together with what is on it, and lit up as a whole, it is substance.

@p
@greek πότερα δὲ ἀίδιος ἡ νοητὴ
Whether the intelligible matter is eternal must be asked in the same way as one would ask it of the Ideas. They have come to be, in that they have a source; they have not come to be, in that their source is not in time: they come from another always, not as always coming to be, like the world here, but as always being, like the world there. For the Otherness there, which makes matter, is eternal. It is the source of matter, it and the first motion; and that is why motion too was called Otherness, because motion and Otherness sprang out together. The motion and the Otherness that come from the First are unbounded, and they need the First in order to be bounded. They are bounded when they turn back to it. Before that, the other is unbounded too, and not yet good, but unlit by the First. For if the light comes from it, then what receives the light, before receiving it, does not have true light; it has light as something other than itself, since the light comes from another. So much, then, about matter in the intelligible world, laid bare here more fully than was needed.

@ch II.4.6 | Why bodies need a substrate
The elements change into one another, so something must remain under the change: what perishes is never wholly destroyed, and nothing comes from what wholly is not. Perishing, induction and analysis all show that bodies are composite, of matter and form. The elements are not form alone, nor first matter, so they are made of both.

@p
About the receiver of bodies, let this be said. That there must be something underlying bodies, other than the bodies themselves, is shown by the change of the elements into one another. For what changes is not wholly destroyed; otherwise some substance would perish into what is not. Nor has what comes to be come into being out of what wholly is not. There is a change of form from another form, and what has received the form of the new thing and thrown off the other remains. This is shown by perishing in general too, for perishing belongs to what is composite; and if so, each thing is of matter and form. Induction bears witness to it, by showing that what perishes is composite; and so does analysis: for example, if the bowl is resolved into gold, and the gold into water, then the water too, when it perishes, calls for something analogous. The elements must be either form, or first matter, or made of matter and form. But they cannot be form: how could they have bulk and size without matter? Nor are they first matter, for they perish. So they are made of matter and form. And the form is in the quality and shape; the matter is in the substrate, which is unbounded because it is not form.
!flag as if, for instance, a pot should be resolved into gold ; 1 but gold into water | The Greek has a bowl (phialē); the gold resolved into water is an old view of metals, which Plotinus uses only as an example.

@ch II.4.7 | Against Empedocles, Anaxagoras, the infinite and atoms
Empedocles' elements perish, so they are not matter. Anaxagoras' mixture, which has everything already, does away with the intellect he brings in. The infinite cannot be matter, whether as a thing by itself or as an attribute of a body. Atoms do not exist, since every body can be divided, and nothing continuous or living could be made of them.

@p
Empedocles, who sets the elements in the place of matter, has their perishing as a witness against him. Anaxagoras makes the mixture matter, and says that it does not have a fitness for all things, but has all things already in act. So he does away with the intellect he brings in, since he does not make it the one that gives the shape and the form, nor make it prior to matter, but simultaneous with it. But to be simultaneous is impossible. For if the mixture shares in being, being comes first; and if both the mixture and being are, they will need a third thing besides them. If, then, the Maker must come first, why did the forms need to be in matter in tiny pieces, and then intellect, with endless toil, to separate them out, when it could have spread quality and shape over the whole of matter, since it has no quality? And how is "everything in everything" not impossible?

@p
@greek ὁ δὲ τὸ ἅπειρον ὑποθεὶς
As for the one who makes the infinite the substrate, let him say what this is. If it is infinite in the sense that it cannot be gone through, it is clear that there is no such thing among beings, neither as the infinite by itself, nor as belonging to another nature, as an attribute of some body. Not the infinite by itself, because a part of it would have to be infinite too; not as an attribute, because what it belongs to would not by itself be infinite, nor simple, nor matter. Nor will atoms hold the place of matter, since they do not exist at all: every body can be divided without end. Besides, the continuity and the fluidity of bodies could not exist, nor could anything exist without intellect and soul, which cannot be made of atoms. Nor can a nature other than atoms be made out of atoms; for no maker will make anything out of a matter that is not continuous. A thousand other things could be said against this view, and have been, so it is pointless to spend longer on it.
!note "The one who makes the infinite the substrate" is Anaximander; the atoms are those of Democritus and Epicurus.
!flag the demiurgus | Taylor keeps the Greek word, the "demiurge" or craftsman of the world; the modern version calls him the Maker.

@ch II.4.8 | Matter has no quality and no size
Matter is not a body, since a body has quality. Being the matter of all sensible things, it has none of their properties: no colour, heat, weight or shape, and no size either. It must be simple. The forming principle brings size with it, as it brings quality; for each kind the form fixes its size.

@p
What, then, is this matter, said to be one, continuous and without quality? That it is not a body, if it is without quality, is clear; otherwise it would have quality. We say it is the matter of all sensible things, not matter of some and form for others, as clay is matter for the potter but is not matter simply. Since we do not speak of it in that way, but of matter for all things, we should attach to it nothing of what is seen in sensible things. If so, besides the other qualities, such as colours, heat and cold, it has neither lightness nor weight, neither density nor rarity, and no shape either. Then it has no size: for to be size is one thing, and to be sized another; to be shape one thing, and to be shaped another. It must not be composite, but simple, one thing in its own nature, for in this way it is empty of all things.

It is Taylor's, from the later Platonists, not Plotinus.

@greek καὶ ὁ μορφὴν διδοὺς
And the one who gives it shape will give the shape as something other than matter, and size, and everything, as if bringing them from what is. Otherwise he would be a slave to its size and would make a thing not as big as he wants, but as big as matter wants; and to say that his will runs along with its size is a fiction. But if the maker comes before matter, matter will be in every way as the maker wants, easily led to everything, and so to size as well. If it had size, it would have to have shape too, so it would be even harder to work. So the form comes on to it, bringing everything with it; and every form has its size and its quantity, with the rational principle and under it. That is why in each kind the quantity is fixed together with the form: a man has one size, a bird another, and this kind of bird another again. It is no more remarkable to bring quantity to matter than to add quality to it; nor is quality a rational principle while quantity is not a form, since it is a measure and a number.

@ch II.4.9 | Quantity is a form
Not everything that exists has size: nothing incorporeal does, and matter is incorporeal. Quantity itself is not a quantity; what shares in it is. As what makes a thing white is not white, what makes a thing so big is not so big but the principle of size. It does not unroll matter into size; it gives it size it did not have.

@p
How, then, can one think of something that exists but has no size? Everything that is not the same as quantity is of this kind; for being and quantity are not the same, and there are many other things besides quantity. In general, every incorporeal nature must be set down as without quantity, and matter too is incorporeal. Even quantity itself is not a quantity; what shares in it is. So it is clear from this as well that quantity is a form. As a thing became white by the presence of whiteness, but what made the white colour in an animal, and the other varied colours, was not a varied colour but, if you like, a varied rational principle, so what makes a thing so big is not itself so big, but magnitude itself, or the size, or the rational principle. Does magnitude, then, come and unroll matter into size? Not at all; for matter was not rolled up into a small space. It gave it a size that it did not have before, as it gave it a quality that it did not have before.

@ch II.4.10 | How matter is thought: a bastard reasoning
How can we think what has no size and no quality? By a kind of unboundedness in the soul, as darkness is the eye's matter. The account of it is bounded, but the soul's grasp of it is not; so its image is "bastard", as Plato says. The soul thinks it dimly, by not thinking, and quickly puts form on it again, afraid of being outside the things that are.

@p
How, then, shall I think of what has no size in matter? And how will you think of something without any quality at all? What is the thought, and what is the mind's grasp of it? It is unboundedness. For if like is known by like, the unbounded is known by the unbounded. An account of the unbounded can be bounded; but the grasp of it is unbounded. If each thing is known by reason and thought, here reason says what it says about matter, but what would like to be thinking is not thinking, but rather a kind of not-thinking. So the image of it would be bastard and not legitimate, made up of something not true and of the other reason. Perhaps that is what Plato was looking at when he said matter is grasped "by a bastard reasoning".
!note "Grasped by a bastard reasoning" is from the Timaeus (52b), where Plato says it of the receptacle.
!flag and wishing to become intelligence, is not intelligence, but, as it were, a privation of intellect | The Greek says that what "would like to be" thinking is not thinking, but rather a kind of thoughtlessness (anoia); Taylor's words are near this, but make reason the one that wishes.

@p
@greek τίς οὖν ἡ ἀοριστία τῆς ψυχῆς
What, then, is the soul's unboundedness? Is it complete ignorance, an absence? Or is the unbounded found in something positive? As darkness is the eye's matter for every colour it cannot see, so the soul takes away whatever, in sensible things, is like light; and having nothing left that it can bound, it becomes like sight in the dark, at that moment the same, in a way, as what it as it were sees. Does it see, then? It sees as one sees shapelessness, colourlessness, lightlessness, and besides as one sees what has no size. If not, it will be giving it a form already. When the soul thinks nothing, is this not the same experience? No. When it thinks nothing, it says nothing, or rather it experiences nothing. But when it thinks matter, it has an experience like an imprint of the shapeless.

For when it thinks things that have shape and size, it thinks them as composites, as coloured and in general as having quality. It thinks the whole, both together. The thought, or the perception, of what is on the thing is clear; that of the substrate, the shapeless, is dim, for it is not a form. So what the soul grasps in the whole composite, with what is on it, when it has taken these apart and separated them, what reason leaves behind, this it thinks dimly as dim and darkly as dark, and it thinks it by not thinking. And since matter itself has not stayed shapeless, but is shaped in things, the soul too at once puts the form of things on it, pained by the unbounded, as if in fear of being outside the things that are, and not bearing to stand long in what is not.

@ch II.4.11 | Matter is not bulk, but has a phantom of bulk
Some say that if matter has no size it contributes nothing, and bodies need no matter. But a receiver need not have bulk: soul receives everything without it. Matter receives its forms in extension because it can receive extension. It has a phantom of bulk: great and small at once, running from one to the other, which is why some called it the void.

@p
Is something else still needed, then, to make up bodies, besides size and all the qualities? Yes: what will receive them all. Not bulk, then? If bulk, it is surely size; but if it has no size, it has nowhere to receive them. And having no size, what would it contribute, if it contributes neither to form and quality, nor to extension and size, which seems to come to bodies from matter, wherever it is? In general, someone may say, just as actions, makings, times and motions are among the things that exist, though they have no matter underlying them, so the first bodies need not have matter either. Each would be wholly what it is, the more varied for its mixture, made up of several forms. So this matter without size would be an empty name.

@p
@greek πρῶτον μὲν οὖν οὐκ ἀνάγκη
First, then, what receives a thing need not be a bulk, unless size is already present in it. Soul receives all things and holds them all together; if size were an attribute of it, it would hold each thing in size. Matter receives what it receives in extension because it is able to receive extension. So animals and plants, as they grow, have their quality drawn out beside the quantity, and if the quantity shrank the quality would shrink too. If someone demands, because in such things some size exists before, underlying what shapes it, that it should be so with matter as well, he is wrong. For there the matter is not matter simply, but the matter of this thing; matter simply must get size too from something else.

@p
@greek οὐ τοίνυν δεῖ ὄγκον εἶναι
So what is to receive the form need not be bulk. It becomes bulk at the same time as it receives the rest of quality, and it has a phantom of bulk, as a first fitness for it, but an empty bulk. That is why some have said that matter is the same as the void. I say "a phantom of bulk" because the soul, too, having nothing to bound when it meets matter, pours itself out into unboundedness, unable to draw a line round it or to come to a point; for then it would be bounding it already. So matter must not be called large by itself, nor small, but large-and-small. In this way it is bulk, and in this way without size: it is the matter of bulk, and shrinking from the large to the small and from the small to the large, it runs through, as it were, a bulk. Its unboundedness is this kind of bulk, the receiving of size in it; in imagination, it is as was said. For of the other things without size, each is a form, defined, so there is no thought of bulk in them at all. But matter, being unbounded and not yet standing still of itself, carried towards every form, this way and that, and easily led in every direction, becomes many by being led to everything and by coming to be; and in this way it has taken on the nature of bulk.
!note "The large-and-small" is Plato's name for matter in the unwritten teaching, as Aristotle reports it (Physics I.4, 187a; Metaphysics I.6, 987b).
!flag because, as being the first matter, it is an aptitude to the reception of it | The Greek says matter has a phantom of bulk "as a first fitness for it"; it does not say "as being the first matter".

@ch II.4.12 | Matter is needed, and known by reasoning, not sense
Forms of bodies are in sizes, but about something sized, other than size; otherwise there would be only rational principles in soul, and no bodies. Actions need no matter, but bodies are composite. So matter is not an empty name. It is grasped by no sense, but by an empty reasoning, a bastard one; and it has no bodiliness either.

@p
So sizes contribute to bodies, for the forms of bodies are in sizes. But they would not have come to be about size, but about what is sized. If they were about size and not about matter, they would likewise be without size and without real existence; or they would be only rational principles, and these are in soul; and there would be no bodies. So here the many must be about some one thing, and this is sized, and it is other than size. Even now, things that are mixed come together because they have matter, and need nothing else for them to be about, since each of the things mixed comes bringing its own matter. Still, they need one thing that will receive them: a vessel, or a place. But place comes after matter and bodies, so bodies would need matter first. Nor, because makings and actions are without matter, are bodies so too. Bodies are composite; actions are not. Matter, when people act, gives them what underlies, remaining in them, but does not give itself to the acting, nor do those who act look for this. One action does not change into another, so that there would be matter for actions too; the one who acts changes from one action to another, so that he is the matter for his actions.
!flag But forms are conversant with soul, and therefore are not bodies | The Greek says that rational principles alone are in soul, and so there would be no bodies; Taylor makes "forms" the subject.

@p
@greek ἔστι τοίνυν ἀναγκαῖον ἡ ὕλη
Matter, then, is necessary both for quality and for size, and so for bodies. It is not an empty name: it is something underlying, even if it is invisible and without size. Otherwise, by the same argument, we shall say there are no qualities and no size either; for each of these, taken by itself alone, might be said to be nothing. But if these exist, though each exists dimly, much more would matter exist, even if it is not clear, since it is not grasped by the senses. Not by the eyes, for it is colourless; not by hearing, for it is no sound; it has no flavours, so neither nose nor tongue grasp it. By touch, then? No, for it is not a body either; touch is of body, since it is of the dense or rare, the soft or hard, the wet or dry, and none of these is in matter. It is grasped by reasoning, not reasoning from intellect, but empty; that is why it is bastard, as was said. Nor is bodiliness in it. If bodiliness is a rational principle, it is other than matter, and matter is something else. But if it has already made something and has been as it were mixed with it, it would plainly be a body, and no longer only matter.
!flag for it has neither moisture, nor vapour | The Greek says only that matter has no flavours (chymoi), so that neither nose nor tongue grasps it; "vapour" is Taylor's.

@ch II.4.13 | Matter is not a quality, not even a privation
If the substrate were a common quality, how could a quality be a substrate? Someone says matter is qualified by having no quality, as a blind man is qualified by blindness. But privation is not a quality; it is an absence, like silence. Matter's own character is just what it is: to be other than everything else, or rather "others".

@p
If the substrate is a quality, common to each of the elements, first we must say what it is. Then, how will a quality be a substrate? How will a quality be seen in something without size, which has no matter and no size? Then, if the quality is defined, how is it matter? If it is something unbounded, it is not a quality but the substrate, the matter we are looking for. What, then, prevents it from being without quality, in that by its nature it shares in none of the others, and yet being qualified by this very fact, that it shares in none, having some character entirely its own and different from the others, a kind of privation of them? For one who is deprived is qualified: the blind man, for instance. If, then, there is a privation of these things about it, how is it not qualified? And if there is privation about it altogether, all the more, if privation is a quality.
!note Privation (sterēsis) is Aristotle's third principle of change, beside matter and form (Physics I.7).

@p
@greek ὁ δὲ ταῦτα λέγων
But the one who says this, what else does he do than make everything qualified and qualities? So quantity would be a quality, and substance too. If it is qualified, a quality is present to it. It is ridiculous to make what is other than the qualified, and not qualified, qualified. If it is qualified because it is other: if it is Otherness itself, even so it is not qualified, since quality itself is not qualified either; if it is only other, it is other not by itself but by Otherness, as it is the same by Sameness. Privation, then, is not a quality nor qualified, but an emptiness of quality or of something else, as silence is the absence of sound or of anything else. Privation is a taking away; the qualified is in something positive. The character of matter is not a shape, for its character is not to be qualified and not to have any form. It is absurd, then, to call it qualified because it is not qualified; it is like saying that, because it has no size, by that very fact it has size. Its character, then, is nothing other than what it is, and the character is not added to it. It lies rather in its relation to the others, in that it is other than them. The others are not only others, but each is something, as a form; matter could fittingly be called only other. Perhaps, rather, "others", so that you do not define it by the singular "other", but show its unboundedness by "others".
!flag For privation is a negation | The Greek word (arsis) means a taking away, a removal; Taylor's "negation" is near, but not the word.

@ch II.4.14 | Is matter privation?
Is matter privation, or is privation in matter? Some say they are one in substrate, two in account. Then each account must be given. If they are separate, they are two; if like the snub nose, or fire and heat, they are still not one. If privation is a not-being, still there are two accounts. But if to be matter is to be unbounded and without quality, how are there two?

@p
But we must ask this: is matter privation, or is privation about matter? Whoever says they are one in substrate but two in account ought to teach us the account to be given of each: an account of matter that will define it without attaching anything of privation, and an account of privation in the same way. For either neither is in the account of the other, or each in each, or only one in the other, whichever it is. If each is separate and neither calls for the other, they will be two, and matter will be other than privation, even if privation is an attribute of it. In the account of each, the other must not be seen even potentially. If they are like the snub nose and snubness, then too each is double, and they are two. If they are like fire and heat, heat being in fire but fire not taken in the heat, and matter is privation in the way fire is hot, privation will be as it were the form of matter, and the substrate something else, which must be the matter. So even in this way they are not one.
!note The snub nose and the fire and heat are Aristotle's examples (Metaphysics VII.5, 1030b).

@p
@greek ἆρα οὖν οὕτως ἓν τῷ ὑποκειμένῳ
Are they, then, one in substrate but two in account in this way: that privation does not signify that something is present, but that it is not, and privation is as it were a denial of the things that are, as if someone said "not-being"? For the denial adds nothing, but says that something is not. Is privation, then, "not-being" in this way? If it is not-being because it is not being, but some other being, there are still two accounts: one touches the substrate, the other shows privation's relation to the others. Or the account of matter is in relation to the others, and the account of the substrate is in relation to the others too; but the account of privation, if it shows matter's unboundedness, would perhaps touch matter itself. Still, in either way they are one in substrate, two in account. But if for matter to be unbounded, to be infinite and to be without quality are the same, how are there still two accounts?

@ch II.4.15 | Matter is the unbounded itself
The unbounded is not an attribute of matter: what orders is limit and principle, so what is ordered must be the unlimited, and an attribute must be a principle. So matter is the unlimited itself. In the intelligible world it is born of the One's infinite power; the matter here, an image fleeing from true being, is more truly unlimited.

@p
We must ask again whether the infinite and the unbounded belong to another nature as attributes, and how, and whether privation is an attribute. Numbers and rational principles are outside infinity, for they are bounds and orders, and other things are ordered by them. What orders these is not the ordered nor order; what is ordered is other than what orders, and what orders is limit, bound and rational principle. So what is ordered and bounded must be the infinite. And matter is ordered, and so are the things that are not matter, by sharing in it or having the character of matter. So matter must be the infinite, but not infinite as an attribute, by the infinite belonging to it. First, what belongs to something must be a rational principle, and the infinite is not a rational principle. Then, to what will the infinite belong? To limit and the limited. But matter is not limited, nor limit. And the infinite, coming to the limited, would destroy its nature. So the infinite is not an attribute of matter: matter is the infinite.
!flag For in the first place, that which happens to any thing ought to be formative ; but the infinite is not formative | The Greek says that what belongs to a thing must be a rational principle (logos), and the infinite is not one; "formative" is Taylor's gloss.

@p
@greek ἐπεὶ καὶ ἐν τοῖς νοητοῖς
For in the intelligible world too matter is the infinite. It would be born from the infinity of the One, or its power, or its everlastingness: not that there is infinity in the One, but the One makes it. How, then, is it there and here? The infinite is double too. How do they differ? As original and image. Is this one, then, less infinite? No, more. The more an image has fled from true being, the more infinite it is; for infinity is greater in what is less bounded, since what is less in the good is more in the bad. So the infinite there, being more, is infinite as an image; the infinite here, being less, the more it has fled from true being and sunk into the nature of an image, is more truly infinite.
!flag Hence the infinite which is in intelligible^, in consequence of having more of being, is but as an image [with respect to the infinity of matter.] | Taylor's words in square brackets are his own; and his chapter, as the scan gives it, has another of his footnotes (from Proclus on the Timaeus) printed into the text after this.

@p
@greek τὸ αὐτὸ οὖν τὸ ἄπειρον
Is the infinite, then, the same as being infinite? Where there is a rational principle and matter, the two are different; where there is matter alone, they must be called the same, or, better, there is here no "being infinite" at all. For that would be a rational principle, and there is none in the infinite, if it is to be infinite. So matter must be called infinite of itself, by its opposition to the rational principle. As the rational principle is a rational principle without being anything else, so matter, opposed to it by its infinity, must be called infinite without being anything else.

@ch II.4.16 | Matter is poverty, and so evil
Matter is not Otherness itself, but the part of Otherness opposed to the true beings, and so it is not-being and privation. Form coming to it does not destroy it, but brings it to what it is, as a seed sown or a female made pregnant. It is evil, not because it wants the good, but because it is poverty itself: of wisdom, virtue, beauty and form.

@p
Is matter, then, the same as Otherness? No, but as a part of Otherness, the part opposed to the things that properly are, which are rational principles. That is why not-being too is in this way a something, and the same as privation, if privation is the opposite of the things that exist in a rational principle. Will privation not be destroyed, then, when what it is the privation of comes to it? Not at all. The receiver of a state is not a state but a privation; and the receiver of limit is not the limited nor the limit, but the infinite, in so far as it is infinite. How, then, will limit, when it comes, not destroy its nature, especially as it is not infinite as an attribute? If it were infinite in quantity, limit would do away with it. But as it is, it does not; on the contrary, it keeps it in being. For it brings what it is by nature to act and fulfilment, as with what is unsown when it is sown; and when the female conceives from the male, the female is not destroyed but becomes more female: that is, it becomes more what it is.
!flag Perhaps it is not the same with difference simply considered,1 but with a part of difference | Taylor's "simply considered" is his own; the Greek says only "not the same, but a part of Otherness".

@p
@greek ἆῤ οὖν καὶ κακὸν ἡ ὕλη
Is matter, then, evil, even when it shares in the good? Yes, because it needed it: it did not have it. What lacks something but has something else might perhaps be between good and evil, if it is evenly balanced between the two. But what has nothing, being in poverty, or rather being poverty, must be evil. For this is not poverty of wealth or of strength, but poverty of wisdom, poverty of virtue, of beauty, of shape, of form, of quality. How, then, is it not ugly? How is it not wholly shameful? How is it not wholly evil? The matter there is being, for what is before it is beyond being. Here, what is before it is being. So matter itself is not-being, being other than being, in addition to the beauty of being.
!note "Beyond being" is from the Republic (509b), where Plato says it of the Good.
!flag but it is the want of wisdom, and the want of virtue, of beauty, strength, morphe, form, and quality | Taylor adds "strength" a second time to the list; the Greek has wisdom, virtue, beauty, shape, form and quality.

@treatise II.9 | Against the Gnostics
Against those who say the maker of the world is evil and the world is bad: the Gnostics among Plotinus' own hearers. There are three principles and no more: the Good, intellect and soul. The world was not made by a soul that fell, nor by reasoning, but by soul's contemplation of what is above it; it is the best possible image of the intelligible world, and the stars have souls better than ours. The Gnostics borrow from Plato and spoil what they borrow, multiply beings, despise the gods, claim to be the only sons of God, offer magic for cures, and teach no virtue. The good person lives here calmly, as in a fine house built by another.

@ch II.9.1 | Three principles, and no more
The Good is simple and first, the same as the One, with nothing above it. After it comes intellect, then soul: no fewer, since they differ, and no more. There is no intellect at rest beside one in motion, nor one that thinks beside one that thinks that it thinks, nor a rational principle between intellect and soul.

@p
Since we have seen that the nature of the Good is simple and first (for everything that is not first is not simple), and has nothing in itself, but is one thing, and that the nature of what is called the One is the same (for it is not something else and then one, nor this something else and then good), when we say "the One" and when we say "the Good" we must think it is the same nature, and call it one, not predicating anything of it, but making it plain to ourselves as far as we can. We call it first because it is the simplest; self-sufficient, because it is not made of many things, for then it would hang on the things it is made of; not in another, because everything in another also comes from another. If, then, it is not from another, nor in another, nor any kind of composite, there can be nothing above it. So we must not go to other principles, but put this first, then intellect after it, the first thinker, then soul after intellect. This is the order according to nature: to set neither more than these in the intelligible world, nor fewer. If fewer, they will say that soul and intellect are the same, or intellect and the first; but it has been shown in many places that they are different from one another.

@p
@greek λοιπὸν δὴ ἐπισκέψασθαι
It remains to look now at whether there are more than these three. What natures could there be besides them? No one could find a principle simpler than, or higher than, the principle of all things we have described. They will not say that there is one in potency and another in act: it is absurd, among things that are in act and without matter, to divide into potency and act and make more natures. Nor among the things after these: we must not imagine one intellect at a kind of rest and another as it were moving. What would the rest of intellect be, and what its motion and going forth? What would the idleness of one be, and what the work of the other? Intellect is as it is, always the same, set in a stable activity. Motion towards it and around it is already the work of soul; and a rational principle goes from it to soul and makes soul intelligent, without making some other nature between intellect and soul.
!flag For they [the Gnostics] will not say 1 that there is one principle in capacity, but another in energy | The Gnostics are not named in the Greek here; Taylor's square brackets say whom Plotinus means. "Capacity" and "energy" are Taylor's words for potency and act (dynamis, energeia).

@p
@greek οὐ μὴν οὐδὲ διὰ τοῦτο πλείους νοῦς
Nor should we make several intellects because one thinks and another thinks that it thinks. Even if, among us, thinking is one thing and thinking that one thinks is another, still it is a single grasp, not unaware of its own activities. It would be absurd to suppose otherwise of the true intellect: the one that thinks that it thinks will be the very same as the one that was thinking. Otherwise one will only think, and the other will think that it thinks, but that it is another who thinks, not itself. If they say the difference is only in our way of conceiving it, first they will give up their many realities. Then we must ask whether our conception even has room to take an intellect that only thinks and is not aware that it thinks. If that happened to us, who always watch over our impulses and our thoughts, we would be charged with folly, if we were even moderately serious people.
!flag if we are moderately worthy, it becomes the cause to us of folly | The Greek says that if this happened to us, who are at least moderately serious people, we would be charged with folly; Taylor's "it becomes the cause to us of folly" loses the charge.

@p
@greek ὅταν δὲ δὴ ὁ νοῦς ὁ ἀληθινὸς
But when the true intellect thinks itself in its thoughts, and the intelligible, what it thinks, is not outside it but it is itself what it thinks, then necessarily in thinking it has itself and sees itself; and seeing itself it sees itself not unthinking, but thinking. So in thinking first it would have thinking that it thinks too, as one thing; and there it is not double even in conception. And if it is always thinking, which it is, what room is there for a conception that separates thinking from thinking that one thinks? If someone brought in a third conception besides the second, the one that thinks that it thinks that it thinks, the absurdity is still plainer. And why not go on so to infinity? As for the one who makes a rational principle come from intellect, and then from it another in soul, so that this is between soul and intellect, he will deprive soul of thinking, if it gets its rational principle not from intellect but from some other between. It will have an image of a rational principle, not a rational principle; and it will not know intellect at all, nor think at all.

@ch II.9.2 | One intellect; and our soul in three parts
There is one intellect, always the same, imitating its father as far as it can. Part of our soul is always with the things there, part with the things here, and part between; the lower part can drag down the middle, but not the whole. The soul of the universe never fell: it orders the world without effort, by looking at what is above it.

@p
So we must set neither more than these, nor superfluous conceptions among them, which they do not admit, but one intellect, the same, always in the same state, unswerving everywhere, imitating its father as far as it can. And of our soul, one part is always with the things there, one part with the things here, and one in the middle of these. Being one nature in several powers, sometimes the whole of it is carried along with the best of itself and of being; sometimes its worse part is dragged down and drags the middle part with it; for it was not lawful for it to drag the whole down. This happens to it because it did not stay in the most beautiful, where the soul that stayed, which is not a part, and of which we are no longer a part, gave the whole body of the world to have from it as much as it can have. This soul stays untroubled, governing not by reasoning, nor setting anything right, but ordering the world with a wonderful power by its contemplation of what comes before it. The more it is turned to that, the more beautiful and powerful it is; and having this from there, it gives it to what comes after it, and as it lights, it is always lit.
!flag For the more it looks to itself | The Greek says "the more it is towards that", what comes before it (pros autēi, read by the editors as towards the intelligible); Taylor's "looks to itself" says the opposite.

@ch II.9.3 | Everything gives of itself: the world has no beginning
Lit always, soul always gives light to what follows, as a fire warms what is round it. Every principle must give of itself to another, or the Good would not be good, nor intellect intellect. So all things follow one another always: what is called "made" was always being made, and will not perish. Matter too is always lit.

@p
So it is always lit and has the light without break, and it gives it to what comes next; and these are always held together and watered by this light, and enjoy life as far as they can. It is as if a fire lay somewhere in the middle and warmed those who could be warmed. And yet fire is limited. But when powers that are not measured have not been removed from the things that are, how could they exist and nothing share in them? Each must give of its own to another as well, or the Good will not be good, or intellect not intellect, or soul not this, unless something lives secondarily after what lives first, as long as what is first exists. So all things must follow one another, and always; and the others are "made" in the sense that they come from others. So what are called made did not come to be at a time, but were always coming to be and will be; nor will they perish, except those things that have something to dissolve into. What has nothing to dissolve into will not perish either.
!flag and should be generated by other things, because they depend on others for their subsistence | The Greek says only that the others are "made" (genēta) in that they come from others; Taylor's "generated" suggests a beginning in time, which Plotinus here denies.

@p
@greek εἰ δέ τις εἰς ὕλην
If someone says things dissolve into matter, why not matter as well? If he says matter too dissolves, what necessity was there, we shall ask, for it to come to be? If they say it had to follow, it has to now as well. And if matter is to be left alone, the divine things will not be everywhere, but in some fenced-off place, walled off, as it were. If that is impossible, matter will be lit.

@ch II.9.4 | The world-soul did not fall
If they say the soul made the world when it lost its wings, the soul of the universe suffers no such thing. If it erred, when, and why then? It made the world not by sinking, but by not sinking; a forgetful soul could not make, and one that remembers would go back. Nor is the world badly made because it has much that is hard; it is the best image of the intelligible that there could be.

@p
If they say that the soul made the world when it had, as it were, shed its wings, the soul of the universe does not suffer this. If they say it erred, let them say what caused the error. And when did it err? If from eternity, by their own account it remains in error. If it began at a time, why not before? We say that the soul that makes did not sink, but rather did not sink. If it sank, it was plainly because it had forgotten the things there. And if it forgot them, how does it make the world? From what does it make, if not from what it saw there? But if it makes remembering them, it did not sink at all; for even if it has them dimly, it would not sink further, so as not to see them dimly. If it had any memory at all, why would it not want to go back up? What would it have reckoned it would gain by making a world? To be honoured, they say, which is absurd, a notion carried over from the makers of statues here.
!note The shedding of the soul's wings is from the Phaedrus (246c, 248c).
!flag For it does not possess nor see them obscurely, if it is without this tendency | The Greek says that even if the soul holds the things there dimly, it would not then sink further and see them more dimly still; Taylor's sentence says something else.

@p
@greek ἐπεὶ καὶ εἰ διανοίᾳ
Again, if it made by reasoning, and making was not in its nature, and its power was not a making power, how would it have made this world? And when will it destroy it? If it repented, what is it waiting for? If not yet, it would not repent now, being used to it and grown fonder of it with time. If it is waiting for the individual souls, they ought by now no longer to come into birth, having long ago had a taste of the evils here in an earlier birth; so they would have stopped coming by now. Nor must we grant that the world has been badly made because there are many unpleasant things in it. That would be to claim too great a dignity for it, if they think it should be the same as the intelligible world, and not an image of it. What other image of it, more beautiful, could there have been? What other fire would be a better image of the fire there than the fire here? What other earth than this, after the earth there? What sphere more exact or more orderly in its motion, after the encircling of the intelligible world in itself? What other sun after that one, before this sun we see?
!flag For this arises from forming too exalted an opinion of this sensible world | Taylor's words are close; the Greek says that those who find fault claim too high a worth for the world, asking it to be the intelligible world and not its image.

@ch II.9.5 | They rank themselves above the stars
It is unreasonable for people who have bodies and passions to claim to touch the intelligible, and deny that the sun's soul is better than theirs, or that the heavens share in immortality. Their soul made of the elements cannot live. They hate the world and dream of a "new earth" as its pattern; but where would this pattern come from?

@p
It is unreasonable that they, who have a body such as men have, and desires, pains and angers, should not despise the power that is in them, and yet say they may touch the intelligible; but that there is not, in the sun, a power more free from passion than this, more ordered and less subject to change, nor a wisdom better than ours, who were born only lately and are kept back by so many deceptions from coming to the truth. Nor is it reasonable to call their own soul immortal and divine, and that of the worst of men, but to say that the whole heaven and the stars there have no share in the immortal soul, though they are made of far finer and purer things. They see the order there, the good shape and the good arrangement, and they themselves above all find fault with the disorder here round the earth: as if the immortal soul had chosen the worse place on purpose, and left the better to the mortal soul.

@p
@greek ἄλογος δὲ καὶ ἡ παρεισαγωγὴ
Unreasonable too is their bringing in of this other soul, which they put together from the elements. How could a compound of the elements have any life? Their mixture makes hot or cold or a blend, or dry or wet or a mixture of these. And how is soul the bond holding the four together, if it came after them and from them? And when they add to it perception and deliberation and a thousand other things, what can one say? They do not honour this making of the world, nor this earth, but say that a new earth has been made for them, to which they will go from here; and that this is the rational principle of the world. And yet why should they need to come to be in the pattern of a world they hate? And where does this pattern come from? By their account it comes from the maker of the pattern when he had already sunk towards the things here. If, then, the maker himself took great care to make another world after the intelligible world that he has (and why should he?), he made it either before this world or after. If before, why? So that souls might be on their guard. Then how is it that they were not, so that it was made in vain? If after, taking it from the world, stripping the form from matter, then the experience of the souls who had tried the world was enough to put them on guard. And if they claim to take the form of the world into souls, what is new in the doctrine?
!note The "new earth" is the Gnostics' own, from Revelation 21:1; the "other soul" made of the elements is their lower soul.
!flag was it in order that souls might be saved | The Greek verb (phylaxōntai) means that souls might be on their guard, warned by the pattern; Taylor's "saved" gives it a Christian sense the Greek does not have.

@ch II.9.6 | What is new in them is not true
Their "sojourns", "antitypes" and "repentances" are empty words to found a sect. What is true in them they took from Plato; what is new is false. They misread the Timaeus and multiply intelligible beings. They should take from the ancients what is well said, argue fairly where they differ, and not abuse the Greeks to win a name.

@p
What should one say of the other realities they bring in: "sojourns", "antitypes" and "repentances"? If they say these are affections of the soul, when it repents, and "antitypes" when it contemplates images of the things that are, as it were, and not yet the things themselves, these are the words of people talking emptily to found a sect of their own. Because they do not take hold of the old Greek teaching, they contrive these things; while the Greeks knew clearly, and said without bombast, that there are ascents out of the cave, going on little by little to a truer and truer sight. In short, some of their ideas are taken from Plato; and the others, all the novelties they bring in to set up a philosophy of their own, have been found outside the truth. The judgments, the rivers in Hades and the passings into other bodies come from there.

@ch II.9.7 | The world-soul is not bound as we are
The world never began and will not end. Our soul's union with the body is not best for it; but to judge the soul of the universe by ours is like blaming a whole city for its potters. We are bound by the body; the world-soul binds and is not bound, and orders its body without effort. What perishes is like a tortoise caught in a great dance.

@p
That this world neither began nor will cease, but always is, as long as those things are, has been said. That our soul's fellowship with the body is not the better thing for the soul was said before them. But to judge the soul of the universe by ours is like taking the tribe of potters or smiths in a well-governed city and blaming the whole city. We must grasp the differences in the way the soul of the whole governs: it is not in the same manner, nor bound. Besides the other differences, a thousand of which have been told elsewhere, we should have kept this in mind: that we are bound by the body, once the bond has been made. For the nature of the body, already bound in the whole soul, binds together whatever it embraces. But the soul of the universe would not be bound by what it binds; for it rules. So it is free from affection by them, while we are not their masters. As much of it as is turned to the divine above remains unmixed and unhindered; as much of it as gives life to the body takes nothing from it in return.
!note "Said before them": the soul's fellowship with the body is not better for it, in the Phaedo (66b-67a) and elsewhere.

@greek ὅλως γὰρ τὸ μὲν ἄλλου πάθημα
For in general what is in another necessarily receives that other's affection, but does not in turn give its own to the other, which has a life of its own. It is as if something were grafted on to another: when the stock suffers, the graft suffers with it; but if the graft withers, it leaves the stock to keep its own life. When the fire in you goes out, the whole fire does not go out; and even if the whole fire perished, the soul there would suffer nothing, only the structure of the body. And if there could be a world made of the other elements, the soul there would not care. For the world is not held together like each living thing. There, the soul runs over it, as it were, and bids it stay; here, the parts, as if trying to slip away into their own order, are bound by a second bond. There they have nowhere to flee to. So the soul need not hold them in from inside, nor press them in from outside; each stays where its nature wished from the beginning. @ch II.9.8 | The world is a clear image of the intelligible gods
To ask why soul made the world is to ask why soul exists: it assumes a beginning to what is eternal. The world shows the greatness of the intelligible, a clear and beautiful statue of the intelligible gods; it had to exist, since the intelligible acts both in itself and on another. The stars are gods with virtue; and if souls came here willingly, why blame the place?

@p
To ask why soul made a world is the same as to ask why soul exists and why the Maker made. First, this is the question of people who take the everlasting to have a beginning. Next, they think he became the cause of the making by turning from something to something and changing. So they must be taught, if they would bear it kindly, what the nature of these things is, so that they stop abusing what deserves honour, as they do so readily, where great reverence would be fitting. For one could not rightly find fault with the governing of the universe. First, it shows the greatness of the intelligible nature. If it has come into life in such a way that its life is not jumbled, like that of the smallest things in it, which its great life brings forth night and day, but is continuous, clear, great, and everywhere a life showing a wisdom beyond reckoning, how could one not call it a clear and beautiful statue of the intelligible gods? If, imitating, it is not that, this itself is natural: otherwise it would no longer be imitating. To say it imitates badly is false: nothing has been left out of what a beautiful image in nature could have.

@p
@greek ἀναγκαῖον μὲν γὰρ ἦν εἶναι
It was necessary for the imitation to exist, and not from reasoning and contrivance. The intelligible could not be the last thing: its activity had to be double, one in itself and one towards another. So there had to be something after it; only that which is the weakest of all has nothing further below it. A wonderful power runs there, and so it worked. If there were another world better than this, which is it? But if there must be one, and there is no other, this is the one that keeps the likeness of that world. The whole earth is full of living things of every kind, and immortal ones, and everything up to the heaven is full of them. And the stars, those in the lower spheres and those in the highest, why are they not gods, moving in order and going round in their good arrangement? Why should they not have virtue? What prevents them from gaining it? The things that make people here bad are not there, nor the badness of the body, troubled and troubling. Why should they not understand, always at leisure, and take into their intellect God and the other intelligible gods? Will our wisdom be better than that of those there? Who, if he had not gone out of his mind, would bear this?

@ch II.9.9 | Wealth, poverty and wrongdoing; the many gods
The good man does not seek equality in wealth or power. Wrongdoing comes from souls like children, not from intellect; and there are judgments and punishments here, and gods who watch. We should try to be as good as we can, but not think ourselves the only good ones: there are good daemons and gods, and the world-soul. God's greatness is shown in the many gods he makes. The Gnostics flatter fools: "you are a son of God".

@p
If someone finds fault with wealth and poverty and the lack of equality in all such things, first, he does not know that the good man does not look for equality in these, nor thinks that those who have much have more, nor rulers more than private people, but leaves this kind of eagerness to others. He has learned that life here is double, one life for the good, another for most people. For the good it leads to the summit, upward. For those who are more merely human it is double again: one kind remembers virtue and shares in some good; the other is the base crowd, like workmen making necessities for the better sort. If someone kills, or is beaten by pleasures through weakness, what is surprising in there being faults, not in intellect, but in souls like boys who have not grown up? And if it is a training ground of winners and losers, how is it not well so? If someone wrongs you, what harm is it to the immortal? And if he kills you, you have what you want. If you already find fault with being a citizen, you are not compelled to be one. It is agreed that there are judgments and punishments here. How, then, can one rightly find fault with a city that gives each his due, where virtue is honoured and vice has its fitting dishonour, and where not only statues of the gods are, but the gods themselves look on from above? They, as is said, will easily escape blame from men, leading all things in order from beginning to end, giving each his fitting portion, in the changes of lives, following what was done before. Whoever does not know this is one of the rasher men, boorish about divine things.

!flag If, however, you still blame the administration of things, there is no necessity for you to continue any longer in life | The Greek says "if you find fault with being a citizen, you are not compelled to be one": not "to continue in life".

@ch II.9.10 | Why Plotinus writes; the fall of Wisdom
Much more could be examined, but Plotinus writes for his own pupils, out of regard for friends still held by these views, not to persuade the Gnostics. He ends with the most absurd thing: their story that soul and a certain "Wisdom" sank down and lit up the darkness, and that from images of images came the maker of this world.

@p
There is much else, or rather everything, that one could examine, and have plenty to say, showing how each point stands. But a certain reticence holds us back with regard to some of our friends, who came upon this teaching before they became our friends, and, I do not know how, still keep to it. Yet they themselves do not hesitate to say what they say, wanting it to seem convincingly true, or perhaps believing that it really is so. But we have said these things speaking to our own pupils, not to them (for there would be no gain in trying to persuade them), so that our pupils should not be troubled by people who bring no proofs (how could they?) but only self-will. There is another way in which one might write in defence against those who dare to disparage what the ancient and divine men said well and in keeping with the truth.

!flag and not to the many who are auditors of these men | The Greek says "speaking to our own pupils, not to them", the Gnostics; Taylor's "the many who are auditors of these men" is not there.

@ch II.9.11 | The image that made the world
If the soul did not come down but only lit the darkness, how did it sink? Why did it alone shine, and not the higher powers? Why did it wait for images to be born? An image of soul would need no matter. Is the image a substance or a "thought"? Either way, their story of a maker made of images falls apart.

@p
First, then, if the soul did not come down, but lit up the darkness, how could it rightly be said to have sunk? If something flowed from it like light, it is not fitting to say at once that it has sunk; unless the darkness lay somewhere below, and the soul went to it in place, and when it came near, lit it up. But if it lit it while staying by itself, doing nothing towards it, why did it alone light it up, and not the beings more powerful than it? If it was able to light it because it formed a reasoned plan of a world, why did it not make the world at the same time as lighting it, instead of waiting for the images to be born? Then too the plan of the world, the "foreign earth" as they call it, made by the greater powers, as they say themselves, did not bring its makers down to sinking. Then, how does matter, when lit, make images of soul, and not the nature of bodies? An image of soul would need no darkness or matter: once it came to be, if it does, it would follow its maker and be joined to it.

@p
@greek ἔπειτα πότερον οὐσία τοῦτο
Next, is this image a substance, or, as they say, a "thought"? If a substance, what is its difference from what it comes from? If it is another kind of soul, if that one is rational, perhaps this one is the soul of growth and generation. But if so, how could it still make the world to be honoured, and how could it make through arrogance and daring? In short, making through imagination, and still more through reasoning, is done away with. And why did it need to make the maker out of matter and an image? If it is a "thought", first they must show where the name comes from. Then, how does it exist, unless they give the thought the power to make? And how is a fiction a making? They say this was made first and that after, but they speak as if they could say what they please. Why fire first?

@ch II.9.12 | How could an image remember, or plan?
Their image, just born, could not remember what it never saw; yet they, true souls, scarcely recollect. Nature does not make fire first and then the rest, but outlines the whole animal at once. Foreseeing the heavens and the zodiac is no work of an image. If the lighting of the darkness was natural, it was always; if not, evil was there before the world, and the cause goes back to the first.

@p
And how does it set to work as soon as it is born? By memory of what it saw? But it did not exist at all, so that it might have seen, neither it nor the mother they give it. Then is it not surprising that they, who came here not as images of souls but as true souls, scarcely and with difficulty, one or two of them, are moved out of the world, and coming to recollection scarcely call back what they once saw; while this image, even if dimly, as they say, but just after it was born, thought of those things, or its mother did, a material image; and not only thought of them and took the idea of a world from that world, but learned what it should be made of? Where did it get the idea of making fire first, thinking this must come first? Why not something else? If it could make fire by thinking of fire, why, thinking of a world (for it must have thought of the whole first), did it not make a world all at once? For those things too were contained in the thought.

@ch II.9.13 | Do not fear the spheres
Whoever blames the world does not know the order of first, second and third. The spheres are not terrors, as the Gnostics' tragedies make them, but bring good things; their bodies are fine and their souls finer. What they do are signs of things to come. Not all can be good; what is less good is not therefore evil, or there would be evil in the intelligible world too.

@p
So whoever blames the nature of the world does not know what he is doing, nor where his boldness is taking him. This is because they do not know the order of things in succession, of first, second and third, and so on down to the last; and that one must not abuse what is worse than the first, but give way gently to the nature of all things, and oneself run towards the first, putting an end to the tragic tale of the terrors they think are in the spheres of the world, which, in fact, "make all things gentle" for them. What do these have that is fearful, to frighten people without experience of argument, who have never heard a cultivated and harmonious knowledge? Even if their bodies are of fire, one must not fear them, since they are in proportion to the universe and to the earth; one should look to their souls, by which these people surely think themselves worthy of honour too. And yet their bodies too, which excel in size and beauty, act with and work together with the things that come about by nature, which could never fail to come about as long as the first things exist; and they fill out the universe and are great parts of it. If men are something honourable beside the other animals, much more are these, which are in the universe not to be tyrants but to give it order and arrangement.

What are said to come from them should be thought of as signs of what will be. @ch II.9.14 | Magic, and diseases as daemons
They make the higher beings open to spells, as if words and hissings could charm what is incorporeal. They say diseases are daemons they can drive out; but diseases have bodily causes, and are cured by purging, bleeding and fasting. Our philosophy, by contrast, shows simplicity of character and pure thinking, with dignity, not arrogance.

@p
Above all, they themselves make the things there not pure in another way too. @ch II.9.15 | They teach no virtue
Of the two schools of the end of life, pleasure and the beautiful, the Gnostics are worse than Epicurus: he removed providence, but they blame its lord, mock the laws and self-control, and leave only pleasure. @treatise III.6 | On the impassibility of things without body
How can the soul, which has no body, be free from affection, when it seems to feel pleasure and pain, fear and desire, and to change with virtue and vice? Plotinus answers that perception, memory and virtue are activities, not imprints; the feelings belong to the body or to the living compound, with soul as their cause; and purification is the soul's waking from images. Then matter: it too is untouched. It is not being but a phantom, a mirror in which images come and go and leave no trace, "the receptacle and nurse" of becoming, which takes on nothing and keeps nothing.

@ch III.6.1 | Can the soul be affected?
Perceptions are not affections but activities and judgments about them. But how is the soul unchanged, when vice, false opinions, pleasures, pains and desires seem to arise in it? If soul has no size and is to be imperishable, we must not give it such affections; we must think of them as "affections without affection", taken over from bodies by analogy.

@p
We say that perceptions are not affections, but activities and judgments about affections. The affections happen in something else, in the body, say, of a certain kind; the judgment happens in the soul, and it is not an affection, for then another judgment would be needed, and we would go up and up without end. Even so we would still have a difficulty here: whether judgment, as judgment, has nothing of what it judges. If it had an imprint, it has been affected. We might also speak of the so-called imprints, and show that the way they come about is quite different from what is supposed, the same as with thoughts, which are activities too, able to know without being affected. In short, our argument and our aim is not to subject the soul to changes and alterations of the kind that bodies undergo when they are heated or chilled. We ought also to look at what is called its affective part, and ask whether we shall grant that this too is unchanging, or allow it alone to be affected. But that comes later; first we must look into the difficulties about the earlier ones.

@p
@greek πῶς γὰρ ἄτρεπτον
How is what comes before the affective part, and before perception, and in general any part of soul, unchanging, when vice comes to be in it, and false opinions, and ignorance? When it feels at home with things and estranged from them, takes pleasure and pain, is angry, envies, is jealous, desires, and in short is never at rest, but is moved and changed by each thing that comes upon it? If the soul is a body and has size, it is not easy, or rather it is wholly impossible, to show it free from affection and unchanging in any of the things said to happen to it. But if it is a substance without size, and imperishability must belong to it, we must take care not to give it such affections, lest we find we have made it perishable without noticing. And if its substance is a number, or a rational principle, as we say, how could an affection come to be in a number or a rational principle? We must rather think that what come upon it are "unreasoning reasons" and "affections without affection"; these words, carried over from bodies, must each be taken in the opposite sense, and by analogy: it has them without having them, and is affected without being affected. And we must look at how such things happen.

@ch III.6.2 | Virtue and vice are not alterations
Virtue is a harmony of the soul's parts, vice a discord; but each part has its own virtue first. Ignorance is an absence; false opinion is like a false sight. Reason "hears" intellect as sight sees: the activity is no alteration. Memory too is a power woken, not a store of imprints. Cowardice, courage and licence are ways each part acts or fails to act, with no change in its substance.

@p
First we must speak of vice and virtue, and say what happens when vice is said to be present. For we say we must take away something, as if there were some evil in the soul, and put in virtue, and give it order, and make beauty in it in place of its former ugliness. If we say that virtue is a harmony and vice a lack of harmony, we would be saying what the ancients thought, and the argument would make no small gain towards what we are looking for. If virtue is the parts of the soul attuned to one another according to nature, and vice their not being attuned, nothing would come in from outside, but each part would come into the harmony as it is, and would not come into it in the discord while being of that kind. So it is with dancers who dance and sing together, even if they are not the same; and when one sings alone and the others do not, and when each sings by himself. For they must not only sing together, but each must sing his own part well, with a musical skill of his own. So in the soul too there is a harmony when each part does what belongs to it. But before this harmony there must be another virtue of each part, and a vice of each before their discord with one another.

@ch III.6.3 | Feelings belong to the body; their source is in soul
We cannot deny that pleasure, pain, fear and desire are real changes; but we must ask what changes. To say the soul blushes or turns pale would be absurd: the shame or fear begins in soul, the blood changes in the body. Soul's movements come from it, as life does, without alteration; memories and images are not stamped in it like wax.

@p
How about feeling at home and estranged? And how are pains, angers and pleasures, desires and fears not changes and affections present and moving in the soul? About these we must distinguish as follows. To say that alterations and strong perceptions of them do not happen would be to contradict what is plain. But granting them, we must ask what it is that changes. For we are in danger of supposing, when we say these things of the soul, something like saying the soul blushes, or turns pale, not reckoning that these affections come through soul but happen in the rest of the composite. Shame is in the soul when an opinion of something shameful arises in it; the body, which the soul as it were holds (not to be misled by words), being under the soul and not like something soulless, was changed in the blood, which is easily moved. As for what is called fear, its source is in the soul; the pallor is the blood withdrawing inward. Of pleasure, the spreading out comes to perception in the body; what is in the soul is no longer an affection. So with pain.
!flag the body, (that we may not err in our conceptions) being as it were contained in the soul | Taylor's words are near the Greek; the warning "not to be misled by words" belongs to the word "holds", that the soul "holds" the body.

@greek ἐπεὶ καὶ τὸ τῆς ἐπιθυμίας
Again, desire: its source is in the soul and is hidden; what goes out from there, perception knows. For when we say the soul is moved in desires, reasonings and opinions, we do not mean that it does these things while being shaken, but that the movements come from it. When we call life a movement, we do not mean an alteration: the activity of each part according to nature is a life that does not leave itself. The heads of the matter are enough. If we grant that activities, lives and longings are not alterations, nor memories imprints sealed in, nor imaginings like stamps in wax, we must grant that in all that are called affections and movements the soul stays the same in its substrate and substance; and that virtue and vice do not come to be in it as black and white or hot and cold do in a body, but in the way that has been said, for both, and in general for all opposites.

@ch III.6.4 | The affective part is a form, like a harmony
The affective part is where feelings followed by pleasure and pain arise. Some follow opinion, some come before it. The image in soul starts the trouble, but the trembling and pallor are in the body. The affective part is a form in matter, rooted in the soul of growth; it moves the body as a harmony might move the strings, itself unmoved.

@p
We must look at what is called the affective part of the soul. In a way we have spoken of it already, when we spoke of all the affections that arise in the spirited and the desiring parts, and how each arises. But we must say more about it, first grasping what the affective part of the soul is said to be. It is said, of course, to be that in which the affections seem to arise; and these are the things that pleasure and pain follow. Of the affections, some arise on opinions, as when someone thinks he is going to die and is afraid, or thinks some good will come to him and is pleased: the opinion is in one place, and the affection is moved in another. Others go first, and of themselves, without choice, put the opinion into the part that is fitted to form opinions. That opinion leaves the opining part unchanged has been said. And fear, which is outside the opinion, comes again from above, from the opinion, giving a kind of understanding, as it were, to the part of the soul said to fear.

What does this fearing do? It causes trouble and alarm, they say, at an expected evil. This affective part of the soul is not a body, but a form; in matter, however, as are the desiring part and the parts of nourishment, growth and generation, which is the root and source of the desiring and affective form. No form should have trouble, or any affection, in it: it stays still, and its matter comes into the affection, when it does, by the presence of the form that moves it. The power of growth, when it makes things grow, does not itself grow, nor when it increases, increase; nor in general, when it moves, is it moved with the motion it causes; either it is not moved at all, or it is another kind of motion or activity. So the nature of the form must itself be an activity and act by its presence, as if a harmony moved the strings by itself. The affective part will be the cause of the affection, the movement coming from it either from the perceptual image or even without an image (we must look into whether this is when the opinion starts it from above), while it itself stays in the form of a harmony. @ch III.6.5 | Then why purify the soul?
If the soul is not affected, why make it free from affection by philosophy? Because the image coming into it is followed by trouble; reason would remove the image, as waking ends a dream. Purification is to leave the soul alone, not looking to other things or holding alien opinions; for the lower part, it is waking from strange images and not leaning down.

@p
Why, then, must we seek to make the soul free from affection by philosophy, if it is not affected in the first place? Because what comes into it in the so-called affective part, an image as it were, makes the affection that follows, the trouble, and joined to the trouble is the image of the evil expected. Reason thought it right to remove such an affection, as it is called, altogether, and not to let it come to be. While it comes to be the soul is not yet in a good state; when it does not, it is free from affection, since the cause of the affection no longer comes to be in it. It is as if someone wanting to remove the images of dreams were to bring the imagining soul to waking, saying that the dreams had made the affections, and calling the outside things, as if they were seen in a dream, affections of the soul.
!flag or as if he should say that external spectacles produce the passions | The Greek is hard. The Workshop takes it that the one who wakes the dreamer treats the dream images as having caused the soul's feelings; Taylor makes a second comparison of it.

@p
@greek ἀλλὰ τίς ἡ κάθαρσις
But what would the purification of the soul be, if it has not been stained at all? Or what is it to separate it from the body? Purification would be to leave it alone, not with others, or not looking to another, nor having alien opinions, whatever the way of opinions or affections is, as has been said; neither to see the images nor make affections from them. If it turns away from the lower things to the other side, how is this not purification? And separation too: of the soul that no longer comes to be in the body as if it belonged to it; it is like light in muddy water, which is untouched even so, though in the mud. The purification of the affective part is a waking from strange images and not seeing them; its separation is not leaning down much, and not imagining the things below. Separating it would also be taking away the things from which it is separated, when it is not on a spirit muddied by gluttony and a mass of impure flesh, but what it is in is thin, so that it may ride on it quietly.

@ch III.6.6 | Being, and the bodies that seem most real
Now matter. First, true being is not what most people think: it lacks nothing, is life and intellect, receives nothing, and is all things at once. Bodies and their substrate are not-being. Hard things seem most real because they strike us, but the heavier a thing, the more it falls short; perceiving is the sleep of the soul, and true waking is a rising from bodies.

@p
It has been said that the intelligible substance, all of it ordered by form, must be thought free from affection. But since matter too is one of the things without body, though in another way, we must look at it as well, to see how it stands: whether it is affected, as is said, and changeable in every way, or whether it too must be thought free from affection, and what that freedom is. First, those who set out to speak of its nature must grasp that the nature of being, substance and existence is not as most people think. What is, what one could truly call being, is real being; and that is what is wholly being; and that is what falls short of being in nothing. Being completely, it needs nothing to keep it safe and in being; rather, it is the cause, for the other things that seem to be, of their seeming to be. If this is rightly said, it must be in life, and in perfect life; otherwise, falling short, it will not be being any more than not-being. And this is intellect, and wisdom in every way. So it is defined and bounded, and in its power there is nothing it is not, nothing of a size that would fall short; otherwise it would fail. So it is always, and always the same, and receives nothing, and nothing comes into it. If it received anything, it would receive something besides itself, and that would be not-being. But it must be being in every way, so it must come to being with everything from itself; it is all things together and all one.
!flag and nothing is there in capacity which does not also possess a mighty power | The Greek is uncertain here; it seems to say that there is nothing in its power that it is not, nothing that would fall short. Taylor's "a mighty power" is a guess.

How, someone will ask, is the nature of bodies not-being, and how is matter, on which they rest? Mountains and rocks and all the solid earth, and everything that resists, and that forces what it strikes with its blows, bear witness that they are real. I think that the more self-sufficient things disturb others less and give them less pain; the heavier and more earthy ones, which fall short and fall and cannot lift themselves, fall from weakness and deal blows by their downward rush and dullness. Dead bodies too are more unpleasant to fall against, and strike harder and do more harm; living ones, sharing in being as far as they share in it, are kinder to their neighbours. Motion, which is like a life in bodies and imitates life, is more in things that have less body; as if the falling away of being made what it leaves behind more body. And in what are called affections one could see that what is more body is more affected: earth more than the others, and the others by the same rule. The others come together into one again when they are divided, if nothing stops them; but earth, once cut, stays apart for ever. Like things worn out in their nature, which with a small blow stay as they were struck and perish, so what has become body most, having come nearest to not-being, is too weak to gather itself back into one. Heavy and violent blows on one another bring a fall; weak falling on weak is strong against it, and not-being against not-being.

This is said against those who put the things that are in bodies, trusting the evidence of their pushes and the images of perception for the truth. Like people dreaming, they think real what they see, which is a dream. For perceiving belongs to the soul asleep: as much of soul as is in body sleeps. True waking is a true rising from body, not with body. Rising with body is a change from one sleep to another, as from one bed to another; the true rising is wholly away from bodies, which, being of the nature opposite to soul, have what is opposite to it in substance. Their coming to be, their flowing, and their perishing, which are not of the nature of being, bear witness to it.

@ch III.6.7 | Matter is a phantom, a mirror of images
Matter is incorporeal, but not soul, intellect, life, form or power: it is truly not-being, a phantom of bulk, invisible, seen when one does not look. What seems to be in it are images in an image, as in a mirror; they come and go and do nothing to it, as forms might pass through a void. So it stays unaffected.

@p
We must return to the matter that underlies, and to what are said to be in it; from these it will be known that it is not, and that matter is free from affection. It is incorporeal, since body comes later and is composite, and matter with something else makes body. It got the same name, "incorporeal", because both being and matter are other than bodies. It is not soul, nor intellect, nor life, nor form, nor rational principle, nor limit, for it is unlimitedness; nor a power, for what does it make? Falling outside all these, it could not rightly take even the name of being; it might fairly be called not-being, and not as motion is not-being, or rest, but truly not-being: an image and phantom of bulk, a longing for existence; standing, but not at rest; invisible in itself, escaping whoever wants to see it, coming to be when one does not look, and not seen when one gazes at it; always showing opposites in itself, small and great, less and more, falling short and exceeding; an image that does not stay, and cannot flee either. It has not even the strength for that, since it has taken no strength from intellect, but has come to be in the lack of all being.
!note Motion and rest are "not-being" in the sense of the Sophist (256d-e), as other than being; matter is not-being in another, truer sense.

@p
@greek διὸ πᾶν ὃ ἂν ἐπαγγέλληται
So whatever it promises, it lies. If it seems great, it is small; if more, it is less; and its being, in imagination, is not being, like a game that runs away. So the things that seem to come to be in it are games, simply images in an image, as in a mirror what is set in one place appears in another. It seems to be filled, and is empty; it has nothing and seems to be everything. The imitations and images of the things that are, which come in and go out, come into a shapeless image, and are seen in it because it is shapeless. They seem to do something to it, but do nothing, for they are feeble and weak and have nothing to push against. Nor does matter push against them; they pass through it without cutting it, as through water, or as if someone sent shapes into what is called the void.
!note "Images that come in and go out" are Plato's words in the Timaeus (50c), quoted in chapter 11.
!flag like images in water | The Greek says the images pass through matter without cutting it, "as through water"; "images in water" is Taylor's.

@p
@greek καὶ γὰρ αὖ, εἰ μὲν τοιαῦτα
For again, if the things seen in it were such as the things from which they came into it, one might perhaps suppose that it was affected by them, granting them some power of their senders that had come into it. But as it is, the things that cast the images are one thing and the things seen in it are another, and from this too one can learn that its being affected is a lie, since what is seen in it is a lie and has no likeness to what made it. Being weak and a lie, and falling into a lie, as in a dream, or in water, or in a mirror, it has of necessity left matter unaffected; and yet in the things just named there is a likeness between what is seen in them and what looks into them.

@ch III.6.8 | What is affected must have opposites in it
What is affected must have qualities opposite to those that come upon it, as heat is altered by cold. When fire perishes, the fire perishes, not the matter; affection is the road to perishing, and matter cannot perish. But how can matter hold so many mingled qualities and not be affected with them?

@p
In general, what is affected must be such as to be in the opposite powers and qualities to the things that come upon it and cause the affection. Alteration comes to the heat in a thing from what cools it, and to the wetness in it from what dries it, and we say the substrate has been altered when it has gone from hot to cold or from dry to wet. What is called the perishing of fire bears witness to this, when it changes into another element. We say the fire has perished, not the matter. So the affections are about that about which the perishing is too; for taking on an affection is the road to perishing, and the same thing perishes that is affected. But matter cannot perish: into what, and how?

@p
@greek πῶς οὖν λαβοῦσα ἐν αὑτῇ
How, then, can it take into itself heats and colds, countless qualities, without limit in a word, and be marked off by them, and have them as it were grown into it and mixed with one another (for each is not apart), itself caught in the middle of them, while the qualities are affected by one another in their mixing, and not be affected along with them? Unless one puts it wholly outside them. But everything in a substrate is present to the substrate in such a way as to give it something of its own.

@ch III.6.9 | Things can be present without affecting
One thing can be present in another in several ways: changing it, improving it without affecting it, or like a shape in wax, or light, which alters nothing. Mirrors are not affected by their images; matter is even less. Only opposites affect opposites; matter has no opposite in it, so it is like a house in which men fight, untouched by the blows.

@p
We must first grasp that one thing being present to another, and one thing being in another, does not happen in one way only. One way is to make the other worse or better by its presence, with a change, as is seen in bodies, at least those of living things. Another is to make it as it were better or worse without its being affected, as was said of the soul. Another is like bringing a shape to wax: there is no affection, so as to make the wax something else when the shape is there, nor any lack when it has gone. And light does not even alter the shape of what is lit. What does a stone that has become cold have from the cold, staying a stone? What would a line suffer from colour? Not even the surface, I think, but perhaps the body underneath. Yet what would it suffer from colour? One must not call being present, or putting a shape round, being affected. If someone said that mirrors, and transparent things in general, suffer nothing from the images seen in them, he would bring a not unlike example, a pattern (paradeigma) that fits. For the things in matter are images too, and matter is even less affected than mirrors.
!flag Nor does a stone, when it becomes cold, possess any thing besides frigidity | The Greek asks "what does a stone that has become cold have from the cold, while it stays a stone?": nothing that makes it other than a stone.

@p
@greek ἐγγίνονται μὲν δὴ ἐν αὐτῇ
Heats and colds come to be in it, but they do not heat it; for to be heated and cooled is for a quality to lead the substrate from one quality to another. (We should look into whether cold is an absence and a privation.) When the qualities come together into it, most of them will not act on one another; rather, those that are opposed will. What would fragrance do to sweetness, or colour to shape, or a thing of one kind to a thing of another? From this one could most of all believe that one thing can be in another, or something different in something different, without harming by its presence the thing to which or in which it is present. As what is harmed is not harmed by just anything, so what changes and is affected would not be affected by just anything: the affection is of opposites by opposites, and other things are unchanged by others. So things in which there is no opposition would be affected by no opposite. So if anything is affected, it must be not matter but a compound, or in general many things together. What is alone, bare of the rest, and wholly simple, would be unaffected by all things, caught in the middle of all that act on one another. When people strike one another in the same house, the house is unaffected, and the air in it. Let the things on matter act on one another, as far as they are fitted to; matter itself will be much more unaffected than the qualities in it that are unaffected by one another because they are not opposites.

@ch III.6.10 | If matter were altered, it would not stay matter
If matter were affected, it would keep something of the affection, and become qualified matter, and then many-shaped, no longer able to receive everything, and so perishable. What is altered must remain while it changes; so matter, whose being is to be matter, cannot be altered as matter, any more than a form can be altered in its substance.

@p
Then, if matter is affected, it must keep something from the affection: either the affection itself, or a different state from before the affection came into it. Then when another quality comes after that one, what receives it will no longer be matter, but qualified matter. And if this quality too went away, leaving something of itself behind by its acting, the substrate would become still more something else. Going on in this way, the substrate will be something other than matter, of many turns and many forms; so it would no longer be the receiver of all things, but a hindrance to the many that come to it. And matter will no longer remain, so it is not imperishable either. So if there must be matter, it must always be the same, as it was from the beginning; to say it is altered is not to keep it matter.

@p
@greek ἔπειτα δέ, εἰ ὅλως τὸ ἀλλοιούμενον
Then, if in general everything that is altered must stay in the same form and be altered in its attributes, not in itself; if what is altered must remain, and what remains of it is not what is affected; one of two things must be: either matter, being altered, leaves its own nature, or, not leaving it, is not altered. If someone says it is altered, but not as matter, first he will not be able to say in respect of what it will be altered; then he will admit that in this way too matter itself is not altered. As the other things, which are forms, cannot be altered in their substance, since their substance lies in this, so, since for matter to be is to be as matter, it cannot be altered in respect of being matter, but must remain. @ch III.6.11 | How matter shares in form without being changed
Plato's words, "the things that come in and go out are always imitations of the things that are", point to the puzzle: not how forms come into matter, but how they are in it while it stays unaffected. Matter gains nothing and loses nothing. If it were truly made beautiful it would cease to be matter; it shares only in seeming, and so stays evil, unaffected even by the good.

@p
That is why I think Plato too had this in mind and spoke rightly when he said "the things that come in and go out are always imitations of the things that are". He did not say "come in and go out" idly, but wanted us to understand, attending to the manner of the sharing. Perhaps the famous difficulty, how matter shares in the forms, is not what most of those before us thought, how they come into it, but rather how they are in it. It does seem truly wonderful that while they are present to it, it stays the same, unaffected by them, even though the things that come in are affected by one another. And also that the things coming in each push out the ones before, and that the affection is in the composite; and not in every composite, but in one that needs what comes or goes, one that is incomplete in its make-up when something is absent, and complete when it is present. But matter gets nothing more for its make-up when anything comes; for it does not become what it is when something comes, nor less when something goes, but remains what it was from the beginning.
!note The quotation is from the Timaeus (50c).
!flag Whence, also, I think that the divine Plato, [in the Timseus] | The Greek says only "Plato"; "the divine" and the square brackets are Taylor's.
!flag According to Plato, however, the entering forms expel those which entered prior to them | The Greek gives this as part of what is wonderful, not as Plato's teaching; "According to Plato" is Taylor's.

@p
@greek τοῦ δὲ κεκοσμῆσθαι
Things that need order and arrangement would need to be adorned; and the adornment could come without alteration, as with what we put round things. But if something were adorned so that the adornment grew into it, it would have to be altered: what was ugly before would become other, and the adorned thing would be beautiful instead of ugly. If, then, matter, being ugly, became beautiful, it is no longer what it was before by being ugly; so in being adorned in this way it will lose being matter, above all if it is not ugly by accident. If it is so ugly as to be ugliness, it could not share in adornment; and if so evil as to be evil, it could not share in good. So the sharing is not by its being affected, but in another way, as seeming. Perhaps in this way the difficulty might be solved, how, being evil, it could long for the good: it does not lose by sharing what it was. If what is called sharing is such that it stays the same, not altered, as we say, but always is what it is, it is no longer surprising how, being evil, it shares. For it does not leave itself; since it must share, it shares somehow, as long as it exists; but by being what it is, in a way of sharing that keeps it so, it is not harmed in its being by what gives in this way. And perhaps for this reason it is no less evil, because it always stays what it is. If it really shared and was really altered by the good, it would not be evil by nature. So if someone calls matter evil, he would speak truly if he called it unaffected by the good; and this is the same as being unaffected altogether.

@ch III.6.12 | Plato's figures: matter is not altered by shapes
Plato makes matter take on figures to show that it stays unaffected: if shapes cause the feelings of living bodies while matter has none, it is not altered by them. His words "burnt" and "made wet" are softened by "receiving the forms of air and water". Fire is not "burnt"; and what has no size cannot be divided.

@p
Plato, thinking this about matter, does not take sharing to be a form coming into a substrate and giving it shape, so that one composite comes about from things that change together, are as it were mixed together and affected together. He wants to show that he does not mean this, and how matter could have the forms while remaining unaffected, and he looks for a pattern of unaffected sharing; and since it is not easy to teach in another way what things, by their presence, most of all keep the substrate the same, he raises many difficulties, hastening to what he wants, and wishing besides to show the emptiness of existence in the things we perceive, and how large the room is for the merely likely. So he supposes that matter makes the feelings of living bodies by figures, while it has none of these feelings itself, and so shows that it stays, letting us infer that it is not affected or altered by the figures either.
!note In the Timaeus (53c-56c) the elements are made of geometrical figures, triangles and solids; the sensations of hot and cold, hard and soft, come from the shapes of their parts (61d-64a).

@p
@greek τοῖς μὲν γὰρ σώμασι τούτοις
For these bodies, which take one shape after another, one might perhaps say that alteration happens, calling the change of shape alteration by the same name. But matter has no shape or size: how could one call the presence of shape in any way alteration, even by the same name? If, then, someone said here "colour by convention, and the rest by convention", because the underlying nature has nothing as it is thought to have, he would not be out of keeping with the argument. But how does it have them, if even "as shapes" does not please? The supposition gives a hint, as far as can be, of its freedom from affection, and of the seeming presence of images that are not present.
!note "Colour by convention" is from Democritus (fragment 9 and 125, Diels-Kranz): only atoms and the void are real.

@p
@greek ἢ πρότερον ἔτι περὶ τῆς ἀπαθείας
Or rather, we must say more first about its freedom from affection, teaching that we must not be carried away by the habits of words into thinking it is affected, when Plato says it is dried, burnt and made wet, but must keep in mind what follows: "and receiving the forms of air and water". "Receiving the forms of air and water" blunts "burnt and made wet", and shows by "receiving forms" that it is not itself shaped, but that the forms are in it as they came. And "burnt" is not said strictly, but rather "becoming fire"; for becoming fire and being burnt are not the same. Being burnt is by another, in which there is also being affected; but how could what is itself a part of fire be burnt? It would be as if someone said the statue had gone through the bronze, to say that fire had gone through matter and burnt it besides. Again, if what comes is a rational principle, how could it burn? Or if a shape? What is burnt is already from both. How, then, from both, unless one thing comes from both? Even if one thing came about, the two would not have their affections in each other, but act upon each other.
!note "Dried, burnt, made wet, receiving the forms of air and water" is from the Timaeus (52d-e).

@p
@greek ἆῤ οὖν ἀμφοτέρων ποιούντων
Do both act, then, or does one keep the other from escaping? But when a body is divided, how is matter not divided too? And when the body has been affected by being divided, how has matter not been affected by the same affection? Or what prevents them by the same argument from saying it is destroyed: how, when the body is destroyed, is matter not destroyed? One must say further that it is a so-much that is divided, a size; and in what is not a size, the affections of size do not come to be; and in general in what is not a body the affections of body do not come to be. So those who make matter affected must grant that it is a body.

@ch III.6.13 | Receptacle and nurse: matter keeps its nature
They say matter flees from form; but matter is held in stones and rocks. It "flees" by being always itself, having form as if it never had it. Plato's "receptacle and nurse of all becoming", the "place" and "seat" in which things appear and from which they go, keep it unaffected. What comes into it is an image into what is not true, like a reflection in an invisible mirror.

@p
They should also attend to this: how they say that matter flees from form. How could it flee from the stones and rocks that hold it? They will not say that it sometimes flees and sometimes does not. If it flees by its own will, why not always? If it stays by necessity, there is no time when it is not in some form. But we must look for the cause of each matter's not always having the same form, and more in the things that come in. How, then, is it said to flee? By its very nature, and always? What would this be but that, never leaving itself, it has form as if it never had it? Otherwise they will not have any use for what Plato says: "the receptacle and nurse of all becoming". If it is receptacle and nurse, and becoming is other than it, and what is altered is in becoming, it would be before becoming, and before alteration. The receptacle, and still more the nurse, keeps it what it is, unaffected; and so does "in which each thing appears, coming to be, and from which it goes out again", and that it is "place" and "seat". And what is said and examined, when he speaks of a place of forms, does not mean an affection about it, but seeks another way.
!note "The receptacle and nurse of all becoming" is from the Timaeus (49a); "in which each thing appears ... and from which it goes out again" (49e-50a); "place" and "seat" (52a-b). That matter "flees" form is the view Plotinus is answering.

@p
@greek τίς οὖν οὗτος
What is this way? Since this nature must be none of the things that are, but must have fled from the whole substance of the things that are, and be wholly other (for they are rational principles, and are truly), it must, by this otherness, keep the safety it has been allotted: not only must it not receive the things that are, but even if there is some imitation of them, it must have no part in it so as to make it its own. So it would be wholly other. Otherwise, taking a form into its home and becoming something else with it, it lost its being other, and being the place of all things, and the receiver of everything. It must stay the same when things come in, and unaffected when they go out, so that something may always come into it and go out. What comes in, then, comes in as an image, and not true into what is not true. Truly, then? How, into what it is not lawful to share in truth, because it is a lie? Then it comes falsely into a lie, and it is much as in a mirror the images of the things seen in it are seen, as long as they look into it.

@p
@greek καὶ γὰρ εἰ ἐνταῦθα ἀνέλοις
For if here you took away the things that are, none of what is now seen in the world we perceive would appear for any time at all. The mirror here is itself seen, for it is itself a form; but matter, being no form, is not seen itself; otherwise it would have had to be seen by itself first. It suffers something like air lit up, which is invisible then too, because it was not seen even without being lit. So the things in mirrors are not believed to be, or less so, because what they are in is seen, and it stays, while they go away. But in matter, matter itself is not seen, neither with them nor without them. If the things that fill the mirrors could stay, and the mirrors themselves were not seen, no one would disbelieve that what is seen in them is real. If, then, there is something in mirrors, let the things we perceive be in matter in this way; if there is not, but it appears to be, there too we must say they appear in matter, making the existence of the things that are the cause of the appearing. The things that are always share in it truly; the things that are not, not truly, since they must not be as they would have been if they were and being were not.

@ch III.6.14 | Poverty, begging at the door
Without matter, nothing would come to be, as there is no image without a mirror. Matter is the Poverty of Plato's myth: it begs, and takes whatever it is given, and is never filled; it lies with Resource, the wisdom of the image. What it seems to receive slides off it, like an echo, or like light on a burning-glass: it holds nothing and stays itself.

@p
What then? If matter did not exist, would nothing have come to be? Not even an image, if there were no mirror or something of the kind. For what is fitted by nature to come to be in another would not come to be if that did not exist: this is the nature of an image, to be in another. If something went out from the things that are, it would be even without being in another. But since they stay, if they are to be imaged in something else, there must be something else that gives a seat to what has not come; and that, by its presence and its boldness, and its begging, as it were, and its poverty, forces itself, as it were, to receive, and is deceived by not receiving, so that the poverty may remain and always beg. For once it had come to exist, the myth makes it a beggar, showing its nature, that it is empty of good. The beggar does not ask for what the giver has, but is content with whatever he gets; so this too shows that what appears in it is other. Its name shows that it is not filled. And its lying with Resource shows that it lies not with being, nor with plenty, but with something clever: that is, with the wisdom of the image, its cleverness.
!note The myth is the birth of Eros in the Symposium (203b-c): Poverty (Penia), begging at the feast, lies with Resource (Poros) and bears Eros.
!flag What then* matter not existing, would nothing have a subsistence ? Nothing except beings | The Greek answers "not even an image, if there were no mirror"; "Nothing except beings" is Taylor's own answer.

For since whatever is in any way outside being cannot wholly fail to share in it (for this is the nature of being, to make beings), while what is wholly not-being is unmixed with being, the thing is a wonder: how, not sharing, it shares, and how it has something, as it were, from being neighbours, though by its own nature it is unable, as it were, to stick. So what it would have taken slides off it, as from a nature foreign to it, like an echo from smooth, even places: because it does not stay, it is by this very fact imagined to be there and to come from there. If it had shared and received in the way someone supposes, what came would have sunk into it and been swallowed up. But as it is, it is plain that it was not swallowed, but matter stayed the same, receiving nothing, holding back the approach like a seat that pushes away, a receptacle of the things that come to the same place and mix there. It is like the smooth things people set facing the sun when they want to catch fire, some of them filling them with water, so that the flame, hindered by the opposite within, does not pass through but gathers outside. So it becomes the cause of becoming, and the things that gather in it gather in this way.

@ch III.6.15 | Images that do not mix
The fire caught in a burning-glass is outside the glass and touches it; the forms are "outside" matter by its otherness alone, not mixing with it, as opinions and images in the soul come and go without mixing. But the soul can push its images away; matter, weaker, has nothing of its own, cannot even say "I am here", and is found only by a deep reasoning.

@p
With the things that gather fire from the sun round themselves, since they take the kindling that happens round them from a fire we perceive, they too are things we perceive; so it is plain that what gathers is outside, next to them, near them, touching them, and that there are two boundaries. But the rational principle on matter is outside in another way. The otherness of nature is enough, needing no double boundary; much more, matter is foreign to every boundary by the otherness of its substance, and keeps itself unmixed by having no kinship at all. That is the cause of its staying by itself: neither does what comes in enjoy it, nor does it enjoy what comes in. Opinions and images in the soul are not blended, but each goes away again, being alone what it is, drawing nothing with it and leaving nothing behind, because it was not mixed; it is "outside", not because it lay on the soul, and what it is on is not seen as different, but reason says so.
!flag But the productive principle in matter, has the external after a different manner | Taylor's "productive principle" is his usual word for logos, the rational principle on matter; the point is that it is "outside" matter only by matter's otherness.

@p
@greek ἐνταῦθα μὲν οὖν οἷον εἴδωλον
Here, then, the image in the soul is like an image, though the soul is not by nature an image; and though it seems to lead many things, and lead them where it likes, the soul still uses it as matter, or something like matter. Yet it has not hidden the soul, which often pushes it away by its own activities; nor has it made the soul hidden, even if it came with all its force, or imagined as itself. For the soul has in itself activities and rational principles opposed to it, by which it pushes away what comes. But matter, being far weaker in power than soul, and having nothing of the things that are, neither true nor even a falsehood of its own, has nothing by which to appear, being an emptiness of everything. It becomes the cause for others of their appearing, but cannot say even this, "I am here". If ever a deep reasoning, starting from other things, should find it out, it would say that it is something left behind by all the things that are, and by those that later seemed to be, drawn into everything, and following, as it seems, and again not following.
!flag but is not able to say even this of itself, I am here, [though I am by no means visible.] | Taylor's words in square brackets are his own; the Greek says only that matter cannot say even "I am here".

@ch III.6.16 | Size comes and goes, and matter stays
A rational principle makes matter great by putting greatness round it; take the form away and the substrate is not great. The size of a horse goes with the horse; the size of fire with the fire. If the heaven and all in it ceased, all size would leave matter, which would remain as it was. Matter is all things by sharing, size among them.

@p
A rational principle came, and led it out as far as it wished, and made it great, putting the great round it from itself, though matter was not great, nor became so; for the great on it would have been a size. If someone takes away this form, the substrate is no longer great and no longer seems so. If what came to be great was a man and a horse, and with the horse the horse's greatness came on, when the horse goes, its greatness goes too. If someone says that the horse comes to be on a great bulk of a certain size, and the greatness stays, we shall say that what stays there is not the horse's greatness but the bulk's. But if this bulk is fire or earth, when the fire or earth goes, the greatness of fire or earth goes. So matter would not enjoy the shape, nor the size; otherwise it would not become something else from fire, but would stay fire and never become what is not fire.
!flag but remaining fire, it would not become fire | The Greek says that if matter kept the size of fire, it would never pass from fire into something else, but would stay fire; Taylor's "would not become fire" loses the sense.

@p
@greek ἐπεὶ καὶ νῦν τοσαύτη γενομένη
For even now, having become so great that it seems as big as this universe, if the heaven and all within it ceased, all size would go from it with them, and plainly the other qualities with it, and it would be left as what it was, keeping none of the things that were about it before. Yet in things that have been affected by the presence of something, something is still left in what received it, when it has gone; but not in things that were not affected: as with air, when light is about it, and when it has gone. If someone wonders how what has no size will be great, let him wonder too how what has no heat will be hot. For being matter and being size are not the same for it, since there is size without matter, as there is shape without matter. If we keep matter as matter, it is all things by sharing; and size is one of all things. In bodies, which are composite, there is size with the rest, though not marked off, since size is contained in the rational principle of body. In matter, not even unmarked size is present, for it is not a body.

@ch III.6.17 | The great runs out, and matter runs with it
Matter is not size itself. The great in intellect and soul wants to be great, and gives its imitators the power to stamp it on another; running out in imaging, it drags matter with it, so that it seems great, stretched but not filled. Each principle images its own size into matter, which stretches to all, lying in the middle between form and nothing.

@p
Nor, again, will it be size itself. For size is a form, not a receiver; and size by itself is not size in this way, stretched out. But since what lies in intellect or soul wants to be great, it gave to the things that, as it were, wish to imitate it, by longing for it or moving towards it, the power to stamp their own affection on another. So the great, running in the going-out of imaging, made what is not great in matter run along with it into this greatness, and made it seem great by being stretched, though not filled. For this is the falsely great: when, by not having greatness, it is stretched out towards it in the stretching. All the things that are make their reflections in other things, and each of the makers was great as it was, and the whole was great in that way. So the greatness of each rational principle came together, of a horse, say, or of anything else, and greatness itself. Matter became great as a whole, lit up by greatness itself, and each part of it something great; and all things together appeared from the whole form to which the great belongs, and from each; and it was stretched out, as it were, towards everything and all things, forced to be in this form and in bulk, as far as the power made what is nothing to be all things.
!flag If matter, likewise, cannot adapt to itself the imitations of beings,1 on this account also it is not magnitude | The Greek says only that size by itself is not size in the way that stretched things are; Taylor's sentence about imitations of beings is not in Volkmann's text.

@p
@greek οἷον αὐτῷ τῷ φαίνεσθαι
So by its very appearing, the colour from what is not colour and the quality here from what is not quality got the same name as those, and so did the size from what is not size, or from what has only the name of size, these being seen between them and matter itself and the form itself. They appear, because they come from there; they lie, because what they appear in is not. Each thing is made great, drawn out by the power of the things seen in it, which make room for themselves; and matter is drawn to all of them without force, because it is matter for all. Each draws by the power it has, and it has it from there. What makes matter great, so that it seems great, comes from the imaging of the great, and this imaging is the greatness here. Matter, on which it is forced to run along, gives itself all together and everywhere; for it is matter of this and not of a this. What is nothing of itself can become even the opposite through another, and having become the opposite, is not that either; for then it would have stood still.

@ch III.6.18 | Like a cloak that can be taken off
A thought of the great, with power to go outward, would meet a nature with no trace of form, and make it great as an image. Matter keeps its nature, wearing this greatness like a cloak it put on while running; take it off and it is as it was. Soul receives forms without bulk; matter, a shadow with no activity of its own, waits to suffer what the maker wills, and receives all things, but in parts.

@p
Someone who has a thought of the great, if his thought had the power not only to be in itself but to be carried, as it were, outward by its power, would meet a nature that is not in the thinker, with no form and no trace of the great, nor of anything else. What would he make with this power? Not a horse, nor an ox; others will make these. Since it comes from a great father, the other cannot contain the great, but will have it as an image. So for what has not had the good fortune to be great itself, it is left to appear great in what is its own, as far as it can. And this is not to fall short, not to be scattered over many places, to have its parts akin in itself, and to lack none. For the image of the great could not bear, being an image of the great, to be equal in a small bulk; but as far as it reached out in hope towards it, it came as near as it could, with what ran along with it, which could not be left behind; and it made great what is not great, nor even seemed so, and the great we see in bulk.

Yet matter keeps its own nature, using this greatness like a garment, which it put on when it ran along with it, while it led it on its course. If the one who clothed it took it off, it stays again the same, as it was of itself, or as great as the form present makes it. For the soul, holding the forms of the things that are, being itself a form, has all things together; and since each form is together with itself, when it sees the forms of sensible things turning back to it, as it were, and coming to it, it will not bear to receive them with their multitude, but sees them with their bulk laid aside; for it cannot become anything other than what it is. But matter, having nothing to resist (for it has no activity), being a shadow, waits to suffer whatever the maker wills.

What goes out from the rational principle there already has a trace of what is going to come to be; for the rational principle moving as in an imaging imagination, or the motion from it, is a division into parts; if it were one and the same, it would not have moved, but stays. And matter cannot house all things together, as the soul can, or it would be one of them. Yet it must receive all things, and receive them not without parts. So, being place for all, it must go to all, meet all, and suffice for every extension, because it is not itself caught in an extension, but lies open to what is to come. How, then, did one thing coming in not hinder the others, which could not be on top of one another? @ch III.6.19 | The mother that does not beget
What comes into matter, its "mother", neither harms nor helps it; qualities affect one another, and living bodies are affected, but matter stays. "Receptacle and nurse" suit it better than "mother", for it begets nothing. So the ancient wise men, in their rites, showed Hermes with the organ of generation, and the Mother of all with sterile attendants: matter receives, but does not beget.

@p
The things that have come into matter, as into a mother, do it no wrong, and do it no good either. Their blows are not against it, but against one another, since powers act on their opposites, not on their substrates, unless one looks at the substrates as taken up with what comes on to them. Hot put an end to cold, black to white, or mixed together they made another quality out of themselves. So what is affected is the things mixed, and for them being affected is not being what they were. In living things too the affections are about the bodies, the alteration coming by the qualities and powers in them. When their make-up is broken up, or comes together, or is shifted against its natural make-up, the affections are in the bodies, and the knowledge of the stronger ones belongs to the souls; if not, they do not know them. But matter stays. It has suffered nothing when the cold went away and the heat came, for neither was its friend or its stranger.
!note Matter as "mother" is the Timaeus' word (50d, 51a), beside "receptacle" and "nurse".

@p
@greek ὥστε οἰκειότερον αὐτῇ
So "receptacle and nurse" are more its own; and "mother" only in the sense said, for it begets nothing. Those seem to call it mother who think that the mother stands to what is born in the place of matter, only receiving and giving nothing to the offspring, since whatever body the child has comes from food. But if the mother gives anything to what is born, it is not as matter, but because she is also form; for only form is fertile, and the other nature is barren. That, I think, is why the wise men of old, speaking in riddles in their mysteries and rites, make the ancient Hermes always have the organ of generation ready for work, showing that what begets the things in the world we perceive is the intelligible rational principle; and they show the barrenness of matter, which always stays the same, by the sterile ones about her. For they make a Mother of all, whom they call by this name, taking the principle as substrate and giving it this name to show what they mean; wishing to show, to those who want to grasp it more exactly and do not search on the surface, that it is not wholly like a mother. From afar, but as well as they could, they showed that it is barren, and not wholly female either: female so far as to receive, but no further, so far as to beget. For what came to her was neither female nor able to beget, but cut off from all power of begetting, which belongs only to what stays male.

@treatise III.7 | On eternity and time
We seem to know what eternity and time are until we try to say. Eternity is not intelligible being itself, nor rest, but the life of being that is all at once, whole and without extension, with no past and no future: "eternity remaining in one". Time is not motion, nor what is moved, nor an interval or measure of motion, nor something that follows motion, as the earlier schools said. It began when soul, restless to put what it saw into another, stepped out of eternity: time is the life of soul in a movement of passing from one way of life to another, the image of eternity, and the world is in it.

@ch III.7.1 | Where to begin
We think we understand eternity and time until we look closely; then we fall back on the ancients' opinions. Some of the ancients found the truth; we must ask who, and how we may understand. We begin with eternity, the pattern, for then its image, time, may be clear; or one may start from time and climb by recollection to what it is like.

@p
We say that eternity and time are each different from the other, and that eternity has to do with the everlasting nature, time with what comes to be and with this universe. At once, as if by a more concentrated grasp of the concept, we think we have a clear experience of them in our souls, as we are always speaking of them and naming them on every occasion. But when we try to go into them and come close, as it were, we are again at a loss in our thoughts. We take up the pronouncements of the ancients about them, some in one way, some in another, perhaps even the same ones in different ways; we rest on these and think it enough if, when asked, we can say what they thought; and content with that, we are released from inquiring any further.
!flag admitting some of the decisions of the ancients about these, and rejecting others | The Greek says that we take the pronouncements of the ancients "some in one way, some in another", perhaps the same ones in different ways; not that we admit some and reject others.

@p
@greek εὑρηκέναι μὲν οὖν τινας
We must believe that some of the ancient and blessed philosophers found the truth. But who most of all hit on it, and how we too might come to understand these things, is fitting to look into. And first we must ask about eternity, what those who make it different from time think it is. For once we know what stands as the pattern, its image too, which they say is time, might become clear. But if someone, before contemplating eternity, should form a picture of what time is, it would be open to him too to go from here to there by recollection and contemplate that which time was made like, if time has a likeness to it.
!note Time as the moving image of eternity is from the Timaeus (37d).

@ch III.7.2 | Eternity is not intelligible being, nor rest
Is eternity the intelligible substance itself, as some say time is the heaven? Both are most venerable, both contain all; but we say the pattern is "eternal", so eternity is something about it. Nor is it rest: rest is not eternal of itself, motion there is eternal too, and eternity must be one and without extension, which rest is not.

@p
What, then, should we say eternity is? The intelligible substance itself, as if someone said that time is the whole heaven and universe? For some, they say, held this view of time too. Since we imagine and think of eternity as something most venerable, and what belongs to the intelligible nature is most venerable, and one cannot say which is more venerable (and of what is beyond, not even this should be said), one might bring them together into one. Besides, the intelligible world and eternity both contain, and contain the same things. But when we say that one lies in the other, and when we predicate "eternal" of them ("for the nature of the pattern", he says, "happened to be eternal"), we again say that eternity is something else, though we say it is about that nature, or in it, or present to it. That each is venerable does not show that they are the same; perhaps the venerableness of one comes from the other. And the containing will be, for the one, as of parts; for eternity, of the whole together, not as a part, but because all such things as are eternal are so by it.
!note "The nature of the pattern happened to be eternal" is from the Timaeus (37d).
!flag and since also, that which is beyond these is not to be predicated in the same way | The Greek says that of what is beyond (the One) we should not even say "most venerable"; Taylor's "in the same way" softens it.

@p
@greek ἀλλ’ ἆρα κατὰ τὴν στάσιν
Must we say, then, that eternity there is by rest, as they say time here is by motion? One might fairly ask whether we say eternity is the same as rest, or not simply, but as the rest that is about substance. If it is the same as rest, first we shall not call rest eternal, as we do not call eternity eternal; for the eternal is what shares in eternity. Then how is motion eternal? For so it would be at rest. Then how does the concept of rest contain "always" in it? I mean not "always" in time, but as we think it when we say "everlasting". If it is the rest of substance, we shall again put the other kinds outside eternity. Then too eternity must be thought of not only in rest but in one; and as without extension, so as not to be the same as time; but rest, as rest, contains in itself neither the concept of the one nor of the unextended. Again, we predicate of eternity that it "remains in one"; so it would share in rest, but not be rest itself.
!note "Remaining in one" is from the Timaeus (37d), "eternity remaining in one".

@ch III.7.3 | Eternity is life all at once
What makes the intelligible world eternal is something gathered from many, and yet one: substance, motion, rest, otherness and sameness, brought together into one life. Eternity is that life, unceasing and the same, holding all things present at once, with nothing past and nothing to come: what neither was nor will be, but only is.

@p
What, then, would this be, by which we call the whole world there eternal and everlasting? And what is everlastingness: the same as eternity, or is eternity by it? It must be not by some one thing, but a thought, or a nature, gathered together from many, either following the things there, or with them, or seen in them; all these being one, yet able to be many, and being many. Whoever has looked at its great power calls it substance in respect of its being, as it were, a substrate; then motion, in respect of the life he sees in it; then rest, in respect of its being in every way the same; and other and same, in that all these are together one.
!flag Shall we say, therefore, that it is necessary to conceive of eternity as one certain thing, but a certain intelligence or nature collected together from many things | Volkmann's Greek has no "not" here, but the sense needs one: eternity is "not by some one thing, but" gathered from many, and the modern version reads it so. Taylor's sentence contradicts itself.

@p
@greek οὕτω δὴ καὶ συνθεὶς πάλιν αὖ
So, putting them together again into one, to be only life together, drawing the otherness in them together, and seeing the unceasing of the activity, the sameness, never other, not a thought or life going from one thing to another, but always the same and without extension: seeing all these, he has seen eternity. He has seen a life that remains in the same, always having the whole present: not now this and then another, but all at once; not now some things and then others, but a fulfilment without parts, as if all were together in a point and never went out into flowing. It remains in the same, in itself, and does not change; it is always in the present, because nothing of it has passed, nor is anything going to be, but what it is, it always is. So eternity is not the substrate, but what shines out, as it were, from the substrate itself, according to the sameness it declares about what is not going to be, but already is: that it is so, and not otherwise.

@p
@greek τί γὰρ ἂν καὶ ὕστερον αὐτῷ
What could come to it later that it does not have now? Nor will there be later what is not already. There is nothing from which it will come into the now, for that was not other, but this. Nor, since there is nothing in the future that it does not have now, will it of necessity have "was" about it: what is there that was for it and has passed? Nor "will be": what will be for it? What is left is that in being it is just what it is. So what neither was nor will be, but only is, having its being at rest by not changing into "will be" nor having changed, this is eternity. So the life about being, in being, all together and full, without extension in every way, is what we are looking for: eternity.

@ch III.7.4 | Eternity is from being, and with it
Eternity is not an accident of being, but from it and with it, as truth and beauty are. The true whole lacks nothing, so nothing can come to it: no "will be". Things that come to be need their future to be what they are, and hasten towards it; the universe moves in a circle out of longing for being. The first beings long for nothing. Eternity is named from "always being".

@p
We must not think that eternity is an accident that has come to that nature from outside; it is from it and with it. For it is seen in substance, in its own nature; since all the other things that we say are there we see as present in it, and say they are all from substance and with substance. For the things that are first must be with the first and in the first; the beautiful too is in them and from them, and truth is in them. Some are, as it were, in a part of the whole of being, others in the whole; just as this true whole is not gathered from parts, but has begotten the parts, so that in this way too it may be truly a whole. And truth there is not agreement with something else, but belongs to each thing whose truth it is.
!flag And in a certain degree, indeed, the whole itself is as it were in a part, and the things which are there are as parts in a whole | The Greek says that some of the things there are as in a part of the whole of being, others in the whole; Taylor's sentence is not what it says.

@p
@greek δεῖ δὴ τὸ πᾶν τοῦτο τὸ ἀληθινόν
This true whole, if it is to be truly whole, must not only be whole in being all things, but must have the whole in such a way as to lack nothing. If so, there will be nothing that "will be" for it; for if something will be, it was lacking that, and so was not whole. And what could happen to it against its nature? It suffers nothing. If, then, nothing could happen to it, it is not going to be, it will not be, and it did not come to be. For things that come to be, if you take away the "will be", since they are always acquiring, not being is at once there for them. For things not of this kind, if you add the "will be", the result is that they slip from their seat in being; for it is plain that being was not part of their nature, if it comes to be in the future, in coming to be and in being later.

@p
@greek κινδυνεύει γὰρ τοῖς μὲν γεννητοῖς
For things that come to be, it seems, substance is to be from the beginning of their coming to be until they reach the last of the time in which they no longer are; this is their being, and if one took it away, their life would be lessened, and so their being. And for the universe it is the same, towards what will be in this way. That is why it hastens to the future and does not want to stand still, drawing being to itself by making one thing after another and moving in a circle, out of a longing for substance. So we have found the cause of the motion that hastens so to be always in the future. But for the first and blessed beings there is not even a longing for the future, for they are already the whole, and they have all the life that is as it were due to them; so they seek nothing, since the future is nothing to them, nor that in which the future is. So the complete and whole substance of being, not only the substance in the parts, but in its lacking nothing further and in nothing not-being coming to it (for not only must all the things that are be present to the all and the whole, but nothing of what at some time is not), this state and nature of it would be eternity: for eternity, aiōn, comes from "always being", aei on.
!note The etymology is Aristotle's (On the Heavens I.9, 279a): aiōn from aei ōn, "always being".

@ch III.7.5 | Contemplating eternity, one becomes eternal
To see eternity is to see what has never had anything happen to it, always the same, so that whenever you look you find it so. One who never stopped looking, with a tireless nature, would himself stand in eternity. Eternity is the being with this condition showing in it: venerable, the same as God, a god showing himself as he is; an infinite life that loses nothing.

@p
And this, when I can speak of it by applying myself to it with something in the soul, or rather when I see it as something about which nothing has come to be at all (for if it had, it would not be always being, or not always something whole being): is it then already everlasting? No, unless such a nature were also present in it that one could be confident about it, that it is so and no longer otherwise, so that if you turned to it again you would find it such. What, then, if someone never stood away from contemplating it, but always stayed with it, wondering at its nature, and able to do so with a tireless nature? Or he too would run to eternity and stand still, swerving nowhere, so as to be like it and eternal, contemplating eternity and the eternal by what is eternal in himself.

@greek εἰ οὖν τὸ οὕτως ἔχον
If, then, what is in this state is eternal and always being, what does not swerve in any way to another nature, having life, which it has all already, not having taken any more, nor taking, nor going to take, then what is in this state would be everlasting; everlastingness would be this condition of the substrate, from it and in it; and eternity would be the substrate with this condition shining in it. That is why eternity is venerable, and the concept says it is the same as God. And eternity might well be called a god, showing himself and bringing himself to light as he is: being, as unshaken and the same, and so too firmly in life. If we say it is made of many things, we should not wonder; for each of the things there is many, through infinite power. For the infinite too is so by never failing; and this strictly, because it uses up nothing of itself. If someone called eternity in this way a life already infinite, by being all and using up nothing of itself, since it has no past and no future (otherwise it would not already be all), he would be close to defining it. @ch III.7.6 | "Eternity remaining in one"
Plato rightly said eternity "remains in one": the life of being around the One, true being that never is not and never is otherwise, with no before or after. "Always" is said only for clearness, as "true" is added to "philosopher". What is in time, however complete, still needs what comes next; eternity needs nothing. Plato's "he was good" refers to the whole, not to a beginning in time.

@p
Since such a nature, so wholly beautiful and everlasting, is about the One, from it and towards it, not going out from it at all but always staying about it and in it and living by it, Plato, I think, said this well, with deep thought and not idly: "eternity remaining in one". So that not only does it lead itself into one, towards itself, but it is the life of being about the One, in the same way. This is what we are looking for; and what remains so is eternity. For what is this, and remains so, and is the remaining itself, which is the activity of a life that remains of itself towards that One and in it, and is false neither in its being nor in its living, would be eternity. For to be truly is never not to be, nor to be otherwise; and that is to be the same; and that is to be without difference. So it has no "this and then that"; nor will you spread it out, unroll it, draw it forward, or stretch it; nor can you take anything before it or after it. If, then, there is no before or after about it, and "is" is the truest thing about it, and is itself, and this in the sense that it is as substance or as living, again what we call eternity comes to us.
!note "Eternity remaining in one" is from the Timaeus (37d).

@p
@greek ὅταν δὲ τὸ ἀεὶ λέγωμεν
When we say "always", and "not being at one time and not being at another", we must think it is said for the sake of clearness. For "always" is perhaps not said strictly; taken to show that it is imperishable, it might lead the soul astray into going out into more, and further into the thought that it will never fail. Perhaps it would have been better to say only "being". But just as "being" is enough of a name for substance, yet since people thought becoming was substance too, they needed, to be understood, the addition of "always". For being is not one thing and always being another, any more than a philosopher is one thing and the true philosopher another; but because there was what put on the mask of philosophy, the addition of "true" came about. So "always" was added to being, and being to "always", so that we say "always being". So "always" must be taken as meaning "truly being", and "always" must be drawn together into an unextended power that needs nothing beyond what it already has; and it has everything. So such a nature is everything, and lacks nothing in any way, and is not full in one way and lacking in another.

@ch III.7.7 | From eternity down to time
We could not speak of eternity unless we had a share in it. To see how we are in time and in eternity, we must go down to time, as time itself came down. The ancients' views of time fall into three: it is motion, or what is moved, or something of motion: its interval, its number or measure, or what follows it.

@p
In saying these things, are we bearing witness to others, as if speaking of things foreign to us? How could that be? What understanding could there be for those not in touch? And how could we be in touch with what is foreign? So we too must have a share in eternity. But how, being in time? How it is possible to be in time and how in eternity might be known once time has been found. So we must go down from eternity to the search for time, and to time. There, our road led upward; now let us speak going down, not all the way, but as time came down.

@p
@greek εἰ μὲν οὖν περὶ χρόνου εἰρημένον
If nothing had been said about time by the ancient and blessed men, we would have had to join what follows to eternity from the start, and say what seems true about it, trying to fit the view we put forward to the concept of it that we have. But as it is, we must first take up what has been said most worthy of account, and see whether our own account will agree with any of it. Perhaps we should divide the accounts given of it into three at first. They would say time is either what is called motion, or what is moved, or something of motion. To say it is rest, or what is at rest, or something of rest, would be wholly remote from the concept of time, which is in no way the same. Of those who say motion, some would say all motion, others that of the universe; those who say what is moved would say the sphere of the universe; others say something of motion: either the interval of motion, or its number or measure, or in general what follows it; and either of all motion or of ordered motion.
!note The views are those of the schools: the sphere of the universe (the Pythagoreans, as Aristotle reports, Physics IV.10, 218b); the interval of motion (the Stoics); the number of motion (Aristotle, Physics IV.11, 219b); what follows motion (Epicurus).

@ch III.7.8 | Time is not motion, nor the interval of motion
Time is not motion: motions are in time, and can stop, while time cannot; the heaven's circuit takes time, half a circuit half the time. Nor is it the sphere. Nor the interval of motion: motions of the same kind differ in speed, and are measured by something else; the interval in a motion is only its quantity, and that is in time; and rest has a duration too.

@p
Time cannot be motion, whether one takes all motions together and makes them one, as it were, or takes the ordered motion; for each motion so called is in time. If someone says there is a motion not in time, it would be much further from being time, since what motion is in is one thing and motion itself another. Of all that could be and has been said, this one thing would be enough: motion can stop and break off, but time cannot. If someone says the motion of the universe does not break off, still this too, if he means its circuit, would go round to the same point in some time, not the time in which half is done; and the one time would be half, the other double, though both are motions of the universe, the one from the same point to the same, the other reaching only half. And saying that the motion of the outermost sphere is the quickest and fastest bears witness to the argument, that its motion and time are different. For it is plainly the fastest of all by covering the greater, the greatest, distance in less time; the others are slower because they cover it, or a part of it, in more. If, then, the motion of the sphere is not time, still less would the sphere itself, which was supposed to be time because it moves.

@p
@greek ἆῤ οὖν κινήσεώς τι
Is it, then, something of motion? If interval, first, it is not the same for every motion, not even for motion of the same kind; for motion, even in place, is faster and slower. Both intervals would be measured by one other thing, which one would more rightly call time. And time would be the interval of which of them, or rather of which of all of them, since they are without number? If of ordered motion, not of all of it, nor of all of that kind, for there are many; so there will be many times at once. If it is the interval of the motion of the universe: if it is the interval in the motion itself, what would it be but the motion, of so much? This so much will be measured either by place, because the motion has gone through so much, and this will be the interval; but this is not time, but place. Or the motion itself, by its continuity and by not stopping at once but always going on, will have the interval. But this would be the muchness of motion. If someone looking at it declares it much, as one might say the heat is much, here too time will not appear or come into view, but motion again and again, like water flowing again and again, and the interval seen on it. The "again and again" will be number, like two or three; the interval will belong to bulk.

@p
@greek οὕτως οὖν καὶ πλῆθος κινήσεως
So too the muchness of motion, like ten, or like the interval that appears on the bulk of motion, as it were, has no concept of time in it; this so much will have come to be in time, or time will not be everywhere but in motion as in a substrate, and we come round again to saying that time is motion. For the interval is not outside it; it is motion not all at once. And what is not all at once, if what is all at once is in time, how will it differ from what is all at once, except by being in time in another way? So spread-out motion and its interval are not time itself, but in time. If someone says time is the interval of motion, not that of the motion itself, but that along which the motion has its extension, as if running along beside it, he has not said what this is. For it is plain that this is time, in which the motion has taken place. And this was what the argument was looking for from the beginning: what time is. It is the same as if someone, asked what time is, said: the interval of motion in time. What, then, is this interval, which he calls time, setting it outside the motion's own interval? And again, whoever sets the interval in the motion itself will be at a loss where to set the duration of rest. For as long as one thing is moving, another may be standing still; and you would say the time of each is the same, as being, plainly, other than both. What, then, is this interval, and what nature has it? It cannot be spatial, since that too is outside.

@ch III.7.9 | Time is not the number or measure of motion
If time is the number or measure of motion, of which motion? The same number for orderly and disorderly, fast and slow, would be like ten measuring horses and oxen: it says what time measures, not what it is. A measure running along with motion, or a number "according to before and after", already presupposes time; and time, being without limit, would exist before it was measured, even before a soul measured it.

@p
How time is the number or measure of motion (measure is better, since motion is continuous) must be considered. First, here too there is the difficulty, as with the interval of motion, if someone says it is of all motion. How could one number disorderly and irregular motion, or what number or measure would there be of it, or by what would the measure go? If by the same thing for both, and in general for all, fast and slow, the number and measure will be such as if ten were measuring both horses and oxen, or the same measure were for wet things and for dry. If it is a measure of this sort, it has been said of what things time is, of motions, but not yet what it is itself. If, as ten can be thought as number even taken without horses, and a measure is a measure having a nature of its own even if it is not yet measuring, so time too, being a measure, must be: if it is in itself something like number, how would it differ from this number of ten, or from any other number of units? If it is a continuous measure, being a measure of some quantity, it will be like the cubit. It will be a magnitude, then, like a line running along with the motion. But how will this, running along, measure what it runs along with? Why should either measure the other rather than be measured? It is better and more convincing to suppose it is not of all motion but of the motion it runs with. This must be continuous, as long as the one running with it holds on.

And which of them will be time, the measured motion or the measuring magnitude? Time will be either the motion measured by the magnitude, or the magnitude that measured, or what uses the magnitude, as one uses a cubit, to measure how much the motion is. In all these cases it is more convincing, as we said, to suppose the motion regular; for without regularity, and further without one motion, that of the whole, the account is harder for whoever puts forward any kind of measure. If time is measured motion, measured by quantity, then, just as the motion, if it had to be measured, had to be measured not by itself but by something else, so, if the motion is to have a measure other than itself, and for this we needed the continuous measure, the magnitude itself will in the same way need a measure, so that the motion may be measured as so much as the thing it is measured by. @ch III.7.10 | What follows motion
To call time something that follows motion does not say what it is before saying what follows; and whatever this is, it is said to be in time. Enough of reviewing others; the views already answered serve against the rest. We must now say what time is.

@ch III.7.11 | Time born when soul set out from eternity
Go back to eternity, the still, whole, unlimited life. Time was not yet. A restless power of soul, wanting to rule itself and seek more than the present, moved, and time moved with it. As a seed unfolds into a weaker length, soul, making the world in imitation, made itself temporal in place of eternity and put the world under time. Time is the life of soul in a motion passing from one way of life to another: the image of eternity, one by continuity, unlimited by always going on, a whole by parts, in soul as eternity is in being.

@p
We must, then, take ourselves back again to that state which we said belonged to eternity, that untroubled life, all together and already unlimited, unbending in every way, standing in one and towards one. Time was not yet, or at least was not for those things; it was to come into being, by the rational order and nature of what comes after. With these at rest in themselves, how time first fell out, one would perhaps not call on the Muses to tell, since they were not yet; and perhaps not even if the Muses were there then. But one might ask time itself, now that it has come into being, how it came to light and came to be. It would say something like this about itself: that before, before it had produced this "before" and needed the "after", it rested with it in being, not being time, and was itself at rest in that. But there was a meddlesome nature, which wanted to rule itself and be its own, and chose to seek more than what it had present; and it moved, and time moved with it; and moving always on to the "next" and the "after", and not the same but other and again other, making a certain length of our journey, we made time, an image of eternity.
!note "An image of eternity" is from the Timaeus (37d). The "meddlesome nature" is the soul.

For the world moves in soul (there is no other place of this universe than soul), and so it moved in soul's time too. For, putting out its activity one after another, then another again in order, it produced, with the activity, the succession; and with another thinking after that one, there went out what was not there before, since neither had the thinking been put into act, nor was the present life like the one before it. So at once there was another life, and in being another it had another time. @ch III.7.12 | The heavens show time; they do not make it
Time is the length of soul's life going on in smooth, silent changes. If soul turned back to eternity and was still, time would be gone, and the sphere with it: so soul's motion and life produce time, which is why Plato says time came to be with the universe. The motions of the stars were made to show and mark off time, the day and night giving the idea of number; time is not itself a measure, and is measured by the circuit only incidentally, as a magnitude is "what a cubit measures".

@p
One must understand from this too that this nature, this length of such a life, going on in changes that are smooth and alike and go on without a sound, has its activity continuous. If, then, in thought we were to make this power turn back and stop this life, which it now has without stopping and never ceasing, because it is the activity of a soul that always is, not towards itself nor in itself, but in making and coming into being; if, then, we supposed it no longer active, but with this activity stopped and this part of the soul turned to what is there and to eternity and remaining at rest, what would there still be after eternity? What would be other and other, with all things remaining in one? What would be before, or after, or more? Where would soul turn its attention to something other than what it is in? Or rather, not even to that; for it would have to have stood apart from it first, to turn its attention to it. For not even the sphere itself would be there, since it does not exist before time; for it too both is and moves in time; and if it stood still while that soul was active, we would measure how long its rest was, as long as that soul is outside eternity. If, then, when that soul withdraws and becomes one, time is done away with, it is plain that the beginning of its motion towards these things, and this life, produces time.

That is why it is said that time came to be together with this universe, because soul produced it along with this universe. For in such activity this universe too came to be; and the activity is time, and the universe is in time. If someone says that the courses of the stars are called times by him, let him remember that he says they came to be for showing and marking off time, and that there might be a clear measure. For since it was not possible for the soul to mark off time itself, nor for people to measure each part of it among themselves, invisible as it is and not to be grasped, and especially for people who did not know how to count, he made day and night, through which it was possible, by their difference, to grasp two, from which, he says, came the idea of number. Then, grasping how long it is from sunrise to sunrise again, they could have so long an interval of time, the kind of motion being regular, which we lean on; and we use such a thing as a measure, a measure of time; for time itself is not a measure.
!note From the Timaeus: time came to be with the heaven (38b); the sun, moon and planets were made to mark off time (38c); day and night gave the idea of number (39b, 47a).

@p
@greek πῶς γὰρ ἂν καὶ μετροῖ
For how would it measure, and what would it say in measuring, "this is as much as I am, so much"? Who, then, is this "I"? Surely that by which the measuring is done. So it is, in order to measure, and is not the measure. So the motion of the universe, being measured, will be measured by time, and time will not be the measure of motion by what it is, but, being something else first, will incidentally show how much the motion is. And the one motion taken in so much time, counted many times, will lead to the idea of how much time has passed. So if someone says that the motion and the circuit in a way measure time, as far as possible, as showing, in their own so much, the so much of time, which cannot otherwise be grasped or understood, he would not be out of place in his way of showing. So what is measured by the circuit, that is, what is shown by it, will be time, not produced by the circuit but shown by it. And so the measure of motion is what is measured by a definite motion, and is measured by it, being other than it; since as measuring it was one thing and as measured another, but measured incidentally. It would be just as if someone said that magnitude is what a cubit measures, not saying what it is, defining magnitude; or as if someone, unable to show motion itself because it is without limit, said it was what is measured by place; for, taking the place that the motion went over, he would say it was as much as the place.

@ch III.7.13 | Time is in soul, as eternity is in being
The circuit shows the time it is in; time itself is first, the "in which" of all else. The philosophers called it the measure of motion, putting the incidental for the essential; Plato says only that it came to be with the heaven as a moving image of eternity. Soul's own motion is first in time and produces it, and is everywhere in the world. Bodily motion leads back to soul's motion, and that to the unextended. Time is in every soul of this kind alike, and all are one, so time is not torn apart.

@p
So the circuit shows time, in which it itself is. But time itself must no longer have an "in which"; it must be first what it is, that in which the other things move and rest smoothly and in order, and it must show itself and appear from something orderly to our understanding, but not come into being from it, whether that thing is at rest or moving, but rather from what is moving. For motion moves us more towards knowing and passing over to time than rest does, and how much something has moved is more knowable than how long it has rested. That is why they were carried into saying "measure of motion" instead of saying "measured by motion", and then adding what it is that is measured by motion, and not saying something that comes about about it incidentally, and these things the wrong way round. But perhaps they did not have them the wrong way round, and it is we who do not understand them; when they said plainly "measure" in the sense of "measured", we missed their meaning. The cause of our not understanding is that they do not make clear in their writings what time is, measuring or measured, writing for those who knew and had heard them.

@p
@greek ὁ μέντοι Πλάτων οὔτε μετροῦν
Plato, however, said neither that its being is measuring nor that it is measured by something, but that, to show it, the circuit took a very small part for a very small part of it, so that from it one could know of what kind and how much time is. But wanting to show its being, he says that it came to be together with the heaven, after the pattern of eternity, and as a moving image, because time does not remain, since the life it runs and races along with does not remain; and together with the heaven, because such a life makes the heaven too, and one life produces heaven and time. If this life were turned back into one, if it could be, at once time would have stopped, being in this life, and the heaven too, not having this life. If someone takes the before and after of this life and motion, and calls it time, saying that it is something, but says that the before and after of the truer motion is not anything, he would be utterly absurd, granting to a lifeless motion that it has a before and after, and time with it, but not granting this to the motion by imitation of which this one exists, from which the before and after first came about, since it is a motion that works of itself, and as it produces each of its activities, so it produces their succession, and with the producing, their passing from one to the next.
!flag that the circulation [of the universe] is allotted something which is the smallest [i. e. the centre,] | Taylor's gloss "the centre" in square brackets is his own and misleads: the Greek says the circuit takes a very small part (as the day) for the smallest part of time, so we can know time from it.
!note "A moving image of eternity" is from the Timaeus (37d), and "together with the heaven" from 38b.

@p
@greek διὰ τί οὖν ταύτην μὲν
Why, then, do we bring this motion of the universe under the embrace of that one, and say it is in time, but not the motion of soul too, which is in itself and in an everlasting passage? Because what is before it is eternity, which does not run along or stretch along with it. This motion, then, is first in time, and it produced time and has it with its own activity. How, then, is time everywhere? Because that soul is apart from no part of the world, as the soul in us is apart from no part of us. If someone says that time is in no real existence or is not there at all, he will plainly be calling God himself a liar when he says "was" and "will be"; for it will be and was in the way that that in which he says it will be is. But for such people there is another kind of argument.
!flag for we are deceived about its essence, in the same manner as when we say of God that he was or will be | Taylor's words are not in the Greek. The Greek says that whoever denies that time is real makes God himself false when he says "was" and "will be", taking "God" as Plato's maker in the Timaeus, or the god who speaks there.

@p
@greek ἐκεῖνο δὲ ἐνθυμεῖσθαι
Beyond all that has been said, one must keep this in mind: when someone takes a man in motion, how far he has gone, he takes how much the motion is too; and when he takes the motion, say, the walking, he sees that the movement in the man before this motion was so much, if it kept the body's motion going for so much. He will lead the body moved for so much time back to the motion of so much, since this is the cause, and to its time; and this motion back to the motion of the soul, which was spread out in equal parts. And the motion of the soul back to what? Whatever he chooses will now be unextended. This, then, is what is first, and that in which the rest are; but it is itself no longer in anything, since it will have nothing to be in. And the same holds of the soul of the universe. Is time, then, in us too? It is in every soul of this kind, alike in all, and all are one. So time will not be torn apart; any more than eternity, which in another way is in all the things of the same form.
!flag he directs his attention to the boundary s of motion existing in the man prior to this motion, in order that he may judge whether he has walked to the full extent of this boundary | The Greek speaks of the movement in the man, in his soul, before the bodily motion, which kept the body's motion going so far; not of a "boundary" of motion.

@treatise IV.2 | On the essence of the soul
A short treatise, the second in Porphyry's order on the soul's essence. Having shown elsewhere that the soul is not a body, nor a harmony, nor an entelechy, Plotinus places it among the intelligibles and goes further. There is what is wholly divided, bodies; there is what is wholly undivided, intellect; and between them, forms and qualities that are divided in bodies, and soul, which is divided about bodies because it is present in every part, yet undivided because it is whole in each. This is what Plato meant when he said the maker mixed a third kind of being from the undivided and the divided. Sensation proves it: if soul were divided like a body, one part would not feel what another suffers; if it were simply one, it would give life to no body.

@ch IV.2.1 | Divided and undivided being
There is being wholly divided, as bodies are, and being wholly undivided, needing no place, like the centre from which the lines of a circle go out. Next to bodies are forms and qualities, divided in them but whole in each piece. Next to the undivided is soul: a being that gives itself to bodies and is divided about them, yet remains one, whole in all their parts and whole in any part. The division belongs to the bodies, not to the soul.

@p
Looking for what the essence of the soul is, we showed it is not a body, and not a harmony among things without body either; we left aside the entelechy, as not true as it is said and not showing what it is; and we said it belongs to the intelligible nature and to the divine portion. Perhaps we would have said something clear about its essence. Still, it is better to go further. Then we divided by distinguishing the perceptible and the intelligible nature, and setting the soul in the intelligible. Now let it lie in the intelligible; but by another road let us track down what is closest to its nature. We say, then, that some things are divided in the first place and scattered by their nature: these are things none of whose parts is the same as another part or as the whole, and their part must be less than the whole. These are the perceptible magnitudes and masses, each of which has its own place, and the same cannot be in many places at once.
!note The earlier work is Ennead IV.7, On the immortality of the soul, which Porphyry places later but Plotinus wrote earlier: it argues that soul is not a body, not a harmony and not an entelechy. "The divine portion" is Plato's phrase (Phaedrus 230a, Republic 493a).
!flag the entelecheia ,* or perfection of the body | Taylor is right that the word is Aristotle's (On the Soul II.1, 412a): the soul as "the first actuality of a natural body". "Of the body" is his own addition.

@p
@greek ἡ δέ ἐστιν ἀντιτεταγμένη
But the being set over against this admits no division at all; it is without parts and undivided, and admits no extension, not even in thought; it needs no place and comes to be in none of the beings, neither in parts nor in wholes, riding, as it were, on all beings together, not to be settled in them, but because the others cannot be, and do not want to be, without it; being always the same, common to all that come after, like the centre in a circle, from which all the lines going out to the circumference, fastened to it, leave it none the less staying by itself; they have their origin and being from it, and share in the point, and the partless is their principle, yet they have gone out fastened to it. With this, which is undivided in the first place among the intelligibles and leader among beings, and that again among perceptibles which is divided every way, there is before the perceptible, and near it and in it, another nature, divided not in the first place, as bodies are, but divided when it comes to be in bodies: so that when the bodies are divided, the form in them is divided too, yet is whole in each of the divided pieces, the same becoming many, each of which stands quite apart from the other, since it has become wholly divided. Such are colours and all qualities, and each shape, which can be whole in many things at once that are apart, with no part of it affected as the other is. So this too must be set down as wholly divided.

Even if the magnitude is one, still what is the same in each part has no fellowship for being affected together, because this "same" is one thing here and another there; for the sameness is an affection, not itself a being. The nature that is at once divided and undivided, which we say is soul, is not one as the continuous is one, having one part and then another; it is divided, because it is in all the parts of that in which it is, and undivided, because it is whole in all of them and whole in any one of them. @ch IV.2.2 | Why the soul must be both
If the soul were divided like a body, the soul in the finger would feel its own pain and no other part would; there would be many souls in each of us. The Stoic account of sensations passed on to a ruling part fails: which part feels, and how could one know a thing from eyes and ears together? If soul were wholly one, it would animate nothing. So soul is one and many, as Plato's "third kind of being" says: soul one and many, forms in bodies many and one, bodies many only, the highest one only.

@p
That the nature of the soul had to be of this kind, and that besides this there cannot be a soul that is only undivided or only divided, but that it must be both in this way, is plain from what follows. If it were like bodies, having one part here and another there, when one part was affected the other part would not come to perceive what the affected part felt; but that soul, say the one about the finger, as being different and on its own, would perceive the affection; and there would be many souls in all governing each of us; and this universe too would have not one but unlimited souls apart from each other. For continuity, unless it comes together into one, is pointless. For what those who deceive themselves say, that perceptions reach the ruling part by passing on, is not to be accepted.
!note The "ruling part" (τὸ ἡγεμονοῦν) and the passing on of perceptions to it are Stoic teaching.

@p
@greek πρῶτον μὲν γὰρ ἡγεμονοῦν
First, a ruling part of the soul is spoken of without examination. How will they divide, and say one part is this and another that? By what size of quantity will they separate each, or by what difference of quality, when the mass is one and continuous? And will the ruling part alone perceive, or the other parts too? If the perception falls on the ruling part alone, in what place will it perceive what is perceived? If on another part of the soul not made by nature to perceive, this part will not pass on its affection to the ruling part, and there will be no perception at all. And if it falls on the ruling part itself, either it will fall on a part of it, and when this has perceived the rest will no longer perceive, for it would be pointless; or there will be many perceptions, unlimited, and not all alike, but one will say "I was affected first", another "I perceived the affection of another"; and where the affection came about, each will not know, except the first. Or each part of the soul will be deceived, thinking it came about where it is itself. If not only the ruling part but any part perceives, why will one be ruling and another not? Or why must the perception go up to it? And how will it know one thing from many perceptions, from ears and eyes?

So the soul must be one and many in this way, divided and undivided, and we must not disbelieve, as if it were impossible for the same thing to be one and in many places.
