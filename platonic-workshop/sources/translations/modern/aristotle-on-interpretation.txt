@treatise Int | On Interpretation
The second of Aristotle's works that the Platonists read, after the Categories. The Categories was about simple words; this work is about words joined into statements, which are true or false. It defines the noun, the verb, the sentence and the statement, and then the affirmation and the denial, and how they are opposed: as contradictories, one of which must be true and the other false, or as contraries, which cannot both be true but can both be false. Chapter 9 asks whether this holds of statements about single future events ("there will be a sea battle tomorrow"), and answers that it does not hold in the same way, since otherwise everything would happen of necessity and there would be no point in deliberating. The later chapters treat statements with "not" attached to the subject or the predicate, statements that combine several predicates, and statements of what is possible, contingent, impossible and necessary; the last chapter asks whether the contrary of a true belief is its denial or the belief in its contrary. The Greek title, Peri Hermeneias, means "on expression": how thought is put into speech.

@ch Int.1 | Words, thoughts and things; truth comes with combination
Spoken words are signs of what the soul undergoes, and written words are signs of spoken ones. Words differ between peoples, but the experiences of the soul, and the things they are likenesses of, are the same for all. Single words are like thoughts without combination: neither true nor false until "is" or "is not" is added.

@p
First we must lay down what a noun is and what a verb is; then what a denial, an affirmation, a statement and a sentence are. Spoken sounds are symbols of what is undergone in the soul, and written marks are symbols of spoken sounds. Just as all people do not have the same writing, so they do not all have the same spoken sounds. But what these are first of all signs of, the experiences of the soul, are the same for all; and the things that these experiences are likenesses of are the same as well. These matters have been discussed in my work on the soul, since they belong to another inquiry.
!flag symbols of the passions of the soul | Taylor's "passions" renders παθήματα, what the soul undergoes or experiences: the impressions and thoughts that things produce in it. Today "passions" suggests strong emotions, which is not what Aristotle means.
!note "Signs ... first of all": spoken words signify the soul's experiences directly, and through them the things. The commentators (Ammonius, Boethius) build their whole account of meaning on this sentence: things, thoughts, spoken words, written words.

@p
@greek Ἔστι δ᾿, ὥσπερ ἐν τῇ ψυχῇ
In the soul there are sometimes thoughts that are neither true nor false, and sometimes thoughts that must be one or the other; and it is the same with speech. For falsity and truth have to do with combining and separating. So nouns and verbs by themselves are like a thought without combining or separating, such as "man" or "white" when nothing is added: they are not yet either false or true. Here is a sign of this. "Goat-stag" signifies something, but it is not yet true or false unless "is" or "is not" is added, either simply or with a time.
!note The goat-stag (τραγέλαφος) was an imaginary beast, half goat and half deer: the word has a meaning though nothing answers to it, and so it shows that meaning comes before truth.

@ch Int.2 | The noun
A noun is a sound that signifies by convention, without time, no part of which signifies on its own. "By convention" because no noun is a noun by nature: animals' cries mean something but are not nouns. "Not-man" is an indefinite noun; "of Philo", "to Philo" are cases of a noun, not nouns.

@p
A noun, then, is a spoken sound that signifies by convention, without time, and no part of which signifies anything when taken apart. In the name "Kallippos" ("Fairhorse"), the "hippos" ("horse") signifies nothing on its own, as it does in the phrase "kalos hippos" ("a fair horse"). But it is not the same with compound nouns as with simple ones. In simple nouns the part does not signify at all; in compound ones the part aims at a meaning, but it signifies nothing taken apart, as in "epaktrokelēs" ("pirate boat") the "kelēs" ("boat") signifies nothing on its own. I say "by convention" because no noun is a noun by nature, but only when it becomes a symbol. Even inarticulate noises, such as the noises of wild animals, make something plain, but none of them is a noun.
!flag a sound significant from compact | Taylor's "from compact" renders κατὰ συνθήκην, "by agreement" or "by convention": a word means what it does because people have settled on it, not by nature.
!flag For illiterate sounds also signify something | Taylor's "illiterate" renders ἀγράμματοι, sounds that cannot be written in letters: inarticulate noises like the cries of animals. It has nothing to do with being unable to read.
!note Edghill renders the two names as "Fairsteed" and "pirate-boat", to keep the point in English: in a compound name the part has no meaning of its own.

@p
@greek Τὸ δ᾿ οὐκ ἄνθρωπος οὐκ ὄνομα
"Not-man" is not a noun. There is no name laid down for what we should call it, since it is neither a sentence nor a denial. Let it be called an indefinite noun, since it applies equally to anything, both to what is and to what is not. "Of Philo", "to Philo" and the like are not nouns but cases of a noun. Their account is the same as the noun's in other respects, except that with "is", "was" or "will be" they do not say anything true or false, whereas the noun always does. "Of Philo is" or "of Philo is not", for instance, says nothing yet either true or false.
!note "Cases" (πτώσεις) are the oblique cases of a noun, the genitive "of Philo" and the dative "to Philo". In Greek, "Philo is" makes a statement, but "of Philo is" does not.

@ch Int.3 | The verb
A verb also signifies time, no part of it signifies on its own, and it always signifies something said of something else. "Is not-healthy" is an indefinite verb; "was healthy", "will be healthy" are tenses of the verb. A verb said by itself is a kind of noun: it signifies something, but not yet that anything is or is not.

@p
A verb is what also signifies time, no part of it signifies on its own, and it is always a sign of things said of something else. I say that it also signifies time: "health", for instance, is a noun, but "is healthy" is a verb, since it also signifies that the health belongs now. And it is always a sign of things said of something else, that is, of things said of a subject or in a subject. "Is not-healthy" and "is not-ill" I do not call verbs. They also signify time and always belong to something, but there is no name for this difference. Let each be called an indefinite verb, since it applies in like manner to anything, both to what is and to what is not. In the same way "was healthy" and "will be healthy" are not verbs but tenses of a verb. They differ from the verb because the verb also signifies the present time, and they the time around it.
!note "Said of a subject or in a subject" are the two ways of belonging set out in the Categories (chapter 2).

@p
@greek Αὐτὰ μὲν οὖν καθ᾿ ἑαυτὰ λεγόμενα
Verbs said by themselves are nouns and signify something: the speaker brings his thought to a halt, and the hearer rests. But they do not yet signify whether anything is or is not. For not even "to be" or "not to be" is a sign of the thing, nor is "being", if you say it bare by itself. By itself it is nothing, but it also signifies a certain combination, which cannot be thought of without the things combined.
!flag Verbs in and by themselves are substantival and have significance | Edghill's "substantival" renders ὀνόματά ἐστι, "are nouns" (or "names"). Aristotle's point is simpler than a grammar term: a verb said alone, like "walks", names something and fixes the mind on it, as a noun does.

@ch Int.4 | The sentence; only some sentences are statements
A sentence is a meaningful sound some part of which means something on its own, as an expression but not as an affirmation. Every sentence signifies by convention, but only those that are true or false are statements. A prayer is a sentence but neither true nor false: such sentences belong to rhetoric and poetry.

@p
A sentence is a spoken sound that signifies by convention, some part of which signifies something when taken apart, as an expression but not as an affirmation or denial. I mean that "man", for instance, signifies something, but not that it is or is not; it will be an affirmation or a denial if something is added. But a single syllable of "man" signifies nothing. Nor does the "ys" in "mys" ("mouse") signify anything: it is only a sound. In compound words the part does signify, but not by itself, as was said before.
!note The Greek word for man is "anthrōpos" and for mouse "mys"; Edghill renders the second "the part '-ouse' has no meaning in itself".

@p
@greek Ἔστι δὲ λόγος ἅπας
Every sentence signifies something, not as a natural tool, but, as was said, by convention. But not every sentence is a statement: only those in which there is truth or falsity. And truth and falsity are not in all of them: a prayer, for instance, is a sentence, but it is neither true nor false. Let us leave the others aside, since their study belongs more to rhetoric or poetry. The statement belongs to the present inquiry.
!flag not as being the natural means by which a physical faculty is realized | Edghill expands three Greek words, οὐχ ὡς ὄργανον, "not as a tool (or instrument)": speech does not signify by nature, as the eye sees, but by convention. Taylor has "not as an instrument".

@ch Int.5 | One statement and many; simple and compound
The first single statement is the affirmation, then the denial; others are one by being joined. Every statement needs a verb or a tense of a verb. A statement is one if it makes one thing plain, or is one by a connective. A noun or verb alone is only an expression. A simple statement says that something belongs or does not belong, in some time.

@p
The first single statement is the affirmation, and next the denial; all the others are one by a connective. Every statement must contain a verb or a tense of a verb. Even the account of man, if "is" or "was" or "will be" or something of the kind is not added, is not yet a statement. Why "two-footed land animal" is one thing and not many is another question, for it is not one because the words are said close together; to say why belongs to another inquiry. A single statement is either one that makes one thing plain, or one that is one by a connective; statements are many if they make many things plain and not one, or if they are not connected.
!note "Is one by a connective": a statement like "Socrates is wise and Plato is wise" is one only because "and" joins its parts; Aristotle counts it among the compounds.
!note The beginning of this chapter is missing in the scan of Taylor's translation as the Workshop has it; what remains of it is shown with chapters 4 and 5.

@p
@greek Τὸ μὲν οὖν ὄνομα ἢ ῥῆμα φάσις
Let a noun or a verb be called only an expression, since one cannot, by saying it alone, make something plain with one's voice in a way that makes a statement, whether someone has asked a question or one speaks of one's own accord. Of statements, one kind is simple, saying something of something or denying something of something; the other is made up of these, a kind of composite sentence. The simple statement is a spoken sound that signifies whether something belongs or does not belong, in the way the times are divided.

@ch Int.6 | Affirmation, denial, contradiction
An affirmation states something of something, a denial states something away from something. Since one can state what belongs as not belonging, and so on, every affirmation has an opposite denial, and every denial an opposite affirmation. Such a pair is a contradiction: the same thing affirmed and denied of the same thing.

@p
An affirmation is a statement of something about something; a denial is a statement of something away from something. One can state what belongs as not belonging, what does not belong as belonging, what belongs as belonging and what does not belong as not belonging, and the same with the times outside the present. So whatever anyone has affirmed can be denied, and whatever anyone has denied can be affirmed. It is clear, then, that every affirmation has a denial opposite to it, and every denial an affirmation. Let a contradiction be this: an affirmation and a denial that are opposite. I call opposite the affirmation and denial of the same thing about the same thing, not homonymously, together with whatever other such qualifications we add to meet the quibbles of the sophists.
!flag to enunciate that which is inherent as if it were not inherent | Taylor's "inherent" renders ὑπάρχον, what "belongs" to or holds of a thing. Aristotle means only that a predicate holds or does not hold; "inherent" suggests something lodged inside the thing.
!note "Not homonymously": the word must be used with the same meaning in both; the Categories opens with homonyms, things with one name and different accounts.

@ch Int.7 | Universal and individual; contraries and contradictories
Some things are universal, some individual. Universal statements made universally ("every man is white", "no man is white") are contraries and cannot both be true. "Every man is white" and "not every man is white" are contradictories: one must be true and the other false. Statements about a universal not made universally ("a man is white", "a man is not white") can both be true. Each affirmation has one denial.

@p
Of things, some are universal and some individual. By universal I mean what is of a nature to be predicated of more than one thing, and by individual what is not: "man", for instance, is a universal, and "Callias" an individual. So we must sometimes state that something belongs or does not belong to a universal, and sometimes to an individual. If one states universally of a universal that something belongs or does not, the statements will be contrary. By stating universally of a universal I mean, for instance, "every man is white" and "no man is white". When one states of universals, but not universally, the statements are not contrary, though what they make plain may sometimes be contrary. By stating of universals not universally I mean, for instance, "a man is white" and "a man is not white". "Man" is a universal, but it is not used universally in the statement. For "every" does not signify the universal, but that the subject is taken universally. To predicate the universal universally of the predicate is not true: no affirmation will be true in which a universal is predicated universally of the predicate, such as "every man is every animal".
!note "A man is white": Greek has no indefinite article, so ἔστι λευκὸς ἄνθρωπος can mean "man is white" or "a man is white". Aristotle calls such statements "indefinite" in the Prior Analytics: they leave open whether all or some are meant.

@p
@greek Ἀντικεῖσθαι μὲν οὖν κατάφασιν
I call an affirmation and a denial contradictorily opposite when one signifies the universal and the other, of the same subject, that it is not universal: "every man is white" and "not every man is white"; "no man is white" and "some man is white". I call them contrarily opposite when the affirmation and the denial are both universal: "every man is white" and "no man is white"; "every man is just" and "no man is just". So these cannot be true together, but the statements opposite to them can sometimes be true together about the same thing, such as "not every man is white" and "some man is white". Of contradictions about universals made universally, one must be true and the other false; and so of those about individuals, such as "Socrates is white" and "Socrates is not white". But of those about universals not made universally, one is not always true and the other false. For it is true at the same time to say that a man is white and that a man is not white, and that a man is noble and that a man is not noble: if he is ugly, he is not noble; and if something is coming to be noble, it is not yet. This might seem strange at first sight, because "a man is not white" seems to signify the same as "no man is white". But it does not signify the same, and they are not necessarily true or false together.
!note The four statements set out here became the "square of opposition" of the later logicians: "every" and "no" are contraries; "every" and "not every", "no" and "some", are contradictories.
!note "Noble" renders καλός, which means both beautiful and fine; the ugly man (αἰσχρός) is its opposite.

@p
@greek Φανερὸν δὲ ὅτι καὶ μία ἀπόφασις
It is clear, too, that there is one denial to one affirmation. For the denial must deny the very thing that the affirmation affirmed, and of the same subject, whether it is an individual or a universal, taken universally or not. I mean, for instance, "Socrates is white" and "Socrates is not white". If something else is denied, or the same thing of something else, it will not be the opposite denial, but a different one. To "every man is white" the denial is "not every man is white"; to "some man is white", "no man is white"; to "a man is white", "a man is not white". So we have said that one affirmation has one denial contradictorily opposite to it, and which these are; that contraries are different, and which they are; and that not every contradiction is such that one is true and the other false, and why, and when one is true and the other false.

@ch Int.8 | A statement is one when it signifies one thing of one thing
An affirmation and denial are one if they signify one thing of one thing. If one name is given to two things that do not make one, as if "cloak" meant both horse and man, then "a cloak is white" is not one statement but two, and its contradiction need not have one true and one false member.

@p
An affirmation and a denial are one if they signify one thing of one thing, whether it is about a universal taken universally or not like that: for instance, "every man is white" and "not every man is white"; "a man is white" and "a man is not white"; "no man is white" and "some man is white", if "white" signifies one thing. But if one name is laid down for two things that do not make up one thing, the affirmation is not one, nor the denial. Suppose someone gave the name "cloak" to a horse and a man: then "the cloak is white" is not one affirmation, nor is its denial one. For it is no different from saying "a horse and a man are white", and that is no different from saying "a horse is white and a man is white". If these signify many things and are many, it is clear that the first also signifies either many things or nothing, since no individual man is a horse. So with these statements too it is not necessary that one member of the contradiction be true and the other false.

@ch Int.9 | Statements about the future: the sea battle
Of what is or has been, one member of a contradiction must be true and the other false. If this held equally of individual future events, everything would happen of necessity and deliberation would be pointless. But we see that deliberating and acting are sources of what will be, and that many things can both be and not be. So it is necessary that there either will or will not be a sea battle tomorrow, but not necessary that there will be one, nor that there will not.

@p
With what is and what has come to be, then, the affirmation or the denial must be true or false. With universals taken universally, one must always be true and the other false, and also with individuals, as has been said; but with universals not taken universally this is not necessary, and this too has been discussed. But with individuals and future things it is not like that. For if every affirmation and denial is true or false, then everything must either belong or not belong. So if one person says that something will be and another denies this very thing, clearly one of them must be speaking the truth, if every affirmation and denial is true or false. For both will not hold at the same time in such cases.
!note This is the most discussed chapter of the work. The Platonists (Ammonius, Boethius) read it as saying that statements about future contingents are true or false, but not definitely so; some modern readers take it to say that they are neither true nor false yet. The modern version keeps to the Greek and leaves the question open, as the Greek does.

@p
@greek Εἰ γὰρ ἀληθὲς εἰπεῖν ὅτι λευκὸν
For if it is true to say that something is white or is not white, it must be white or not white; and if it is white or not white, it was true to affirm or to deny it. If it does not belong, one speaks falsely; and if one speaks falsely, it does not belong. So the affirmation or the denial must be true or false. Then nothing is or comes to be by chance, or as it happens, nor will be or not be, but everything is of necessity and not as it happens: for either the one who affirms or the one who denies speaks the truth. Otherwise it would be just as likely to come about as not, since what happens as it happens is no more so, or not so, now or in the future. Again, if something is white now, it was true earlier to say that it would be white, so that it was always true to say of anything that has come to be that it is or will be. And if it was always true to say that it is or will be, it cannot not be, or not be going to be. But what cannot not come to be is impossible not to come to be; and what is impossible not to come to be must come to be. So everything that will be comes to be of necessity. Nothing, then, will be as it happens or by chance; for if by chance, then not of necessity.
!flag there are no real alternatives | Edghill's phrase renders ὁπότερ᾿ ἔτυχεν, "whichever way it happens", what can turn out either way. "Real alternatives" is Edghill's gloss; the modern version keeps closer to the Greek with "as it happens".

@p
@greek Ἀλλὰ μὴν οὐδ᾿ ὡς οὐδέτερόν
Nor can we say that neither is true, that it neither will be nor will not be. First, if the affirmation is false, the denial is not true, and if the denial is false, the affirmation turns out not to be true. Besides, if it is true to say that something is white and large, both must belong to it; and if they will belong tomorrow, they will belong tomorrow. But if something neither will be nor will not be tomorrow, there would be no "as it happens": take a sea battle. It would have to be that a sea battle neither comes about tomorrow nor fails to come about.
!note The OCR of Edghill's page has lost part of this paragraph (from "though facts should prove" to "both white and large"); the modern version follows the Greek here.

@p
@greek Τὰ μὲν δὴ συμβαίνοντα ἄτοπα
These, then, are the absurd results, and others like them, if of every affirmation and denial, whether about universals taken universally or about individuals, one of the opposites must be true and the other false, and nothing happens as it happens among the things that come to be, but everything is and comes to be of necessity. Then there would be no need to deliberate or to take trouble, thinking that if we do this, that will happen, and if we do not, it will not. For nothing prevents one person from saying, ten thousand years ahead, that this will happen, and another from denying it; so that whichever of the two it was true to say then will happen of necessity. Nor does it make any difference whether anyone actually made the affirmation and the denial or not. For clearly things are so, even if one person did not affirm and another deny: it is not because of the affirming or denying that something will be or will not be, nor any more so ten thousand years ahead than at any other time. So if in all time things were such that one of them was true, it was necessary for this to come about; and each thing that came to be was always such as to come to be of necessity. For what someone has truly said will be cannot fail to come about; and of what comes about it was always true to say that it would be.

@p
@greek Εἰ δὴ ταῦτα ἀδύνατα
But these things are impossible. For we see that what will be has a source both in our deliberating and in our doing something, and that, in general, in things that are not always active there is the possibility both of being and of not being. In these both are possible, being and not being, and so also coming to be and not coming to be. Many things are plainly like this. This cloak, for instance, can be cut up, and yet it will not be cut up but will wear out first. Likewise it can also not be cut up; for it could not wear out first if it could not also not be cut up. And so with all other comings to be that are spoken of with this kind of possibility. It is clear, then, that not everything is or comes to be of necessity. Some things happen as it happens, and the affirmation is no more true than the denial; with others one way is more likely and happens for the most part, yet the other can come about instead.
!note "For the most part" (ὡς ἐπὶ τὸ πολύ) is Aristotle's phrase for what usually but not always happens, between the necessary and pure chance.

@p
@greek Τὸ μὲν οὖν εἶναι τὸ ὂν ὅταν ᾖ
That what is should be when it is, and that what is not should not be when it is not, is necessary. But it is not necessary that everything that is should be, nor that everything that is not should not be. For it is not the same to say that everything that is, is of necessity when it is, and to say that it simply is of necessity. Likewise with what is not. And the same account holds of the contradiction. Everything must either be or not be, and either be going to be or not; but one cannot divide them and say that one or the other is necessary. I mean, for instance: it is necessary that there will be a sea battle tomorrow or not, but it is not necessary that there will be a sea battle tomorrow, nor that there will not; it is necessary, though, that it either comes about or does not. So, since statements are true in the way the things are, it is clear that where things are such that they happen as it happens and admit of contraries, the contradiction must be like them. This is what happens with things that do not always exist or do not always fail to exist. With these, one member of the contradiction must be true or false, but not this one or that one: as it happens. One may be more likely true than the other, but it is not yet true or false. So it is clear that it is not necessary, of every affirmation and denial that are opposite, that one is true and the other false. For it is not the same with things that are not but can be or not be as with things that are; it is as we have said.
!flag but it cannot be either actually true or actually false | Edghill's "actually" brings in the language of actuality, which the Greek does not use here. The Greek says οὐ μέντοι ἤδη ἀληθῆ ἢ ψευδῆ, "but not yet (or not already) true or false", as Owen has it: "yet not already true nor false".

@ch Int.10 | Statements with "not" in the subject or the predicate
Every affirmation is made of a noun or indefinite noun and a verb. With "is" alone there are pairs like "man is" and "man is not", "not-man is" and "not-man is not". With "is" as a third element ("a man is just") there are four statements, and four more with "not-man" as subject. "Every" shows only that the subject is taken universally. Changing the order of noun and verb does not change the statement.

@p
An affirmation signifies something about something, and this subject is either a noun or what has no name; and what is in an affirmation must be one thing and about one thing. (The noun and what has no name were discussed before: "not-man" I do not call a noun but an indefinite noun, since an indefinite noun too signifies in a way one thing; just as "is not-healthy" is not a verb but an indefinite verb.) So every affirmation and denial will be made either of a noun and a verb or of an indefinite noun and a verb. Without a verb there is no affirmation or denial, since "is", "will be", "was", "comes to be" and the like are verbs by what was laid down: they also signify time. So the first affirmation and denial will be "a man is" and "a man is not"; then "a not-man is" and "a not-man is not"; again, "every man is" and "not every man is"; "every not-man is" and "not every not-man is". And the same account holds for the times outside the present.
!flag ‘every man is’, ‘every man is not’ | Edghill's "every man is not" renders οὐκ ἔστι πᾶς ἄνθρωπος. In today's English "every man is not" sounds like "no man is", the contrary; the Greek denial is the contradictory, "not every man is", as Aristotle explains in chapter 7.

@p
@greek Ὅταν δὲ τὸ ἔστι τρίτον
When "is" is predicated as a third element, the opposed pairs are said in two ways. I mean, for instance, "a man is just": here I say that "is" is put together as a third element, noun or verb, in the affirmation. So for this reason there will be four statements, of which two will stand to the affirmation and denial in their order of sequence as the privations do, and two will not. I mean that "is" will be added either to "just" or to "not-just", and so too with the denial; so there will be four. We understand what is meant from the table written below. "A man is just": its denial, "a man is not just". "A man is not-just": its denial, "a man is not not-just". For "is" and "is not" are added here to "just" and to "not-just". These, then, are arranged as is said in the Analytics. It is the same if the affirmation is about the noun taken universally: "every man is just", its denial "not every man is just"; "every man is not-just", its denial "not every man is not-just". But here it is not possible in like manner for those across the diagonal to be true together, though it is possible sometimes. These two pairs, then, are opposed; and there are two others, when something is added to "not-man" as a kind of subject: "a not-man is just" and "a not-man is not just"; "a not-man is not-just" and "a not-man is not not-just". There will be no more opposed pairs than these. These last will stand apart from the others, by themselves, as using "not-man" as a noun.
!note "As the privations do": "a man is not-just" stands to "a man is just" as "a man is unjust" (the privation) does. Edghill's notes discuss whether "is" here is the copula or means "there is"; the modern version keeps the copula, as the Greek's example suggests.
!note "The table written below" was a diagram in Aristotle's copy, with the four statements at the corners; the diagonal joins "a man is not just" to "a man is not-just" and "a man is just" to "a man is not not-just".

@p
@greek Ἐφ᾿ ὅσων δὲ τὸ ἔστι μὴ ἁρμόττει
Where "is" does not fit, as with "is healthy" and "walks", the verb so placed does the same as "is" would if it were attached: "every man is healthy" and "not every man is healthy"; "every not-man is healthy" and "not every not-man is healthy". For in these one must not say "not-every man": the "not", the denial, must be added to "man". For "every" does not signify the universal, but that the subject is taken universally. This is clear from these: "a man is healthy" and "a man is not healthy"; "a not-man is healthy" and "a not-man is not healthy". These differ from the others in not being universal. So "every" and "no" signify nothing further than that the affirmation or the denial is about the noun taken universally. The rest must be added in the same way.
!note The sentence about "not-every man" is hard and read in more than one way. The modern version takes it, with most readers, to mean that in "every not-man" the "not" belongs with "man" and not with "every".

@p
@greek Ἐπεὶ δὲ ἐναντία ἀπόφασίς
Since the denial contrary to "every animal is just" is the one that signifies that "no animal is just", clearly these will never be true together, nor of the same thing; but their opposites sometimes will be, such as "not every animal is just" and "some animal is just". These follow: from "every man is not-just", "no man is just"; and from "some man is just", its opposite, "not every man is not-just", since there must be some just man. It is clear too that with individuals, if it is true to deny something when asked, it is also true to affirm something. "Is Socrates wise? No." Then "Socrates is not-wise". But with universals the statement made in like manner is not true; the denial is. "Is every man wise? No." Then "every man is not-wise": this is false. But "not every man is wise" is true: this is the opposite, the other is the contrary. Opposites made with indefinite nouns and verbs, such as "not-man" and "not-just", might seem to be denials without a noun and a verb. But they are not. For a denial must always be true or false; but someone who says "not-man", unless something is added, has said nothing more true or false than someone who says "man", but rather less. "Every not-man is just" signifies the same as none of those statements; nor does its opposite, "not every not-man is just". But "every not-man is not-just" signifies the same as "no not-man is just".

@p
@greek Μετατιθέμενα δὲ τὰ ὀνόματα
Nouns and verbs signify the same when their order is changed: "man is white" and "white is man". For if this were not so, there would be more than one denial of the same affirmation. But it has been shown that one affirmation has one denial. For the denial of "man is white" is "man is not white"; and the denial of "white is man", if it is not the same as "man is white", will be either "white is not not-man" or "white is not man". But the first is the denial of "white is not-man", and the second the denial of "man is white"; so there will be two denials of one affirmation. It is clear, then, that when the noun and the verb change places, the affirmation and the denial stay the same.
!note The Greek examples, ἔστι λευκὸς ἄνθρωπος and ἔστιν ἄνθρωπος λευκός, differ only in word order, which Greek can change freely; English cannot, so the modern version, like Edghill, turns them into "man is white" and "white is man".

@ch Int.11 | When several predicates make one statement
Affirming one thing of many, or many of one, is one statement only if the many make up one thing ("two-footed tame animal"), not if they do not ("white walking man"). So a question about many is not one dialectical question. Predicates true separately are not always true combined: a man may be good and a cobbler without being a good cobbler. What is accidental, or already contained in the other, does not combine.

@p
To affirm or deny one thing of many, or many things of one, is not one affirmation or one denial unless something one is made plain by the many. I do not call it one if one name is laid down but there is not one thing made from them. Man, perhaps, is animal and two-footed and tame, and yet one thing comes from these; but from white and man and walking, not one thing. So if someone affirms one thing of these, it is not one affirmation: it is one spoken sound but many affirmations. Nor is it one if he affirms these of one thing: they are likewise many.
!note "Two-footed tame animal" was a stock definition of man in the schools; its parts make one thing, a definition, whereas "white walking man" is a heap.

@p
@greek Εἰ οὖν ἡ ἐρώτησις ἡ διαλεκτικὴ
A dialectical question is a request for an answer, either for a premise or for one part of a contradiction, and a premise is one part of one contradiction. If so, there could not be one answer to these questions, since the question is not one either, not even if it is true. This has been discussed in the Topics. At the same time it is clear that "what is it?" is not a dialectical question either. For the one questioned must be given the choice, by the question, of stating whichever part of the contradiction he wishes. The questioner must specify further, asking, for instance, whether a man is this or not this.
!note A dialectical debate, as the Topics describes it, goes by questions answered "yes" or "no". "What is a man?" cannot be answered so; "Is a man a two-footed animal?" can.

@p
@greek Ἐπεὶ δὲ τὰ μὲν κατηγορεῖται συντιθέμενα
Some things predicated separately are also predicated combined, as one whole predicate, and some are not. What is the difference? Of a man it is true to say "animal" separately and "two-footed" separately, and these as one; and "man" and "white", and these as one. But if he is a cobbler and good, he is not therefore a good cobbler. For if, because each is true, the combination must also be true, many absurd results will follow. Of a man both "man" and "white" are true, and so the whole. Again, if "white" is true, so is the whole, so that he will be a white white man, and so on without end. Again, "musical white walking": and these can be woven together many times without end. Again, if Socrates is Socrates and a man, he is Socrates the man Socrates; and if he is a man and two-footed, he is a two-footed man man. It is clear, then, that if one says simply that the combinations always come about, many absurd results follow. Let us now say how it should be laid down.

@p
@greek Τῶν δὴ κατηγορουμένων
Of things predicated, and things they happen to be predicated of, those that are said by accident, either of the same thing or one of the other, will not be one. A man, for instance, is white and musical, but "white" and "musical" are not one, since both are accidents of the same thing. Nor, even if it is true to say that the white is musical, will "musical white" be one thing; for the musical is white by accident, so "white musical" will not be one thing. This is why a cobbler is not simply good, but he is a two-footed animal, since this does not belong to him by accident. Again, things that are contained in the other do not combine either. So "white" is not repeated, and a man is not a man-animal or a two-footed man, since "animal" and "two-footed" are contained in "man".

@p
@greek Ἀληθὲς δέ ἐστιν εἰπεῖν κατὰ τοῦ τινὸς
But it is true to say something of the individual simply as well: that a particular man is a man, or a particular white man a white man. Not always, though. When something opposite is contained in what is added, from which a contradiction follows, it is not true but false: to call a dead man a man, for instance. When nothing of the kind is contained, it is true. Or rather: when it is contained, it is never true, and when it is not, it is not always true. Homer, for instance, is something, a poet. Does it follow that he is, or not? "Is" is predicated of Homer by accident: it is predicated of him because he is a poet, not in its own right. So with predications that contain no contrariety if accounts are said in place of the names, and where the predicates are said in their own right and not by accident, it will be true to say the particular thing simply too. But what is not, because it is believed in, cannot truly be said to be something; for the belief about it is not that it is, but that it is not.
!note "A dead man" contains an opposite: a man is a living animal, and "dead" contradicts "living"; so a dead man is not simply a man. Homer, long dead, is still "a poet", but it does not follow that he "is".

@ch Int.12 | The denials of "possible" and "necessary"
How are statements of the possible, the contingent, the impossible and the necessary opposed? As the denial of "to be a man" is "not to be a man", not "to be a not-man", one might think the denial of "possible to be" is "possible not to be". But the same thing can be and not be, and contradictories cannot both be true; so the denial is "not possible to be". "Possible", "necessary" and the rest are the additions that make the affirmation and the denial; "to be" and "not to be" are their subject.

@p
With these things settled, we must consider how the denials and affirmations of "possible to be" and "not possible", "contingent" and "not contingent", and of "impossible" and "necessary", are related to each other; for this has some difficulties. Of combined expressions, those are opposed to each other as contradictories that are ranged by "to be" and "not to be". The denial of "to be a man", for instance, is "not to be a man", not "to be a not-man"; and the denial of "to be a white man" is "not to be a white man", not "to be a not-white man". For if either the affirmation or the denial is true of everything, it would be true to say that a log is a not-white man. If this is so, then where "to be" is not added, what is said in place of "to be" will do the same. The denial of "a man walks" will not be "a not-man walks" but "a man does not walk", since there is no difference between saying that a man walks and that a man is walking. So if this holds everywhere, the denial of "possible to be" will be "possible not to be", and not "not possible to be".

@p
@greek Δοκεῖ δὲ τὸ αὐτὸ δύνασθαι
But the same thing seems to be able both to be and not to be. Everything that can be cut or can walk can also not walk and not be cut. The reason is that whatever is possible in this way is not always active, so that the denial will belong to it too: what can walk can also not walk, and what can be seen can also not be seen. But it is impossible for opposite statements to be true of the same thing. So "possible not to be" is not the denial of "possible to be". For it follows from this either that one affirms and denies the same thing of the same thing at the same time, or that the affirmations and denials are not made by adding "to be" and "not to be". If the first is impossible, the second must be chosen. So the denial of "possible to be" is "not possible to be". The same account holds of "contingent to be": its denial is "not contingent to be". And likewise with the others, such as "necessary" and "impossible". In the earlier cases "to be" and "not to be" were additions, and the underlying things were "white" and "man". In the same way here "to be" and "not to be" become the subject, and "can" and "is contingent" are the additions, which determine the possible and the not possible, just as "to be" and "not to be" determined the true and the false in the earlier cases.
!note "Contingent" (ἐνδεχόμενον) is what may happen, what is open to being or not being; here Aristotle uses it as another word for "possible".

@p
@greek Τοῦ δὲ δυνατὸν μὴ εἶναι ἀπόφασις
The denial of "possible not to be" is not "not possible to be" but "not possible not to be"; and the denial of "possible to be" is not "possible not to be" but "not possible to be". So "possible to be" and "possible not to be" might seem even to follow from each other: for the same thing can be and not be, since such statements are not contradictories of each other. But "possible to be" and "not possible to be" are never true of the same thing at the same time, since they are opposites; nor are "possible not to be" and "not possible not to be" ever true of the same thing at the same time. Likewise the denial of "necessary to be" is not "necessary not to be" but "not necessary to be"; and of "necessary not to be", "not necessary not to be". And the denial of "impossible to be" is not "impossible not to be" but "not impossible to be"; and of "impossible not to be", "not impossible not to be". In general, as has been said, "to be" and "not to be" must be laid down as the subjects, and one must join to "to be" and "not to be" the words that make the affirmation and the denial. These must be taken to be the opposite statements: possible, not possible; contingent, not contingent; impossible, not impossible; necessary, not necessary; true, not true.

@ch Int.13 | What follows from what: the possible, the impossible, the necessary
"Possible" brings with it "contingent", "not impossible" and "not necessary", and so on, as a table shows. The impossible follows the possible by contradiction but in reverse. The necessary needs correcting: what follows "possible to be" is "not necessary not to be". What is necessary is possible in one sense, as actual, not in the sense of what can also not be. The necessary is actual, so actuality is prior to potentiality: some things are actualities without potentiality, the first substances.

@p
The ways in which one statement follows another work out reasonably when the statements are laid down like this. From "possible to be" follows "contingent to be", and the second converts with the first; and "not impossible to be" and "not necessary to be" follow too. From "possible not to be" and "contingent not to be" follow "not necessary not to be" and "not impossible not to be". From "not possible to be" and "not contingent to be" follow "necessary not to be" and "impossible to be". From "not possible not to be" and "not contingent not to be" follow "necessary to be" and "impossible not to be". Let what we say be seen from the table. First: possible to be; contingent to be; not impossible to be; not necessary to be. Second: not possible to be; not contingent to be; impossible to be; necessary not to be. Third: possible not to be; contingent not to be; not impossible not to be; not necessary not to be. Fourth: not possible not to be; not contingent not to be; impossible not to be; necessary to be.
!note Aristotle corrects this table later in the chapter: what follows from "possible to be" is not "not necessary to be" but "not necessary not to be".

@p
@greek Τὸ μὲν οὖν ἀδύνατον καὶ οὐκ ἀδύνατον
"Impossible" and "not impossible" follow "contingent" and "possible", and "not contingent" and "not possible", as contradictories, but in reverse. The denial of "impossible" follows "possible to be", and the affirmation follows the denial: from "not possible to be" follows "impossible to be". For "impossible to be" is an affirmation, and "not impossible to be" a denial. How it is with the necessary we must look into. Clearly it is not so: here the contraries follow, and the contradictories are kept apart. For the denial of "necessary not to be" is not "not necessary to be", since both can be true of the same thing: what is necessarily not, is not necessarily. The reason why the necessary does not follow in like manner to the others is that the impossible is given in the contrary sense to the necessary, though it has the same force. For if it is impossible to be, it is necessary, not that it be, but that it not be; and if it is impossible not to be, it is necessary that it be. So if the impossible statements follow the possible and the not possible in the same way, the necessary ones follow from the contrary side, since "necessary" and "impossible" do not signify the same, but, as was said, in reverse.

@p
@greek Ἣ ἀδύνατον οὕτως κεῖσθαι
Or is it impossible to lay down the contradictories of the necessary like this? For what is necessary to be is possible to be. Otherwise the denial would follow, since one must either affirm or deny; so if it is not possible to be, it is impossible to be; so what is necessary to be would be impossible to be, which is absurd. Yet from "possible to be" follows "not impossible to be", and from that, "not necessary to be"; so it turns out that what is necessary to be is not necessary to be, which is absurd. But neither does "necessary to be" follow from "possible to be", nor does "necessary not to be". For with the possible both can happen, but if either of these is true, both will no longer be possible. For the same thing is possible both to be and not to be; but if it is necessary that it be, or that it not be, both will not be possible. It remains, then, that "not necessary not to be" follows from "possible to be". For this is true also of what is necessary to be. And it becomes the contradictory of what follows "not possible to be"; for from that follow "impossible to be" and "necessary not to be", whose denial is "not necessary not to be". So these contradictories too follow in the way described, and nothing impossible results when they are laid down so.

@p
@greek Ἀπορήσειε δ᾿ ἄν τις
One might ask whether "possible to be" follows from "necessary to be". If it does not, its contradictory, "not possible to be", follows; and if someone says that this is not the contradictory, he must say "possible not to be"; but both of these are false of what is necessary to be. Yet again, the same thing seems to be capable both of being cut and of not being cut, both of being and of not being, so that what is necessary to be would be contingently not to be; and this is false. It is clear, then, that not everything that is capable of being or of walking is capable of the opposites too. There are things of which this is not true: first, things capable not by reason, as fire can heat and has a capacity without reason. The capacities that go with reason are capacities for more than one thing, for contraries; but not all the capacities without reason are. As was said, fire cannot both heat and not heat, nor can anything else that is always active. Some, though, even of the capacities without reason can take on opposites at the same time. But this has been said to make the point that not every capacity is for opposites, not even all those said to be of the same kind.
!note A doctor's art, a capacity with reason, can cure or harm; fire, a capacity without reason, can only heat. Aristotle works this out in Metaphysics IX (Θ), chapters 2 and 5.

@p
@greek Ἔνιαι δὲ δυνάμεις ὁμώνυμοί
Some capacities are homonymous, for "possible" is not said in one way only. In one sense it is said because it is true, as being actual: something is possible to walk because it is walking, and in general something is possible to be because it already actually is what it is said to be possible to be. In the other sense, because it might be active: something is possible to walk because it might walk. This second capacity belongs only to things that can change; the first belongs also to things that cannot. Of both it is true to say that it is not impossible to walk, or to be: both of what is already walking and active, and of what is able to walk. To say "possible" in the second sense of what is necessary without qualification is not true; in the first sense it is true. So, since the universal follows the particular, being able to be follows what is of necessity, though not in every sense. And perhaps the necessary and the not necessary are the first principle of everything's being or not being, and the rest must be considered as following from these.

@p
@greek Φανερὸν δὴ ἐκ τῶν εἰρημένων ὅτι τὸ ἐξ ἀνάγκης
It is clear from what has been said that what is of necessity is actual. So if the eternal things are prior, actuality too is prior to potentiality. Some things are actualities without potentiality, such as the first substances; others are actual with potentiality, and these are prior by nature but later in time; and others are never actualities, but only potentialities.
!flag Some things are actualities without potentiality, namely, the primary substances | "Primary substances" are, in the Categories (chapter 5), individual things like this man or this horse. Here αἱ πρῶται οὐσίαι are the first substances, the eternal and unchanging movers of Metaphysics XII, which are always actual. Taylor's "the first essences" keeps them apart.
!flag are energies without capacity | Taylor's "energies" and "capacity" render ἐνέργεια and δύναμις, actuality and potentiality. "Energy" today means a physical quantity, which is not Aristotle's sense; the modern version says "actuality" and "potentiality".
!note The Platonists made much of this sentence: the commentators (Ammonius) read the first substances, always actual, as the divine causes, and saw here Aristotle's agreement with Plato that the first principles are prior to all becoming.

@ch Int.14 | Is the contrary of a statement its denial or its contrary affirmation?
Is "every man is just" contrary to "no man is just" or to "every man is unjust"? Spoken statements follow beliefs, so ask: is the belief that the good is not good, or that it is bad, contrary to the true belief that it is good? Error is most about what a thing is in itself, and the denial is about that; so the belief in the denial is the contrary. So the universal denial is the contrary of the affirmation. True beliefs and statements are never contrary.

@p
Is the affirmation contrary to the denial, or the affirmation to another affirmation? Is the sentence "every man is just" contrary to "no man is just", or "every man is just" to "every man is unjust"? Take, for instance, "Callias is just", "Callias is not just", "Callias is unjust": which of these are contrary? If spoken sounds follow what is in thought, and in thought the contrary belief is the belief in the contrary, as "every man is just" to "every man is unjust", then it must be likewise with the affirmations in speech. But if in thought the belief in the contrary is not the contrary belief, then the affirmation will not be contrary to the affirmation, but the denial we spoke of will be. So we must consider which false belief is contrary to a true belief: the belief in the denial, or the belief that the contrary is so.
!note Edghill's text joins the end of chapter 13 and the start of chapter 14 in one paragraph; in the Greek this is where chapter 14 begins.

@p
@greek Λέγω δὲ ὧδε.
I mean this. There is a true belief about the good, that it is good; another, false, that it is not good; and another, that it is bad. Which of these is contrary to the true one? And if they are one, by which is it contrary? To think that contrary beliefs are defined by being about contraries is false. For the belief about the good, that it is good, and about the bad, that it is bad, may well be the same, and true, whether they are one or more. Yet these things are contraries. But beliefs are not contrary by being about contraries; rather by being contrary to each other. Now there is a belief about the good, that it is good, and another, that it is not good; and there is something else, which does not and cannot belong to it. We must not take any of the other beliefs as the contrary, neither those that believe that what does not belong belongs, nor those that believe that what belongs does not belong; for both are without limit, both those believing that what does not belong belongs and those believing that what belongs does not. We must take those in which there is deception. And these are the ones from which comings to be start; and comings to be are from opposites, and so are deceptions.
!note Things come to be from their opposites (the hot from the cold, Physics I); so error, Aristotle says, lies between opposites too, not among the endless things a thing is not.

@p
@greek Εἰ οὖν τὸ ἀγαθὸν καὶ ἀγαθὸν
Now the good is both good and not bad: the first in itself, the second by accident, since it happens to it not to be bad. And if the true belief about each thing is more true when it is about what the thing is in itself, the false belief is also more false when it is about that. Now the belief that the good is not good is false about what belongs to it in itself, and the belief that it is bad is false about what belongs to it by accident. So the belief in the denial of the good would be more false than the belief in the contrary. And the person most deceived about each thing is the one with the contrary belief, since contraries are the things that differ most within the same kind. If, then, one of the two is the contrary, and the belief in the contradictory is more contrary, it is clear that this would be the contrary. The belief that the good is bad is compound, for presumably the same person must also suppose that it is not good. Further, if it must be likewise in other cases, this too would seem to be right; for the contradictory is the contrary either everywhere or nowhere. And where there is no contrary, the false belief is the one opposite to the true: someone who thinks that a man is not a man is deceived. If, then, these are contraries, so are the other beliefs in the contradictory.

@p
@greek Ἔτι ὁμοίως ἔχει ἡ τοῦ ἁγαθοῦ
Again, the belief about the good that it is good stands like the belief about the not good that it is not good; and, besides these, the belief about the good that it is not good stands like the belief about the not good that it is good. What, then, would be contrary to the true belief about the not good, that it is not good? Not the belief that says it is bad: for that might be true at the same time, and a true belief is never contrary to a true one; some things that are not good are bad, so both can be true together. Nor the belief that it is not bad: for this too is true, and these might hold at the same time. It remains, then, that the contrary of the belief about the not good that it is not good is the belief about the not good that it is good; for this is false. And so the belief about the good that it is not good is contrary to the belief about the good that it is good.

@p
@greek Φανερὸν δὲ ὅτι οὐδὲν διοίσει
It is clear that it will make no difference if we lay down the affirmation universally: the universal denial will be the contrary. To the belief that everything good is good, for instance, the contrary is the belief that nothing good is good. For the belief about the good that it is good, if the good is taken universally, is the same as the belief that whatever is good is good; and this is no different from the belief that everything good is good. Likewise with the not good. So if this is how it is with belief, and spoken affirmations and denials are symbols of what is in the soul, it is clear that the contrary of an affirmation is the universal denial about the same thing. To "everything good is good", or "every man is good", the contrary is "nothing good is good", or "no man is good"; the contradictory is "not everything good is good", or "not every man is good". It is clear too that neither a true belief nor a true denial can be contrary to a true one. For contraries are about opposites; and about these the same person can speak truly; but contraries cannot belong to the same thing at the same time.
!note The last chapter returns to the first: spoken words are symbols of what is in the soul, so the logic of statements follows the logic of beliefs.
