@treatise Prologue | Proclus' introduction to the dialogue
Proclus opens his lectures on the dialogue the school read first. Every being has its own good, fitted to what it is, so we must know what we are before we can know our good: knowing ourselves is the starting point of all philosophy, as the Delphic "Know yourself" teaches. The First Alcibiades is where Plato makes this his aim, so it comes first. Proclus then sets out what the dialogue is for, how it answers to the parts of the universe, why Iamblichus put it first of the ten dialogues, and how it divides: not by its manner of speech, nor into ten arguments, but, with Iamblichus, into three parts, every argument leading to self-knowledge.

@ch Pr.1 | Each being has its own good
Every kind of being has its own perfection, matched to the rank of its being: the good is given out by a fitting equality, more to the higher, less to the lower. So the good of intellect is in eternity, that of soul in time, that of body in being as nature made it.
@taylor 2 3

@p
@greek Τῶν Πλατωνικῶν διαλόγων
We hold that the most authoritative and most secure starting point of Plato's dialogues, and, one may say, of all philosophical study, is to discern what our own being is. Once this is rightly laid down, we shall be able to learn far more exactly both the good that belongs to us and the evil that fights against it. For just as each of the things that are differs in its being, so too, by nature, it differs in its perfection: one perfection for some, another for others, as their being falls lower.
!flag of the whole theory of that philosopher | The Greek says "of all philosophical study, so to speak"; it is not Plato's theory alone.
!flag and this according to a subjection of essence | The Greek word (hyphesis) means a lowering, a falling off: perfection differs as being falls away from the first. Taylor's "subjection" means this, in an old sense.

@p
@greek Εἴτε γὰρ ἀπὸ τῆς αὐτῆς
Suppose that being and the good come forth from the same hearth and the very first source, as Aristotle says. Then, surely, what is intellective in perfection must come to each thing by the measures of its being. Or suppose the good comes from one cause, older and holier, and being and existence come to things from another. Even so, as each thing shares in being more dimly or more clearly, so too it will share in the good: the things that are first, in a greater and more complete good; those set in the middle, in a second good, by their rank; and the last of the things that are, in the last. How else could things be, when gods and providence rule them, than with the good given out to each as it deserves? Intellect, surely, does not lead the things that are into order and give each the measure of being that fits it, while the good, which is older even than intellect, gives itself to things and channels its goods to them without order: giving the same goodness to things that differ in being, causes and their effects, say, or handing out to things that are the same in being the perfections of both the higher and the lower. For, as Timaeus says, it was not and is not right for the best to do anything but what is most beautiful and best proportioned. But what is best proportioned is not the same good for what is first and for what is second, nor, for things that are like one another, a perfection that differs in being. Rather, as the Athenian Stranger says, the most musical and best of all is the equality that gives unequal shares to unequal things: greater to the greater, less to the less.
!note Timaeus' words are in the Timaeus (30a), which has only "the most beautiful"; "and best proportioned" is Proclus' own. The Athenian Stranger speaks of this equality in the Laws (book 6, 757b-c).
!note "What is intellective in perfection" renders the Greek as printed (τὸ νοερὸν τῆς τελειότητος), as Cousin's Latin does ("id quod intellectuale est perfectionis"); the phrase is hard, and Taylor leaves it out.
!flag it is certainly necessary that perfection should be imparted to every thing according to the measures of essence | The Greek has "what is intellective in perfection" coming to each thing; Taylor leaves out the word. The modern version keeps it, and a note says it is hard.
!flag a cause more ancient and more characterized by unity | The Greek says "older and holier" (hagiōteras); "more characterized by unity" is Taylor's reading.
!flag For, how otherwise can things participate of deity and providence, and a distribution according to their desert? | The Greek says that, since gods and providence rule, things can be no other way than with the good given out to each as it deserves; things do not "participate of deity" here.
!flag but that the good, or the ineffable principle of things, which is more ancient than intellect | "Or the ineffable principle of things" is Taylor's; the Greek says only "the good, which is older than intellect".
!flag a distribution of inequality to things unequal, and of equality to things equal | The Greek has only the equality that gives unequal shares to unequal things; "and of equality to things equal" is Taylor's.

@p
@greek Κατὰ ταύτην δὴ οὖν
By this equality, then, the good too is different in different things, and the good in each thing is by nature matched to its nature. So the perfection of intellect is in eternity, and that of soul in time; the good of soul lies in living by intellect, and the good of body in being as nature made it. And again, the perfection of gods is one thing, that of angels and daemons another, and that of particular souls another. Whoever thinks that, where the being of these differs, their perfection is the same, misses the truth about the things that are. For, as Homer says, "never alike is the race" of gods and of men, nor of the kinds between them and the two extremes.
!note Homer's words are from the Iliad (5.441), where Apollo warns Diomedes that the race of the immortal gods and that of men who walk the earth are never alike.
!flag but of the rational soul in time | The Greek says "of soul", not "the rational soul"; and "the good of soul" is "according to intellect", with no word for Taylor's "energy".

@ch Pr.2 | Know a thing's being before its good
Perfection belongs to the being that shares in it, so in every rank we must know the being first: without parts or divided, eternal or in time, simple or composite. So the first principle of all philosophy is a pure knowledge of ourselves, held by reasoning from the cause.
@taylor 4

@p
@greek Εἰ δὴ ταῦτα ὀρθῶς
If this is rightly understood, then in every rank of the things that are we must know the being before the perfection. For perfection does not belong to itself; it belongs to the being that shares in it. So we must look first at the being: whether it is one of the beings without parts, or of those divided among bodies, or of those set in the middle; whether it is one of the eternal things, or of those that exist through all of time, or of those that come to be in some part of time; and whether it is one of the simple things, set up before all putting together, or of the composite things that are always held together by bonds that cannot be undone, or of those that can be broken up again into the things they were put together from. If we look at each thing in this way, we shall be able to understand its good as well, of whatever kind it is.
!flag such as intellectual natures, or to such as are divisible about bodies, viz. corporeal forms and qualities | The examples (intellectual natures; bodily forms and qualities) are Taylor's; the Greek names only the kinds of being.

@p
@greek Δῆλον γὰρ αὖ ὅτι
For again it is plain that, for those whose lot is a being without parts, the good is eternal; for those whose being is divided, the good too is by nature in time and in motion; and for those in between, perfection too is seen by the measures of their existence: it needs time, but the first time, which can measure the periods of things without body. So we may count this as the most authoritative starting point of all philosophy and of Plato's work, as we said: a pure, unalloyed knowledge of ourselves, marked out in the bounds of knowledge and firmly tied down by reasoning from the cause.
!note "Tied down by reasoning from the cause" recalls the Meno (98a), where true opinions become knowledge when they are tied down by reasoning out the cause.
!flag is to be considered according to the measures of subsistence and perfection | The Greek says that the perfection of these is seen "by the measures of their existence"; perfection is what is measured, not one of the measures.

@ch Pr.3 | The Delphic "Know yourself"
We must begin where the god at Delphi bids us. As the uninitiated were kept out of the inner shrine at Eleusis, "Know yourself" shows the way up to the divine: whoever knows himself can be joined to Apollo. Socrates too began philosophy from the inscription.
@taylor 4

@p
@greek Καὶ πόθεν γὰρ ἄλλοθεν
Where else should we begin our own purification and perfection, if not where the god at Delphi bade us? Those who went into the precinct of the goddesses at Eleusis found a notice telling them not to pass into the inner shrine if they were uninitiated and not yet perfected in the rites. In the same way, I think, the words KNOW YOURSELF, written up before the temple at Delphi, showed the way of being led up to the divine and the most effective road to purification. They all but say plainly, to those able to understand, that whoever has known himself, beginning from the hearth, can be joined to the god who reveals the whole truth and leads the life of purification; but whoever does not know who he is, being uninitiated and unperfected, is not fit to share in the providence of Apollo.
!note "Beginning from the hearth" is a Greek saying for beginning at the very start, at home: here, from oneself.
!flag if they are uninitiated in the highest of the mysteries | The Greek says only "uninitiated and unperfected"; "in the highest of the mysteries" is Taylor's.
!flag as being uninitiated both in the lesser and greater mysteries | Again Taylor's gloss: the Greek has "being unperfected and uninitiated", with no lesser and greater mysteries.

@p
@greek Αὕτη τοίνυν ἔστω
Let this, then, be the starting point of philosophy and of Plato's teaching: the knowledge of ourselves. For it is fitting, I think, that Apollo's companion should begin the perfecting of the imperfect from the very place where the god himself bids us begin. Socrates, too, who says he is a fellow servant of the swans, and who had the gift of prophecy from the god no less than they, is said to have begun his drive towards philosophy here, when he came on the Pythian inscription and took it to be a command of Apollo himself.
!note The Workshop takes "Apollo's companion" to be Plato, whose teaching this is; the next sentence turns to Socrates.
!note Socrates calls himself a fellow servant of the swans, sacred to Apollo, in the Phaedo (85b); he speaks of the Delphic inscription in the Phaedrus (229e).

@ch Pr.4 | Why the First Alcibiades comes first
No dialogue makes the study of our being its aim as this one does, so the reading of Plato begins here. Nothing is nearer to us than ourselves; we are all, like Alcibiades, ignorant of ourselves, and need the same help.
@taylor 4

@p
@greek Ἐντεῦθεν τοίνυν καὶ ἡμῖν
From here, then, we too must begin, following the god. And we must ask in which of the dialogues above all Plato has this as his aim, the study of our being, so that we may make it the very first starting point of Plato's writings too. Could we name any other before the Alcibiades, and the conversation of Socrates handed down in it? Where else shall we say that our being, what it is, is shown in this way? Where else are man and the nature of man looked into? Where is the Delphic inscription, whatever it means, fully tested? Or how could we look into anything else among the things there are, whether things that are or things that come to be, before these, when we have heard Socrates himself say: "It seems strange to me to look into other things while I do not know myself"? For nothing is nearer to us than ourselves. If we do not know what is nearest, what way is there to know what is further off, and what is by nature known through us?
!note Socrates says this in the Phaedrus (229e-230a), where Plato's words are "it seems absurd to me" (geloion); Cousin prints "strange" (alloion), and the modern version follows the Greek shown.

@p
@greek Εἰ δὲ δὴ μὴ ταῦτα
And if you consider not only that these things are worked out at length in this dialogue, but also that this is the first conversation Socrates begins with Alcibiades, and that it is he who says that the beginnings of perfection hang on the study of ourselves, you would no longer doubt that everyone eager to be perfected must begin here.

@p
@greek Καὶ γὰρ ἡμῶν ἕκαστος
For each of us, and of other people too, more plainly or more dimly, is subject to the same troubles as the son of Clinias. We do not know ourselves, held down by the forgetfulness that comes with birth into this world, and knocked off course by the turmoil of the unreasoning forms of life. And we think we know many things we do not know, because of the rational principles that are in us by our being. And we need the same help, so that we may stand back from our excessive conceit and find the care that is right for us.

@ch Pr.5 | The aim of the dialogue
Others gave the dialogue other aims; Proclus answers that its aim is what is most complete in it and runs through all of it: knowing our being. Exhortation, refutation, midwifery, praise and blame are the means, without which it cannot be reached, as purifications come before the mysteries.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Ἀλλ’ ὅτι μὲν προηγεῖσθαι
That knowing oneself must come before every other inquiry, and before all philosophical study, so to speak, has now been said. But someone may reproach us for so readily setting this down as the aim of the Alcibiades, since many other famous interpreters have written accounts of it, some giving it one purpose, some another. All the same, I should be ashamed of myself if, instead of what is most complete in the dialogue, which needs the greater knowledge and is the bound that takes in all its problems, namely what shows our being, what it is, and runs through all its main heads, I made the things that come second, less complete and more particular, the aims of the work before us.

@p
@greek Οὔτε γὰρ ἐπὶ μόνον
For we should not refer the dialogue's purpose to Alcibiades alone, as some have supposed: scientific study looks to what is common, and to what reaches to every like state. Nor should we put forward the instruments of dialectic, such as exhortation, midwifery or refutation, as the aims of the conversation; we should rather see to what end these too are directed. Nor should we leave the matters before us and carry the inquiry over to other beings, divine or daemonic, that have nothing to do with us (for the aim must be proper to the first foundation of philosophical study, since Socrates also says that he now approaches the young man for the first time). Nor, letting this aim go, should we contrive some other purpose for the dialogue, made up from outside it, when Socrates himself bears witness to this one.
!note Midwifery is Socrates' art of helping another bring his thoughts to birth, as he describes it in the Theaetetus (149a-151d).

@p
@greek Εἰ γὰρ παντὸς ἄλλου
If knowing ourselves must come before every other inquiry, what end of the first conversation could be more to the aim than to know ourselves? So exhortations and dissuasions, refutations and midwifery, praise and blame, count among the things without which the aim is not reached. Without them one cannot know oneself. We need exhortation towards what is really good, and dissuasion from what is really bad; midwifery to bring forth the reasons in us that are not twisted, and refutation to purify us of the double ignorance; praise to win over those being perfected, and blame to help them and cure them where they act badly.
!note "Things without which" is Plato's way of naming what a cause needs but is not the cause, in the Phaedo (99b).
!note The double ignorance is not knowing, and not knowing that one does not know; one of Taylor's footnotes explains it so.

@p
@greek Ὥσπερ οὖν ἐν ταῖς
As in the mysteries purifications come first, with sprinklings and cleansings, which are exercises for what is done in secret and for sharing in the divine, so, I think, the philosophical rite of perfection purifies and prepares beforehand those who set out towards it for the knowledge of themselves and for seeing our being as it shows itself.

@ch Pr.6 | What comes before the aim, what follows, and the dialogue as a universe
Some things in the dialogue lead to its aim, others follow from it; the aim is to know ourselves, and the good it brings is to become like the divine. Each dialogue has what the universe has: here likeness to God answers to the Good, self-knowledge to intellect, the arguments to soul, the style to form, the persons and setting to matter.
@taylor 6 8

@p
@greek Ὥστε σκοπὸς μὲν οὗτός
So this, and no other, is the aim of the dialogue. Of the things shown in it, some come before this purpose and others follow it, like conclusions tied to this end. Before it come the putting away of the double ignorance, the exhortation, and the like; after it come the showing of what virtue and happiness are, the condemnation of the many arts as knowing neither themselves nor what is theirs, nor anything at all, and whatever else of this kind is built up together at the end of the dialogue. But the most final and most central thing in the whole conversation is the study of our being. Indeed, if someone says that the end of the dialogue is the care of ourselves and the knowledge of it, he puts it rightly; but let him note that this belongs to us as the end, as the good, from what is proved, while the problem sought, for whose sake all the arguments are made, is to know ourselves.
!flag So that he will not err who establishes the care and knowledge of ourselves, as the end of the dialogue. | The Greek goes on to tell the end apart from the problem sought: the care of ourselves is the end, the good that comes from what is proved, while what the arguments seek is to know ourselves. Taylor stops before the distinction.

@p
@greek Ἄλλο γὰρ ἦν τὸν σκοπὸν
For the aim of the dialogue, to know, was one thing; the good, the one that comes from such a purpose, was another. As we have said elsewhere about the dialogues, each must have what the universe has: in each, something is set as answering to the Good, something to intellect, something to soul, something to form, and something to the underlying nature itself. Let us say, then, that here being made like the divine through care of ourselves answers to the Good; knowing ourselves, to intellect; the many proofs that lead us to this, the conclusion and, so to speak, all the reasoning in the dialogue, to soul; the character of the style, the weaving together of figures and forms of speech, and whatever else belongs to the power of expression, to form; and to matter, the persons, the occasion, and what some call the setting (hypothesis).
!note The first sentence is hard in the Greek. The Workshop reads it as the distinction just made: the aim is knowing ourselves, and the good is what that knowledge brings.
!flag and that which is called by rhetoricians the hypothesis, to matter | The Greek says "what some call the hypothesis" (para tisin); "by rhetoricians" is Taylor's.

@p
@greek Ταῦτα μὲν οὖν ἐν παντὶ
So much is in every dialogue. As for this one: the aim of this work is to know our being, and we are lovers of this knowledge so that we may reach our own perfection. And as among the causes themselves intellect hangs from the Good, so here what the dialogue seeks is yoked to the end of the conversation; and the end is what we have said.

@ch Pr.7 | The beginning of all philosophy
As knowing ourselves is the start of philosophy, this dialogue holds the seed of all of it: logic, ethics, the study of nature and of the divine. That is why Iamblichus set it first of the ten dialogues that hold Plato's whole philosophy.
@taylor 5

@p
@greek Ταῦτα καὶ περὶ τῆς προθέσεως
Let this be our account of the dialogue's purpose, now that we have shown first that one must begin one's own perfecting here, from a pure knowledge of oneself. This dialogue is the beginning of all philosophy, just as knowing ourselves is. That is why many logical theorems are scattered through it and handed down in it; many ethical ones, which help towards our whole inquiry into happiness, are cleared up; and many teachings that guide us towards the study of nature and the truth about the divine things themselves are sketched in outline. So, as it were, in this dialogue the common, single and whole outline of all philosophy is held, coming to light through our very first turning towards ourselves. And this, I think, is why the divine Iamblichus gives it the first place among the ten dialogues in which he thinks the whole philosophy of Plato is contained, the whole course of those dialogues being taken up beforehand in this one, as in a seed. Which the ten are, how they should be ordered, and how they are gathered up in the two that come after them, we have treated in its own place elsewhere.
!note Iamblichus' ten dialogues, and the two after them, the Timaeus and the Parmenides, are the curriculum the Workshop's Library follows (the Anonymous Prolegomena, ch. 26).
!flag It likewise contains information with respect to many things which contribute to physiology | "Physiology" is Taylor's word for the study of nature (physiologia), not the science of the body.

@ch Pr.8 | How others divided the dialogue
Some divided the dialogue by its manner of speech, into praise, refutation, exhortation and midwifery; others, more seriously, into ten arguments. Proclus praises the second, but finds both still busy with instruments rather than the matters themselves.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Ἀλλ’ ἐπειδὴ καὶ ταῦτα
Now that this too has been set out well enough, we must speak next of how the dialogue is divided. Some cut it by its manner of speaking, and have divided it into praise, refutation, exhortation and midwifery. The philosopher, they say, needs praise to win the young man over; refutation, to purify him of his excessive conceit; exhortation, to rouse him to share in virtue; dissuasion, to draw him away from becoming like the popular leaders, and from envy of those who have great power in the city; and midwifery, so that he may call him to the study of human nature and of the care that befits such a nature. They do say this when they divide the dialogue, but not as fully as could be done. For they spend their time on what is third from the truth, labour over the last things and lay hold of the instruments, but fall short of setting out the matters themselves.
!note "Third from the truth" is Plato's phrase for the imitation of an imitation, in the Republic (book 10, 597e).

@p
@greek Ἕτεροι δὲ αὖ τούτων
Others, more serious than these, let go of how the manner of speaking is divided, and go up to the reasoning and proving in the dialogue, and from there think fit to make the division. They count, first, an argument showing that Alcibiades does not know what is just; second, one declaring that the many are not good teachers of what is just; third, one concluding that, where there is question and answer, the one who speaks is the one who answers, not the one who asks; fourth, one in which it is shown that persuading many and persuading one are the work of the same knowledge; fifth, one reasoning that what is just is advantageous; sixth, another tied to it, which concludes that only the beautiful is good; seventh, one establishing that Alcibiades, in the double ignorance, does not know himself or the matters at hand; eighth, one proving that he has not even looked into who his real rivals are; ninth, one that shows him wholly ignorant of the way to take right care; and tenth, besides these, one in which our being is cleared up, with its three ways of being known and the kind of treatment that fits it.
!note The three ways are whether a human being is the soul, the body, or the two together (Alcibiades 129e-130c).

@p
@greek Οὐκοῦν καὶ τοὺς ταῦτα
We shall praise those who say this, then, as more refined than those before them and closer to the matters; but we shall not say that even they make the division as the matters require. They stand in the second rank, and have not wholly left off busying themselves with the instruments.

@ch Pr.9 | Iamblichus' division into three parts
The best division is Iamblichus', from the matters themselves: first, taking away the ignorance of reason; second, showing that natural gifts are not enough without complete virtue; third, recalling our true being and finding the right care. All the rest serves these three.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Ἐκεῖνος δὴ οὖν ὁ τρόπος
The way of dividing the dialogue that seems to me the most complete of all is the one the philosopher Iamblichus also approved. It starts from the matters themselves, gathers the whole plan of the work into three main heads, and refers to them both the methods of reasoning and the handling of the speech. For what comes second, and serves as an instrument, must always keep hold of the first and chief parts, and be handed down with its eye on them. How, then, do we say the dialogue is divided into its nearest and chief parts?

@p
@greek Πῶς δὲ ἄλλως ἢ
How else than this? Its aim is to bring to light the being of the human being, and to turn each of us towards himself, away from the drive that looks outward and from busying ourselves with what is not ours. This must come about as we purify our reason from what stands in its way and cuts off its turning, and train what is without reason and call it back to the perfection that accords with reason. So one main head comes first: the one that takes away the ignorance of reason, and clears away the obstacles to knowledge that arise in reason through birth into this world, with many arguments of every kind. Second after it is set the part of the dialogue which establishes that one must not be content with natural advantages and fall short of the practices that belong to complete virtue. Third after these is the part that gives the recollection of our true being, and the finding of the right care, and brings the fitting end to the whole purpose of the discussion. These, then, are the three parts of this dialogue. All the rest is taken up for their sake and helps towards them, both the proofs and what belongs to the expression.

@ch Pr.10 | Every argument leads to knowing ourselves
Proclus goes through the ten arguments and shows that each leads to self-knowledge: from our ignorance of what is just, through the soul's self-motion and learning as recollection, to the last, which shows that the human being is the soul.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Καὶ γὰρ τῶν συλλογισμῶν
For each of the arguments will be seen to lead us up to this one end: the study of what we are, and the knowledge of ourselves. If you will look at each on its own and examine the force it has, you will find that all of them go back to this one starting point. The first, which shows the ignorance of what is just that we all have from youth, blames nothing other than our own lack of knowledge. For though we have the rational principles of these things in our being, we do not turn to ourselves, nor take up a clear, articulated knowledge of them. This is the cause of our conceit that we know, and of the refutations we run into because of that conceit.

@p
@greek Ὁ δὲ δεύτερος ἀτιμάζων
The second, which dishonours the great crowd of people as no judge of what is just, draws us away from the whole multitude and from the life carried along by opinions, and leads each of us up to the one reason and the one knowledge, which exist in the soul itself by its being. The third, which shows that the one who answers is the one who speaks, makes plain the soul's power to move itself, shows that bringing forth reasons, and learning, are recollections, and does nothing but turn the one being perfected towards himself. The fourth, in which he concluded that persuading one and many belongs to the same knowledge, shows what the complete form of activity is, which does not stand outside itself but is turned back towards itself, as intellect is. For it fills everything that is less than itself, and is present alike to one and to many, neither divided among those that share in it nor altered by them, but staying one and the same in itself, and from itself perfecting the others. The fifth, after this, which proves that what is just is advantageous, places the advantage of each person in the soul, where justice also is, and persuades us to look for our being nowhere but in the soul, where the good also exists together with the just. For our advantage is not in one place and our existence in another.
!note The soul as what moves itself is from the Phaedrus (245c-e); learning as recollection, from the Meno (81c-d) and the Phaedo (72e-73b).
!note At "it fills everything that is less than itself", Cousin notes that both his manuscripts read differently; the modern version follows his printed text.

@p
@greek Ὁ δὲ ἕκτος, ὁ τὸ καλὸν
The sixth, which brings the beautiful and the good together into one, draws the soul away from the beauties that appear, leads it up to the beauty that belongs to intellect, to reason and to knowledge, and prepares it to turn towards itself and see these things in itself, not in what lies outside it. The seventh, which purifies away, with the double ignorance, everything that blocks our knowing ourselves and keeps us from turning towards ourselves, shows, by driving out the excessive conceit, that the greatest of evils is this: not knowing ourselves. Along with it comes to light the greatest and most complete of goods, which has its existence in knowing ourselves. The eighth argument shows him who his real rivals are. And this too helps towards the whole aim, since we do not know ourselves, whether in the power of our soul, or of our body, or in what we have outside us.

@p
@greek Ἵν’ οὖν μὴ παντελῶς
So that he may not, wholly ignorant of himself and of how he falls short of his rivals in everything, fail to notice that he has fallen into the greatest misfortunes, this argument too, I think, is necessary to the whole purpose of the dialogue.

@p
@greek Ὁ δὲ ἔννατος
The ninth, in which he is shown not to know the way to take care of ourselves, makes plain that the most authoritative starting point of this knowledge too is to discern our being. For on this hangs the judging of the right care; or rather, it is marked out by this very knowledge. For everything that moves itself perfects itself by turning towards itself. Whoever has known himself has also grasped, within himself, the care of himself; whoever does not know himself is without knowledge of the way of care too. Last of all, the tenth, which shows that the human being has his existence in the soul, brings to light at once the form of our being, and gives us the most complete knowledge of the life that acts of itself, taking together in one proof both our existence and our perfection. If, then, we say that each of the arguments in the dialogue, and all of them together, lead us round to knowing ourselves, we should perhaps hit on Plato's meaning.
!note The tenth argument is in the Alcibiades (129b-130e): the human being is the soul, so "Know yourself" bids us know the soul.

@treatise 103a | On the opening words: what the preface shows
Proclus comments on the dialogue's first words, "Son of Cleinias, I think you wonder that I, who was the first to love you, am the only one who does not leave you now the others have stopped." First he reads their wording. The preface is not there for show: it turns Alcibiades to himself, and through himself to Socrates' knowledge, as the whole dialogue will. Socrates says "I think" because he speaks of a changing thing, and because the young man is proud; he calls him by his father's name because the boy loves that name, because the father stands for the true father of souls, and because love comes from the Father. Of Socrates' three sciences, dialectic, midwifery and love, it is love that leads here.

@ch 103a.1 | The preface serves the aim of the dialogue
Plato's prefaces are neither ornament nor mere history: like the rites of a mystery, all their parts serve the one aim of the dialogue. Here the very first words show that aim, self-knowledge, by turning Alcibiades to himself and making him want to learn why Socrates alone still loves him.

@ch 103a.2 | Three kinds of turning
Everything that turns, turns to what is worse, to itself, or to what is better. The turn to the worse is the fall of a soul that has lost its wings; the turns to itself and to the better are found in the gods too, as the Parmenides shows. So the end of the dialogue says that whoever turns to himself will see the divine; and the preface already turns Alcibiades to himself, and from himself to love of Socrates' knowledge.

@greek Ἔστι μὲν οὖν τὰ εἴδη

@ch 103a.3 | Why Socrates says "I think"
A good man fits his knowledge to what he knows: intellect for what is intelligible, reasoning for the middle things, the senses for things sensed, conjecture for images, as the Republic divides them; the Timaeus says the same of accounts, and the Cratylus of names. Alcibiades' state of mind is a changing thing, so Socrates rightly says "I think": the word marks the changing object, not doubtful knowledge.
@taylor add001

@p
@greek Δεύτερον δὲ ἕτερον ἐπὶ
A second thing follows from this. A good man always brings to bear on each object of knowledge the knowledge matched to it. About things that can be otherwise and are carried now this way, now that, he does not claim a knowledge that is fixed, defined and unchanging; and about things that are necessary and always the same, he does not hold an opinion that is doubtful, undefined and unsteady. Rather, as Socrates himself divided them in the Republic, he looks at all intelligible things together by simple, single and intellective knowledge; he examines the middle accounts of the things that are by the scientific and reasoning power that unfolds the forms and puts them together; third from the truth, by perception, which touches its own objects through instruments, he judges all things sensed as is fitting; and lastly, by the power of conjecture, he knows the objects of conjecture, which are images of things sensed.
!note The four powers are those of the divided line in the Republic (511d-e): intellect (noēsis), reasoning (dianoia), trust in the senses (pistis) and conjecture (eikasia).

He says that the accounts of what truly is cannot be refuted and are fixed, while those of things not of that nature, but carried along in becoming, are likely and have a share of chance, as he says somewhere. The word marks the knowable thing as easily turned; it does not convict Socrates' knowledge of being undefined, mixed with ignorance and open to dispute. It is not right, says Aristotle, to accept a geometer speaking persuasively, or an orator claiming to demonstrate. So neither a man who speaks of necessary things by conjecture, nor one who speaks of things conjectured as if they were fixed, would ever be a sensible interpreter of things. This is why Socrates had to add "I think".

@treatise 103a-love | On love: its orders, and the two kinds of lover
Having said that the opening words show Socrates as a lover, Proclus turns from the wording to the matter: what love is. As other gods have their own powers, the whole order of love turns all things to divine beauty; it is the middle between beauty and those it leads up, which is why the Symposium calls Love a great daemon. Its ranks run from the hidden summit among the first gods down through the gods before the world to the many loves in the world: angels, daemons, heroes and the souls of men. Souls that choose the life of love come down to care for the well-born and lead them up with themselves; souls that fall from love's gift through bad upbringing chase the images of beauty and sink into matter. Then, from Socrates' own first words, Proclus draws five marks that part the divine lover from the vulgar one, and says why Socrates still calls the others lovers.

@ch 103a.8 | Love turns all things to divine beauty
As each kind of god has its own power, making, giving life, guarding or saving, so the whole order of love is the cause that turns all things to divine beauty, leading the lower up to it and filling them from it. It stands in the middle, between beauty and those it leads up, which is why the Symposium calls Love a great daemon: love holds among the gods the middle place that daemons hold between the divine and the mortal.

Some help by prophecy, some by the rites, some by divine medicine; souls that choose the life of love are moved by the god who watches over beautiful boys to care for the well-born, and from visible beauty they lead themselves and their beloved up to beauty itself.

And indeed the souls of men too have a share in such inspiration. Through their kinship with the god they are moved about the beautiful, and they come down into the place of becoming to do good to less perfect souls, in providence for those who need saving. For gods, and the gods' followers, stay in their own abodes and do good to all that is second to them, and turn it to themselves; but the souls of men, coming down and touching becoming, copy the good-like providence of the gods.

@ch 103a.11 | True love and its counterfeit
Souls possessed by love use visible beauty as a clean vehicle to be carried round to intelligible beauty, and light the way for others. Souls that fall from love's gift through bad upbringing, but are lovers by nature, take the images of beauty for beauty: they admire bodies, fall away from the divine into the darkness of matter, and, thinking they hasten to union, are carried off into scattering and the sea of unlikeness. Visible beauty, mixed with matter, can never be pure.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Αἱ μὲν οὖν κάτοχοι τῷ ἔρωτι
Souls possessed by love, then, and sharing the inspiration from there, use visible beauty as an undefiled vehicle and are carried round to intelligible beauty, and they set that up as the end of their activity. Kindling a light for the less perfect souls, they lead them all up too to the divine, and dance with them round the one source of all beauty.

@p
@greek αἱ δὲ τῆς ἐκεῖθεν δόσεως
But the souls that have fallen from the gift that comes from there, through bad upbringing, though they were given a lover's nature, fall upon the images of beautiful things through ignorance of the true ones. They make much of the beauties that are in matter and divided, and are struck with wonder at them, not knowing what has happened to them. @ch 103a.12 | Five marks of the two lovers, from Socrates' first words
Love's gift taken in badly makes the tyrannical, unbridled life, as Plotinus says an outflow of intellect makes cunning. Socrates' first words then show five marks of the true lover against the vulgar one: he gives wonder instead of feeling it; he watches the beloved from infancy and comes when the bloom is gone; he stays the same; he keeps apart until the time is right; and he is the one first and only lover, where the others are many.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Ἐπειδὴ τοίνυν ἀπ’ ἀλλήλων
So we have told apart the many forms of the orders of love, and the souls that share in it truly from those that turn aside the gift that comes from there. (For "an outflow of intellect makes cunning", says Plotinus, and a share of wisdom gone wrong makes the sophistry here below; so too love's light, when it finds a bad receiver, produces the tyrannical, unbridled life.) Since, then, we have told these apart, let us go through Plato's very words and look at the differences between the lovers, which Socrates sketches for us by his first words; let us consider who is the divine lover and what he gives the beloved, and who the common lover is, who follows the multitude in the soul.
!note Plotinus' saying is given as Proclus quotes it; the modern version does not name the place in the Enneads.

@p
@greek Ὁ μὲν τοίνυν δημώδης
The common and vulgar lover wonders at the beloved, pursues him, and is all aflutter about him; the true lover gives the beloved cause for wonder, and stirs him in every way to amazement at the life within him. And this is fitting. For the worse lover owns that he is the beloved's subject, and says he needs him; the other is sufficient in himself, full of power, and promises to give his own goods to the souls of those he loves.
!note Socrates' first words are "Son of Cleinias, I think you wonder" (103a): it is the beloved who wonders.

@p
@greek Δεύτερον τοίνυν
Second, the inspired lover watches over the beloved from his swaddling clothes, and comes to him when his bloom has ended, when the bodily tumults have ceased; for he is not a lover of the body, but of the soul, and when the body's flower is over, the beauty of the soul shines out. But the common lover reaches for the beloved's prime as for ripe fruit, and takes no trouble over him before or after: as soon as the bloom ends he leaves the boy, as befits his own principles. Being a lover of the body, he measures his love by the prime of visible beauty.
!note So the dialogue itself (131c-d): the lover of Alcibiades' body goes away when it stops flowering; the lover of his soul stays, "as the body's bloom ends".

@p
@greek Τὸ τρίτον τοίνυν
Third, the one is steadfast and always the same, since he is bound to something steadfast; the other changes easily, and while there is vulgar, but quick to tip and ready to leave the boy. For his friendship for what is in matter makes him like what he loves, and so he too, surely, becomes unstable, and readily changes his intimacy with the boy.
!note Pausanias' speech in the Symposium (183e): the vulgar lover "is not steadfast, since he loves nothing steadfast; as soon as the bloom of the body he loved ends, he flies away".
!note "Like what he loves": Cousin prints τῷ ἐραστῷ, "what is lovable" to him, that is the body; one of his manuscripts (A) has τῷ ἐραστῇ, which would make the beloved "like his lover".

@p
@greek Τέταρτον στοιχεῖον
Let the fourth mark of the difference between the men be this: the one, even when present with the beloved, is in a way apart from him, neither touching him nor talking with him, nor speaking to him at all, until the time for helping his soul has come; the other loves to be joined to him through the senses, and becomes a nuisance, ever changing, and inventing every kind of pretext to talk to him.
!note Socrates says at 103a that the other lovers "were a nuisance to you, talking with you", while he "did not even speak to you" in all these years.
!note "In a way apart from him": Cousin prints κεχώρισται, "is apart"; his manuscripts read κεχάρισται, "does him a kindness", and he refers to his annotations.

@p
@greek Πέμπτον ἀπ’ αὐτῶν
A fifth you could take from the very words: the divine lover is called one, first and only, as being akin to the one and the good, and as stretching up to the simple, one-formed pattern of beautiful things; the vulgar lover is many, and heaped together at random. For in calling the lovers of images "others", but the inspired friend "first and only", Socrates shows up their life as unbounded, divided and scattered every way, and honours his virtue as godlike, far from the multitude in matter, and set above all the passions of becoming.
!note At 103a Socrates calls himself "the first of your lovers", who "alone" does not leave, "the others having stopped".

@ch 103a.13 | Why Socrates still calls the others lovers
Much else parts the two kinds of lover. Socrates still lets the many be called lovers because it is not yet settled what a man truly is, and where Alcibiades himself is to be placed; once that is settled, Socrates will be seen as his only lover, and the others as lovers of what is Alcibiades', not of Alcibiades, wearing an image of love about an image of beauty. Each order of love goes with its own beauty, and the eyes of the many cannot bear to look at the truth, as the Eleatic Stranger says.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Πολλὰ δ’ ἂν καὶ ἄλλα
One could say much else, stirred by these words, about what separates these lovers from one another.

@p
@greek Ἐραστὰς γὰρ οὐκ ἀποδοκιμάζει
Socrates does not yet refuse to call the many lovers too, because it has not yet been settled what the true man is, and where Alcibiades himself is to be placed. @treatise 103a-first | Why Socrates loved first, and stays: the lover as a good daemon
Proclus turns to Socrates' own words, that he was the first of the lovers and alone stays when the rest have stopped. As the One is at work before, with and after all things, the divine lover cares for the beloved before the vulgar lovers, alongside them, and after they have gone, and speaks only when the crowd has scattered. Proclus compares the scene to the mysteries, where earthly daemons appear and distract before the god shows himself, and sets Socrates in the place of Alcibiades' good daemon: watching from childhood, silent, speaking only when the soul is ready. Read another way, Socrates is to Alcibiades what intellect is to the soul, and the vulgar lovers what the many-headed beast is; the two readings join, since the same being is a daemon to a man and intellect to a soul. Then he asks why Alcibiades wonders (the many measure care by time, not by perfection), why the vulgar lovers still bear the name of lover, and how Socrates, from his first words, shows himself the only lover.

@ch 103a.14 | First, with, and after the others, as the One is
Socrates begins before the other lovers, keeps on with them, and stays when they stop. So the more perfect embraces the work of its images, and is present to anything before the lower; so the One is at work before all things, with them and after them. The divine lover alone is called "first", while the many lovers are given the rank of the divided multitude; he waits until they have tired, and comes when the beloved is calm and fit to share his company.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Ταῦτα μὲν οὖν περὶ τούτων
So much for this. That Socrates begins before the others, carries his care for the young man along with theirs, and does not leave when the others have stopped, shows, I think, through such a life, that the higher patterns and the more perfect everywhere take in the activities of their images in every way, and that the lower things are present to nothing to which the more perfect have not given themselves long before. It shows too that in every order of the things that are, the One has taken in all the rest, and is at work before them, with them and after them.

@p
@greek Ἄντικρυς οὖν
So the text plainly gave the inspired friend the eminence proper to the One, calling him alone and first to care for the less perfect, and gave the many lovers the rank of the multitude that is divided in every way. And as there the One is at work before the others, with the others and after the others, if it is right to say so, so the divine lover attends to the beloved before the vulgar lovers, and while they are with him does not stand back from caring for him. It is then above all that he takes thought, lest the boy give himself up unawares to the image of love that lives in matter and belongs to the crowd. @ch 103a.15 | An image from the mysteries
Everywhere what is less perfect slips in ahead of the more perfect and draws unready souls away. In philosophy, the life of the sophist pulls the less perfect from the sight of what is to appearance; in the holiest rites, earthly daemons rush in before the god appears, so the gods bid us not look at them until the rites have armed us, as the Oracles say. So the vulgar lovers, like earthly daemons, draw the young man away from his ascent; when they are gone, the inspired lover appears, as the god does when the rites have cleansed away the daemons that drag souls down.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Καὶ εἴ με δεῖ τοὐμὸν λέγειν
And if I must say what I think, this too seems to me to give a wonderful image of what is done in the mystery rites. For everywhere the less perfect slips in ahead of the more perfect, draws round to itself the souls that are not yet set right, and pulls them away from what is better. So, just as in the intellective sights of philosophy the life of the sophist is a hindrance, and keeping company with it drags the less perfect away from the contemplation of the things that are to the appearance that goes with what comes to be and passes away, so too, in the ascent to divine love, the crowd of vulgar lovers stands in the way: it slips in under the guise of the true lover, and drags the young man's soul down from the Olympian goods above to what is earthly and dark.

@greek Κατὰ τὰ αὐτὰ δὴ καὶ ἐν ταῖς ἁγιωτάταις
In the same way, in the holiest of the rites, before the god's presence, onrushes and apparitions of certain earthly daemons appear, throwing those being initiated into confusion, tearing them away from the undefiled goods and calling them forth into matter. That is why the gods bid us not look at those daemons before we have been fenced about by the powers that come from the rites: "For you must not look on them before your body is initiated." And for this reason the Oracles add that they always charm souls and lead them away from the rites. Like the earthly daemons, then, are the vulgar lovers, who flow round the young man and lead him away from the ascent to the divine, and make him unfit for the company of the inspired friend. So, as there the divine shows itself after the cleansing away of every such daemonic thing that leads souls down into matter, so too the inspired lover appears after the flight of the many lovers bound to becoming, and gives the beloved a share in his company.

!note "Fenced about by the powers that come from the rites": the Greek (φραχθῶμεν) is "be fenced, armed".

@ch 103a.16 | Socrates in the place of a good daemon
Socrates holds towards Alcibiades the place of a good daemon. The daemon watches over us from youth, begins its care before our parents, stays with us through life as the pilot of the whole of it, and after death leads us to our judges, as the Phaedo says; so Socrates comes before all Alcibiades' friends, stays alongside the many lovers, and when they stop, calls him to justice. As the daemon is with us unseen and speaks only to those most ready, so Socrates follows the boy in silence and speaks when he sees his life loosened from matter and free of the crowd.

@p
@greek Ἀτεχνῶς οὖν μοι δοκεῖ
So it seems to me that Socrates simply holds the place of a good daemon towards Alcibiades: like it, he watches him from youth, guards him, oversees all his activities, and looks on his words and deeds. And as the daemon begins its care for us even before our parents, stands over us with our parents as the pilot, so to speak, of our whole life, and after our release from here takes thought for our journey to our judges ("for", he says, "it leads each one, the daemon whose lot he was while living"), in the same way Socrates is ahead of all the young man's kin and friends in caring for him, adds his own concern alongside many lovers, and when the others have stopped, he himself visits and keeps company with him, and calls his beloved back to justice as a whole and to the goods that truly belong to the soul.

@ch 103a.17 | Wonder, the start of philosophy
Socrates comes at the moment when the beloved is most awake to receive him, and says so: "You wonder what it is with me, and how I stay when the other lovers have fled." Wonder is the start of philosophy, as the Theaetetus says, which is why Iris is called the daughter of Thaumas; so release from the vulgar lovers and the flutter towards philosophy run together, for souls cleansed of the evils of becoming are made ready for the divine goods.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Πρόσεισιν οὖν ὁ Σωκράτης
So Socrates comes at this moment, when he saw the beloved most awake to receive his company; and surely he shows this himself, for "you wonder", he says, "what my case is, and how I stay when the other lovers have fled". This wondering is the beginning of philosophy, as he says himself in the Theaetetus; and for this reason, in the ancient story, Iris is called the daughter of Thaumas, Wonder. If, then, he comes to the young man just when the young man wonders at the philosopher's life and seeks the causes of what goes on in it, the young man would have the start of his impulse towards philosophy. And release from the vulgar lovers and the flutter of excitement about philosophy somehow run together: for souls purified of the evils that pour round them from becoming make themselves ready to share in the divine goods.
!note The Theaetetus (155d): "this feeling, wondering, is very much a philosopher's; there is no other beginning of philosophy than this, and whoever said Iris was the child of Thaumas did not get the genealogy wrong."
!note "What my case is, and how I stay": Proclus gives the sense of 103a-b in his own words.

@ch 103a.18 | Alcibiades between Socrates and the vulgar lovers
Socrates, as an inspired lover led up to intelligible beauty, stands to Alcibiades as intellect to the soul; the vulgar lovers, carried along by sense and imagination, are like the soul's many-headed beast. Alcibiades is the rational soul in between, torn from below by the passions as the Titans tore, held up from above by intellect as by Athena, the saviour. And as intellect is always at work in us but shows itself only when the soul has put off the crowd that grew on it from becoming, so Socrates is always present but speaks only when the lovers have gone.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Σκόπει δὴ καὶ κατὰ τὴν ἀναλογίαν
Consider too, by this analogy, what rank Alcibiades has towards his lovers, and how this also helps the study before us. Socrates, as an inspired lover led up to intelligible beauty itself, set himself in the place answering to the intellect of the soul (for what else is joined to the intelligible but intellect, and all that has an intellective life?); while the motley, vulgar tribe of lovers, swept along with sense and imagination and given over to the many-headed beast of the soul, copies the life that answers to it, the life by which it marks its own life too (for every soul is that part by which it lives, and has defined itself by it). Alcibiades is somehow between them: torn from below by the lovers ruled by passion, and dragged down to the worse lot; helped from above by Socrates, who is one, saved by him, and watched over, lest he suffer something at the hands of the lovers flowing round him. We must, then, by the analogy of the extremes, place Alcibiades too in the rational soul. On it the passions and the irrational powers still hang, plotting, as it were, against the rational life and trying to tear it in pieces like Titans; above it is set intellect, holding it up, as Athena does, from its leaning and its slide into matter. For it is Athena's to keep life undivided, for which Pallas Athena was called the saviour; and it is the Titans' to divide it, and call it forth into becoming.
!note The many-headed beast is the Republic's image of the appetites (588c-e).
!note Tearing like Titans recalls the story, told by the Orphics and read by the Neoplatonists, of the Titans who tore the child Dionysus to pieces; Proclus does not tell it here.

@p
@greek Ὥσπερ τοίνυν ὁ νοῦς οὐκ ἀεὶ
So, just as intellect does not always show itself to souls, but only when they put off the great crowd that grew on them later from becoming, as Timaeus says, so Socrates gives the young man a share in his company when he has become free of the many lovers who also later ranged themselves round him, and has leisure for philosophy and what can lead to it. And as intellect is always at work about us and always gives the light of thought, both before we sink into unreason, and while we live with the passions, and when the passions in us have been put to sleep, yet we are not always aware of it, but only when we are clean of the great surge of becoming and bring our life to anchor in a kind of calm (for then intellect shows itself to us and, as it were, speaks to us; then the one who was silent before, and present in quiet, gives us a share in its voice): so too the divine lover is present to the beloved before the many lovers, with them and after them, but silent, taming him, and only watching over him; and when they have stopped, he gives the beloved a share in speech, offers him a common life, shows him who he is, and that his love is provident, like the good, and leads upward, not like that of those many lovers, divided, needy, in matter and a thing of images.
!note The Timaeus (42c): the soul overcomes "the great crowd that later grew on it, from fire and water and air and earth".

@ch 103a.19 | Two ways of seeing it: daemon and intellect
The lover of such sights may go by either analogy: Socrates as the good daemon, the vulgar lovers as earthly daemons of matter, the young man as one being initiated who flees the daemons that drag him down; or Socrates as intellect. The two join: Alcibiades is both a soul and a soul using a body. As soul he has Socrates for his intellect, as soul using a body for his good daemon. The Symposium sets the daemonic between gods and men; the Timaeus sets soul between intellect and body. Since the dialogue asks what man is, Socrates will not speak of intellect but remind the young man of his daemon, calling himself now a daemon, now a god.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Ἔστι μὲν οὖν καὶ διὰ ταύτης
So the one who loves the sight of such things can travel by this analogy; and he can go by the one we spoke of before, likening Socrates to a good daemon, the vulgar lovers to earthly daemons of matter, and the young man to one being initiated, who runs from the tribe of daemons that drag down, brings himself to the good daemon, and hands it the whole steering of his life.

@p
@greek Εἰ δὲ δεῖ καὶ συνάπτειν
And if we must join both approaches, think of Alcibiades as twofold: as a soul simply, and as a soul using a body. These are not the same, just as the pilot and the man by himself are not the same, nor the charioteer and the person underneath the role; and in general what is not shared in differs from what is shared in, what is by itself from what is seen together with another, and what stands apart from what has been given a place beside something else. Since, then, Alcibiades is thought of in two ways, as a soul and as a soul using a body, in so far as he is a soul Socrates keeps towards him the place of intellect; in so far as he is a soul using a body, the place of the good daemon. And the same being is a daemon in relation to a man, and intellect in relation to a soul. For when we think of the daemonic middle, we see it between gods and men; when of intellect, we fasten soul to its highest point, and the body to soul. You have the one division in the Symposium, where he says somewhere that the daemonic is a middle between gods and men; and the other in the Timaeus, for soul stands in the middle between intellect and body. The same being, then, is daemon and intellect: intellect of the soul, daemon of the human soul. That is why here, since the inquiry is about the nature of man, there will be no mention of intellect, but Socrates will remind the young man of the providence of the daemon, calling himself sometimes a daemon, sometimes a god.

!note The Symposium (202e): "everything daemonic is between god and mortal". The Timaeus (30b): the maker put "intellect in soul, and soul in body"; soul is in the middle.

@ch 103a.20 | Why Alcibiades wonders: time, not perfection
Why does Alcibiades wonder at Socrates' long care? Less perfect people measure the efforts and activities of souls by the length of time, not by how perfect the activity is, as the many call a man skilled because he spent long with teachers. So Alcibiades wonders that Socrates has loved him longer than the others, and Socrates, knowing the young man's nature, guesses his wonder, though he himself began first, kept his love steadfast, and is only now beginning to show himself.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Ἀλλὰ τούτου μὲν ἡ αἰτία
But the cause of this will be told more clearly later. Let us go back to the start and ask what is the reason that Alcibiades wonders at Socrates' zeal for him, his constant attendance, and the lasting of his love when the others have stopped, and how Socrates guesses what the young man is thinking. We say that the less perfect among men are used to measuring the efforts and activities of souls by the length of time, and not by the perfection of the activity. Or have we not noticed that the many call the man who spent longer with teachers skilled or knowing in whatever he happened to spend that time on?

@p
@greek Καίτοι θαυμαστὸν οὐδὲν
Yet it is no wonder that the better-natured needs less time and work to be made perfect. Still, those who cannot judge the form itself, by itself, and the settled state, think that time has been given them by nature as a sufficient measure. Since, then, the many are used to judging each thing in this way, it was likely that Alcibiades too would wonder what Socrates' case was, who had spent longer in love for him than the other lovers; and that Socrates, knowing the youthfulness of his nature, would have guessed the wonder in him; even if he himself, for other reasons, gave himself first to caring for him, kept his lover's character steadfast, and has not even now ever left off his love. On the contrary, it is only now that he begins to make himself plain to his beloved.

@ch 103a.21 | Why the vulgar lovers are still called lovers
The licentious man never claims the name of temperate, nor the coward that of brave; yet vulgar lovers, who drag the beloved down, want the name of lover that the inspired lovers have. Proclus gives three reasons: all lovers share one end, kinship with the beautiful, and the lowest beauty too bears the name of beauty; all lovers are mad, though one madness is above self-control and the other has fallen below it; and what is more divine, by its overflowing power, gives even its fallings-away an image of itself, so the image shares the pattern's name.
@taylor none

@treatise 103a-secret | The more secret account of love: faith, truth and love
Proclus goes back to love and gives the more secret account of it. Love is neither among the first of things, since the beloved is beyond it, nor among the last, since whatever loves shares in it: it stands between, second to the beautiful and first of the rest. Among the hidden intelligible gods there are three causes, the good, the wise and the beautiful, and from them come three monads, faith, truth and love, which go forth through every order of gods, each order receiving them in its own way. Love comes down from the intelligibles to the things in the world, turning all things to divine beauty; the Chaldean Oracles say that all things are governed and exist in these three, and bid the theurgists join themselves to the god through them. Where things are divided, love is the middle that binds them and leads the lower up; and the divine lover imitates his god, while the vulgar lover does the opposite and drags souls down into matter.

@treatise 103a-crowd | "In crowds", and not a word: the providence that stands apart
Proclus comments on Socrates' next words: "and that the others came to talk to you in crowds, while I in so many years have not even spoken to you." Divine and daemonic providence has two marks: it reaches down to the last things, leaving nothing without its care, and it takes in nothing of what it governs, staying unmixed. Plato gives both to Socrates: wakeful about the beloved, yet untouched by him; present and not present. So it is with the gods, who love what they beget, and whose love is the cause of love in human souls. Socrates begins with silence because the unspeakable comes before speech among the gods, and love first came to be in their silent order. The others came "in crowds": a crowd is a multitude without order or bond, the image of a life scattered among the passions. Alcibiades' great praise is that he let them come no nearer than talk, and so, as the Athenian Stranger trains the young in drinking with a sober ruler present, Socrates watched over him as judge until he had mastered them all, and then called him to himself.

@ch 103a.26 | The two marks of providence, and Socrates' care

@ch 103a.27 | So too the gods, and their love for what they beget
If this is so with divine men, still more is it so with the gods and the good daemons: present to all and apart from all, filling all and mixed with none. When the myths say Zeus loved Kore, or Aphrodite herself, they mean a love that provides, saves, perfects and holds together, without ties and undefiled. Everything good in souls has its cause in the gods, as Plato puts the patterns of virtues and bodily goods there; so the first cause of love, given by divine gift, must be among the gods too. Higher gods love lower ones by providing for them, lower ones love higher by turning to them.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Εἰ δὴ ταῦτα τοῦτον
If this is how things are with divine men, what must we say of the gods themselves, or of the good daemons? Are they not, while present to all, set apart from all? Though they have filled all things with themselves, are they not unmixed with all, and, passing everywhere, have they not ranked their own life nowhere? And what should we say of the gods who are said to love their own offspring, as the makers of myths make Zeus love Kore or Aphrodite herself? Is it not that such love provides for its beloved, saves them, perfects them and holds them together? Is it not that it is free of ties, unmixed, good in form and undefiled? @ch 103a.29 | What a crowd is
A crowd is a multitude, but one without bounds, confused and without order: not like a chorus, nor like a people bound to itself, which is why mob rule differs from democracy. To come in crowds is the mark of a scattered life that drags the beloved down to the varied passions, as the Timaeus calls all unreason a crowd. So the others were divided among themselves, and filled the boy with the same vice, doing the opposite of love, which, as Agathon says, empties of estrangement and fills with kinship.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Τίς οὖν ὁ ὄχλος ἐστίν
What, then, is the crowd? That it is a multitude is plain to all; but a multitude without bounds, a confused multitude, a multitude with no order. It is not like a chorus, nor like a people. A people is a multitude bound together with itself; a crowd is a multitude torn apart. That is why, among constitutions, they say that mob rule differs from democracy: the one is disorderly and out of tune, the other ordered by the laws. It is clear, then, that coming "in crowds" is the mark of a life swept along and confused, which drags the beloved down to the material, scattered and many-limbed kind of variety that the passions have. For the Timaeus too called all unreason a crowd, as being without bounds of itself, out of tune and disorderly: "the great crowd that grew on later, out of fire and earth and air and water". So, just as "the others" showed their division and their torn-apart life, so too coming "in crowds" shows the abuse that came from them upon the young man, and that they were drawing him down to a divided and material kind of life. Not only, then, were they divided and at odds with one another in this way; they also filled the young man with the same vice, doing the very opposite of what is the power of love. For love "empties us of estrangement and fills us with kinship", as Plato's Agathon says; but these were full of division and discord even among themselves, being "others", and they held out this estrangement of theirs to the beloved too; for to everyone what crowds him is foreign by nature and painful. Such, then, is the lover of the many.

@treatise 103a-daemon | "Not human, but a daemonic opposition": Socrates' daemon and love
Proclus comments on the next words: "The cause of this was nothing human, but a daemonic opposition, whose power you will learn later too." Socrates was already wonderful to the young man for his constancy in love and his care free of ties; now he lays the cause of his silence on the daemon, so that he seems not only a good man but one who acts by a daemon, and stirs the greater amazement that leads a proud nature to philosophy, as the rites amaze before they show. Only the silence is laid to the daemon, because caring for the lower belongs to souls as souls, but caring without taking anything in belongs to gods and good daemons. Then Proclus shows why the account of the daemon belongs with the account of love: love is itself a great daemon, the middle that binds; it has its first, hidden being in the intelligible intellect, as the Chaldean Oracles and the Orphic verses say, and three ranks, in gods, in daemons and in souls, answering to the three kinds of intellect. So Socrates, lover and daemonic man at once, begins his speech with love and ends it with the daemon.

As, then, in the holiest of the rites some awe-striking things go before what is done, some through what is said and some through what is shown, bending the soul down before the divine, so too at the porch of philosophy the guide stirs up wonder and amazement about himself in the young man, so that the arguments, as they go forward, may work on him and call him out to the philosophic life. This must be done above all with those full of pride and boasting. For such pride is right towards the many, but towards serious men it hinders the benefit to be had from them. So, lest Alcibiades, having mastered the many lovers, should think he will likewise look down on Socrates, Socrates straight from the opening shows himself wonderful to him by his intensity, his watchfulness, and his life by a daemon. Of the two things he has told him, that he has loved him longer than all those many and varied lovers, and that till now he went on keeping silent towards him, he refers the cause of the second to the daemonic. "The cause of this", he says, "of not even speaking, is a daemonic opposition."
!note "His watchfulness" (σκοπή): Socrates' watching of the boy, which he goes on to describe at 103b ("I have observed, watching you in this time, how you have treated your lovers").

@ch 103a.32 | Why only the silence is laid to the daemon
The daemon is surely the cause of Socrates' love too, for he could not have lived the lover's life so well without a daemon's choosing. But loving the boy and caring for him is the work of providence for the less perfect, which souls have as souls, since souls came down to care for the mortal; to govern without ties, taking nothing in, belongs to gods and good daemons, and to human souls only by a divine or daemonic gift. So it is the care that stands apart, shown by the silence, that he lays to the daemon.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Καίτοι καὶ τοῦ ἔρωτος
And yet the daemon was surely the cause of his love too, for he would not have followed the lover's life so excellently unless he had made both the choice of it and the zeal for it by a daemon. But since loving the young man and caring for him is the work of the providence that has to do with the less perfect, while watching over him without ties, unmixed and apart, is more daemonic than the human life allows, for this alone he referred the cause to the daemonic. To perfect the worse and provide for the less perfect belongs to souls too, as souls: their very descent came about for the sake of providence for things in becoming and care for the mortal. But to take nothing in from what is governed, not to be mixed with the worse, but to set it in order without ties, belongs to gods and to good daemons; and when it belongs to human souls too, it belongs to them by some divine or daemonic gift. Of this, then, he made the daemonic the cause: of the care about him that stands apart. For watchfulness is a token of not leaning outward, of holding back the activities that are carried outside, and of a life with no ties to the worse.

@ch 103a.33 | Love and the daemon belong together: love is a great daemon
The lover and the daemonic man are the same: the inspired lover enjoys a daemon next above him and through it is joined to the gods, since goods come from gods to men through daemons, as Diotima says; and the daemonic man is joined to his daemon by love, the god the Oracles call "the binder of all". Love is itself a great daemon, filling the middle between the beloved and the lover. Among the hidden intelligible gods it unites intellect with the first beauty; outside them it shines an unbreakable bond, as the Oracles' "bonding fire"; and it joins gods to the intelligible beauty, daemons to gods, and us to both, above being in gods, in being in daemons, by shining-in in souls.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Καὶ μὴν καὶ ἡ μνήμη
And indeed the mention of the daemon is in tune with the being and power of love. For the lover and the daemonic man are the same. The lover, if he is inspired, enjoys the daemon next above him, and through it is joined to the gods, and receives the very breath of inspiration through the daemon as a middle; for the goods from the gods come to men through the daemons in the middle, as Diotima's account says. And, again, the daemonic man is a lover of some kind: or how does he enjoy the daemonic, unless he has joined himself to it and shared his life with its powers? And what brings about this bond of the lesser with the stronger, if not love? For the Oracles call this god "the binder of all, who mounts upon all"; and not the binder of some and not of others. So it is he who joins us too to the oversight of the daemons.
!note Diotima's account is in the Symposium (202e-203a): through the daemonic "all the intercourse and conversation of gods with men" takes place. The Oracles are the Chaldean Oracles.
!note "Through the daemon": manuscript A has "through the daemonic" (δαιμονίου).

@p
@greek Ἔτι καὶ αὐτὸς ὁ ἔρως
Moreover love itself is a great daemon, as Diotima says, in so far as it everywhere fills the middle between the beloved and those who hasten towards it through love. Among the intelligible and hidden gods it unites the intelligible intellect to the first and hidden beauty by a kind of life stronger than understanding; which is why the theologian of the Greeks calls that love eyeless: "Shepherding in his heart swift, eyeless Love". Outside the intelligibles it shines an unbreakable bond on what it perfects. For a bond is a union, but with more division; and so the Oracles have called the fire of this love "bonding": "Who first leapt forth from intellect, clothing fire in fire, the bonding fire". Going forth from the intelligible intellect, it binds all that comes second both to one another and to that. So it joins all the gods to the intelligible beauty, the daemons to the gods, and us to both daemons and gods: in the gods it stands first, in the daemons second, in particular souls by a kind of third procession from the sources; in the gods above being, for all the race of gods is such, in daemons by their being, in souls by shining-in.

@ch 103a.34 | Love's three ranks, like the three intellects
The three ranks of love answer to three kinds of intellect, as the Timaeus shows them: the unparticipated intellect, apart from all particular kinds; the participated intellect, which the souls of the gods share; and the intellect that comes to be in souls as their perfection. Love in gods answers to the first, love in daemons to the second, love by shining-in in souls to the third. Love's first, hidden being is in the intelligible intellect, from which it leapt forth; and Plato, finding in Orpheus the same god called love and a great daemon, took up the same hymn.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Καὶ ἔοικε τριπλῆ τάξις
And this threefold order seems to answer to the threefold power of intellect. For one intellect is the unparticipated, set apart from all particular kinds; another is the participated, which the souls of the gods share, as being stronger; another is the one that comes from this to be in souls, which is the perfection of the souls themselves. And you have these three meanings of intellect in the Timaeus. To the unparticipated intellect, then, we must take love among the gods as analogous, for it too is set apart from all that it possesses and lights up; to the participated intellect, love among daemons, for it too is in being and complete in itself, as the participated intellect is, and stands close above souls; and to the intellect that is a settled state, clearly, the third, which is in souls by shining-in.
!note "Stands close above souls": Cousin prints ἐπιβατέων, a form of ἐπιβατεύω, "to stand upon, ride upon", the verb Proclus uses a few pages on of the daemons who stand next above the parts of the world (ἐπιβατεύουσι, Cousin's p. 186).
!note "One intellect": both manuscripts have ἄλλως, "otherwise"; Cousin prints ἄλλος, "one ... another", as Ficino's Latin (alius) reads.

And it is fitting, I think, that love too is seen in rank with this difference among intellects, since it has its first, hidden being in the intelligible intellect: for if it leapt forth from there, it is settled there as in its cause. !note The verses are Orphic; Cousin cites them from Hermann's Orphica (p. 508). For "always treads" both manuscripts have ἐπίσχυνη, which Hermann keeps; Gesner and Bentley read ἐπ’ ἴχνη, "on the tracks", which Cousin prints.

@ch 103a.35 | Why Socrates' speech on love ends with the daemon
So Diotima rightly calls love a great daemon, and Socrates joins the account of his daemon to the account of love: all that is daemonic hangs on love's middle place, and love and daemon are both middles, one between lover and beloved, the other between man and god. Socrates, a lover and a daemonic man at once, steers his love by his daemon's will and calls down its inspiration by his zeal in love.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Εἰκότως ἄρα καὶ ἡ Διοτίμα
Rightly, then, does Diotima call him a great daemon; and Socrates joins to his account of love and of lovers the showing of his daemon and of the daemonic cause. For as all that is daemonic hangs on love's middle place, so the account of the daemonic is joined to the account of love and is kin to it. Love is a middle between the beloved and the lover, and the daemon between man and god; and through this middle place they have been given a very great communion with each other. By it, too, Socrates, going through his account of himself, began from love and ended in the account of the daemonic. For he is at once a lover and a daemonic man: he steers the activity of the lover by the will of his daemon, and calls forth the daemon's breath of inspiration by his zeal in love.

@treatise 103a-daemons | Daemons in general, and the daemons allotted to us
Proclus now keeps the promise he made: to speak first of daemons in general, then of the daemons allotted to us, and last of Socrates' own. Daemons take their being from the life-giving goddess; they are souls in their being, more intellectual in the highest, more rational in the middle, more varied and material in the lowest, and they serve the gods, ranked under the gods above the world and under the gods within it, whose names they bear. They are the middle that binds the divine to the mortal, as Diotima says, and so they are not human souls that have changed their life, nor are any of the gods daemons. Of the daemons allotted to us there are six ranks, from the divine daemons that join souls to their gods down to those that guard matter. Our daemon is not our rational soul, nor the part of the soul that is active, nor a partial intellect: it is one guardian, the same from birth to judgment, who governs the whole of our life.

@ch 103a.36 | What daemons are
Proclus begins with daemons in general, since inquiry should go from the more universal down to the individual. Daemons come from the life-giving goddess and have the being of soul, in three degrees. They serve the gods, divided according to the twelve gods above the heavens and according to every character of the gods within the world, and they take the names of their gods. Through them mortal things share in the gods, and so the first and the last are in sympathy; they are the centres on which the maker fixed the order of the universe.
@taylor n006

@p
@greek Ταῦτα μὲν οὖν περὶ τούτων
This, then, is what we had to say about these things. We must next speak about daemons themselves, then about those allotted to us in common, and third, after these, about the daemonic sign of Socrates. For inquiries ought always to start from what is more universal and go on from there down to individuals. This is both according to nature and more akin to knowledge.
!note Taylor gives this whole account, in his own English, in his footnote on 103a ("Let us now speak in the first place concerning dæmons in general"); the sections from here on are checked against that note.

@p
@greek Οἱ μὲν τοίνυν δαίμονες
Daemons, then, who received their first existence from the life-giving goddess and flow from there as from a spring, were allotted the being of soul: more intellectual in those that are greater and more perfect in their being; lowered and more rational in those in the middle, which have received the second rank; varied, more irrational and more bound up with matter in the third, which bring the daemonic order to its end. Having this kind of being set before them, they are divided out together with the gods, since they have received a power that serves and ministers to them: in one way to the gods who are free of the world and lead the wholes before the cosmos, in another to the gods within the cosmos, who stand directly over the parts of the universe. For one division of them follows the twelve gods above the heavens, the other follows all the characters of the gods in the cosmos. Every god within the cosmos leads some daemonic order, to which he directly gives his own power: the god who makes, the power to make; the god who does not turn, the undefiled power; the god who brings to completion, the power to perfect. And about each of the gods there is an unspeakable multitude of daemons, honoured with the same names as the gods who lead them; for they are glad to be called Apollos and Zeuses and Hermeses, since they bear the stamp of the character of their own gods.

From these, in turn, mortal things too share in the streams that flow from the divine, and so animals and plants are made, some bearing the images of some gods, some of others. And this is why the last things are in sympathy with the first. For there are reflections of the first in the last, and the causes of the last are held beforehand in the first; and the middle kinds, the daemons, fill out the wholes and bind them, and hold their communion together, sharing in the gods and shared in by mortal things. So if anyone were to say that the maker of all fixed the centres of the order of the universe in the daemons, he would not miss the truth about them. For Diotima too gave them this rank: binding the divine and the mortal together, ferrying across the streams from above, leading all secondary things up to the gods, and filling out the wholes by holding the middle together.

@ch 103a.37 | Daemons are not human souls, nor are gods daemons
So we must not say that daemons are human souls that have changed this life. A daemon by relation is not a daemon in being, and the everlasting middle of the cosmos cannot be made out of a life that changes; for soul has no fixed rank, as the Republic says. Nor are some of the gods daemons, as Amelius said of the planets: Plato makes the gods rulers of the universe and sets the herds of daemons under them, and Diotima puts the daemonic in the middle.
@taylor n006

@p
@greek Οὐκ ἄρα ἀποδεξόμεθα
We shall not, then, accept those who say that daemons are the souls of men that have changed the life here. For one must not bring the daemonic by relation to the same thing as the daemonic in being, nor make the everlasting middle of all things in the cosmos stand out of a life that changes itself in many forms. The daemonic guard always stands the same, holding the wholes together; but soul has no fixed rank, as Socrates says in the Republic, for at different times it chooses different lives. Nor shall we praise those who make some of the gods daemons, the wandering ones for instance, as Amelius did. We shall obey Plato, who says the gods are rulers of the universe and sets the herds of daemons under them, and everywhere we shall keep Diotima's account, which gives the daemonic the middle rank between all things divine and mortal.

@ch 103a.38 | The six ranks of the daemons allotted to us
Of the daemons in the middle, the highest are divine daemons, often taken for gods, because the first of each rank keeps the form of the rank above it. Second come intellectual daemons, over the ascents and descents of souls; third, those that pass on the works of divine souls; fourth, those that carry the powers of whole natures into what is born and dies; fifth, the bodily daemons that bind the extremes in bodies; sixth, those about matter, which guard the outline of the forms in it.
@taylor n006

@p
@greek Κοινῇ μὲν οὖν περὶ
About the whole daemonic order in common, then, this is the thought we ought to have. After this we must speak particularly about the daemons allotted to us. Of these daemons, ranked, as we said, in the middle, the first and highest are divine daemons, and are often taken for gods, because of their surpassing likeness to the gods.

@p
@greek Ὡς γὰρ καθ’ ὅλου φάναι

Second after these are the daemons that share in the intellectual character, stand over the ascents and descents, and in general unfold and hand out to all things the making of the gods. Third are those that distribute the makings of the divine souls to what comes after, and complete the bond of the things that receive their outflowings with them. Fourth are those that carry over the active powers of the whole natures into things that are born and perish, and breathe into the partial natures life, order, reasons, and every kind of completion of mortal things. For how are everlasting bodies joined to perishable ones, how are the makers joined to what they make, except through this middle? It is this that governs the goods of bodily things too, and provides for all the advantages that come by nature. Sixth, after these, are those that turn about matter, holding together the powers that come down into matter from the heavenly matter above, guarding it always, and keeping the shadow-painting of the forms in it.

@ch 103a.40 | Our daemon is not our rational soul
The daemon is other than man, as Diotima puts daemons between gods and men and Socrates sets the daemonic against the human; man is a soul using a body; so the daemon is not the rational soul. When the Timaeus calls reason the daemon of the living thing, that holds only by analogy: there is a daemon in being, a daemon by analogy, and a daemon by relation. The daemon we seek is the daemon of the man, the one that leads him to judgment after death, as the Phaedo says.

@p
@greek Εἰ δὴ ταῦτα ὀρθῶς λέγοιμεν

For a daemon is other than a man, as Diotima says, who sets the daemons in the middle between gods and men; and Socrates shows it too, setting the daemonic against the human: "the cause", he says, "was not human, but a daemonic opposition". And yet this too is plain, that Plato himself in the Timaeus says that reason is settled in us as the daemon of the living thing; but that is true only as far as analogy goes. For the daemon in being is one, the daemon by analogy another, the daemon by relation another. In many places they are accustomed to call a daemon what lies directly over something in the rank of a daemon, set over what is lower; so in Orpheus Zeus says to his own father Kronos:
"Set our race upright, far-famed daemon."
And Plato himself in the Timaeus called daemons the gods who directly set becoming in order: "to speak of the other daemons and to know their birth is beyond us". The daemon by analogy, then, is such: what provides directly for each thing, even if it is a god, or one of those ranked between. The daemon by relation would be one who, through likeness to the daemonic kind, puts forth activities more wonderful than a man's, and has fastened his whole life to the daemons. This, I think, is how Socrates in the Republic called daemons those who had lived well and passed over into a better lot and a holier place. The daemon in being, again, is not called a daemon through its relation to secondary things, nor through its likeness to something else; it has this character of itself, and is marked out by a being of its own, its own powers, and different ways of acting. @ch 103a.41 | Nor the part of the soul that is active, nor a partial intellect
Some leave the rational soul aside and call the daemon whatever part of the soul is active, or what stands directly above it. But daemons rank above heroes in the Republic and cannot be parts of our souls; and if they were, our daemon would change as our life changes, and one man would have many daemons in one life, which is impossible, since the same daemon leads us from birth to the judges, as the Phaedo says. Nor is our daemon a partial intellect, for daemons stand in the breadth of souls, and the intellectual order is another.
@taylor n006

@p
@greek Ἀλλ’ οὐδὲ εἴ τινες
But neither, if some leave the rational soul aside and say that the daemon is the part of the soul that is active, reason for instance in those who live by reason, the spirited part in the spirited, or if some take it to be what stands directly above the part of our life that is active, as reason above the spirited, and spirit above those who live by desire, do these seem to me to aim at the truth of things. For, first, to make daemons parts of souls belongs to people who wonder at human life beyond all measure, and who take no notice of Socrates in the Republic, who ranks after god and daemon the heroic and then the human kind, and who rebukes the poets because they bring in heroes no better than men, and like them caught up in the same passions. One who finds fault with this would be far from making the daemons, who are higher even than the heroes, the same as the parts and powers of souls; for it would follow that what is better in being fills out what is lower. Second, the changes of lives will bring in changes of daemons of many kinds. For the lover of money often falls into the life of the lover of honour, and he into the life of right opinion, and he into the life of knowledge. So the daemon too will be different at different times, for the active part is different at different times. @treatise 103a-socrates | Socrates' own daemon
Last, Proclus turns to Socrates' own daemon, and says three things of it. First, Socrates calls it a god as well as a daemon, because it was among the first and highest daemons, the divine ones that guide souls who choose the intellectual life. Second, he perceived it as a voice: the inspiration went through his whole soul, from thought down to the organs of sense, and ended as a voice known by inner awareness rather than by hearing. Third, it never urged him on and always held him back, because Socrates was already moved of himself to share the good with everyone, and needed a daemon only to rein him in from those who were not fit for it, as a good charioteer reins in a horse born for the race. More deeply, it was a cleansing daemon under Apollo, drawing him in from the many to the life within, as Apollo holds Dionysus back from the Titans.

@treatise 103a-power | "Whose power you will learn": why Socrates speaks of the daemon's power
Proclus asks why Socrates says Alcibiades will learn later not the daemonic itself but its power. He gives four reasons. The being of daemons, as Iamblichus says, is very hard to see for those whose intellect is not wholly cleansed, while powers are known from their works. Power is the middle between being and activity everywhere, and daemons are the middle between gods and us. Alcibiades was a lover of power, and Socrates shows him that true power is in the daemons, not in ruling crowds of little men. And the dialogue's aim, self-knowledge, is itself a middle, so the middle term, power, suits it.

@ch 103a.47 | Why "its power", and not the daemonic itself
Being is hard to see, as Iamblichus says, and even the soul's being only the Timaeus fully declares; powers are seen from their activities. Power is everywhere the middle, between the father and intellect, between being and activity; so it fits the daemons, the middle between gods and us. Alcibiades loved power, and Socrates turns him to the true power in the daemons, unmixed with weakness; and the dialogue's aim, knowing oneself, is the middle knowledge, between knowing the divine and the life that leans outward.

So much must be said about these things. So the kinds better than us, which are in the middle between the gods and us, are fittingly signified through power, since it everywhere received the middle rank.

@treatise 103a-hope | "Now that it no longer opposes me": why the daemon allowed the talk
Proclus comments on the next words, "Now, since it no longer opposes me, I have come to you; and I am hopeful that it will not oppose me in future either." People ask how the daemon could allow a meeting that did Alcibiades no good, when he went on to mock the Mysteries, urge the Sicilian expedition and bring his country to ruin. Proclus sets aside the answers that the meeting made him less bad, or that his fall came only after he left Socrates, or that a daemon cannot know the free swing of our choice: the gods know the undetermined in a determined way, as higher natures know what lower ones cannot. With Iamblichus and his own teacher he keeps three things: what befits the daemon, what befits Socrates, and the young man's good. Alcibiades was made better, as the Symposium shows, and the good of a meeting is measured over the soul's whole period, not one life; and the good, like the sun, give their goods to all, so that one who will not take them has only himself to blame, as Laius had when he ignored Apollo. Last, he explains "I have come" and "I am hopeful": Socrates comes to visible beauty as to a statue of the divine, and uses the daemon's will to urge the young man on.

And further this must be said to this inquiry: that daemons, and serious men, act for their own sake and for the good in themselves, and do whatever they do for the end each of them has set for their life. @ch 103a.51 | "I have come" and "I am hopeful"
Divine things seem to come to us without leaving their seat, because we are stretched up towards them, as in invocations and things seen face to face; and Socrates comes to visible beauty as to a statue of intelligible beauty, as theurgists honour the visible images of the gods. @treatise 103b | "I have pretty well observed how you treated your lovers"
Proclus comments on the words "I have pretty well observed, watching in this time, how you treated your lovers: many as they were, and high-minded, there is none who, outdone by you in pride, has not fled." Socrates had read the signs of a fit nature in the young man's beauty and stature, as the Pythagoreans judged those who came to them by the body, and then tested him further by his deeds and life, as a good daemon judges the one in its care. The word "almost" marks how hard natural virtue is to know, being nearest to matter; "observed" marks a knowing gathered all at once at the end of a long search. Alcibiades deserves wonder because he shook off the crowd of vulgar lovers, whose high-mindedness was only apparent; and his pride, rightly trained, is a beginning of the soul's salvation, since high and lofty characters are akin to the divine beauty.

@ch 103b.2 | Why "almost"
Had the thing known been immaterial, pure and standing, Socrates would have called his knowledge firm; but what is mixed with matter and unstable makes our knowing of it uncertain, as one contingent premise makes a whole argument contingent. Natural virtue is such, so "almost" marks its dimness. "Observed" names the knowing gathered at once, which bears an image of intellect, and "watching" the search through time, a motion of soul, as the Athenian Stranger says.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Ἀλλ’ εἰ μὲν ἦν τὸ γνωστὸν
But if this object of knowledge were immaterial, pure and standing, he would surely have said that his knowledge of it too was fixed and beyond dispute; but since it is wholly the opposite, undetermined and imperfect, he adds "almost" to his observing, as to one that does not have exactness in every way. For just as those skilled in dialectic say that, even if someone uses ten thousand necessary premises but takes in one that is contingent, the conclusion is wholly made like the worse; in the same way, if the thing known has something woven into it of the material and unstable nature, it makes our knowledge of it undetermined and doubtful. For the knowledges of things that do not always stand in the same way are not beyond refutation, but such things are caught, barely, through conjecture. And natural virtue is of this kind, since everywhere the imperfect, being akin to matter, is easily changed. Moral virtue, at least, you would say has the firm and the standing more than this; and civic virtue, which is already joined with knowledge, more than that; and the further we pass over towards the steep and loftier form of the virtues, the more we come into accounts of them beyond refutation. The cause is that we come nearer to the intellectual being, with which is the abiding, the determined, and what is always the same.

And where could wisdom be, when the leading part does not lead? But since, eager to win the beloved, some brought him horses, some hunting dogs, some other things, courting and flattering him, and thought little of what they had, they are called high-minded; and just as they are called lovers though they have fallen away from true love, so too they are called high-minded though robbed of true high-mindedness. For cowardice belongs above all to appetite; the lovers, then, who run away are those who love through appetite and its sting. And third, this is a hymn to those truly worth loving, that they are not loved by the many and the low-born. Such, then, Alcibiades too shows himself from what lies before us. @treatise 104a | "You say you need no one": praise that is a hidden accusation
Proclus comments on the words "I want to go through the reason for which you have looked down on them: you say you need no man for anything, since what you have is great, so as to need nothing, beginning from the body and ending in the soul." Socrates turns the young man's scorn for his lovers into a reason, and urges him to live by reason. The whole speech seems to praise Alcibiades but is a hidden accusation, mixed so that he can bear to be refuted, as fits natural virtue, which is partly praiseworthy and partly blameworthy. Four things show the hidden charge: "you say", the excess of "need no one for anything", the "greatness" of what is acquired, and goods that begin from the body. Alcibiades is right that the happy man is self-sufficient, which the gods are first of all, but wrong to think himself happy: his major premise comes from common notions, his minor from appetite.

@ch 104a.2 | Why Socrates praises him: praise that hides a charge
One should not praise a man to his face, nor a young man, nor a beloved; yet Socrates does all three. The speech seems praise but is a hidden accusation, shaped so for two reasons: natural virtue is itself a sketch, partly to be praised as a trace of true virtue, partly to be blamed as imperfect and bodily; and Socrates wants both to win the beloved and not to puff him up. He mixes praise with refutation so that the young man can bear even "you live with the uttermost ignorance".
@taylor none

@p
@greek Ἀλλὰ τί, φαίης ἂν
But why, you might say, does Socrates come down to praising the young man? For surely one must not praise a man who is present, since such a work belongs to a flatterer; nor a young man, since it makes him vain; nor a beloved, since it is a hindrance to persuading the beloved to turn his intellect to oneself. Yet Socrates puts forward both the beloved and the present man for his praises. We must say, then, that this whole speech seems to be praise of the young man, and is, to look at, a speech of praise, but is rather a hidden and figured accusation. And he has needed this manner of speech, first, because natural virtue too is of this kind: a thing sketched in outline by nature, imperfect, and neither simply to be praised nor to be blamed, but sharing somehow, with reason, in both. For in so far as it is a virtue and the last trace of the true one, it has something to be praised; but in so far as it is imperfect, material and bodily, it is to be blamed for that. So the manner of the speech has imitated the character of natural virtue, having the praising part on view, as that has what is to be praised, and the opposite hidden, as that has what is to be blamed. Second, Socrates makes his reckoning of both things: of winning the beloved to himself (for what else comes before this for a lover?), and of not making him vainer and unfit for virtue. This sketched praise, then, wins him by what is seen, and draws him in by the hidden accusation; and if any orator or historian ever figured his speech, so too has Plato here, and one may, testing each of the things said, show the refutation of ignorance lying together with the praise: a natural readiness for the beautiful shown in the young man, but one imperfect, unformed, and missing true virtue. And further, by another reason too, Socrates praises openly and reproaches in secret. For before the unmixed refutations he offers them mixed with praises, accustoming the young man little by little to bear refuting words. So he begins from the figured praises, fitting this manner of speech to persuade, and, kindly praised, to have the hearer hear the stranger words too, and accept even this: "for you live with the uttermost ignorance". Such a kind of speech, at any rate, becomes bearable through the mixing now practised.

@ch 104a.3 | Four marks of the hidden charge
"You say" sends all that follows back to Alcibiades' own conceit: he praises himself, from double ignorance, thinking himself happy and self-sufficient. "Need no one for anything" oversteps human nature, and light words bring the heaviest penalty. "What you have is great" shows a greatness acquired, not native to the soul, where true greatness lies. And goods that begin from the body and end in the soul show their rottenness, since the things of the soul are divine and should lead, and the mortal follow.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Πῶς οὖν, ἅπερ εἴπομεν
How, then, is what we said present in the words before us, and how is the good speech mixed with the refutations? First, "you say" (he takes up "you say" by way of addition) separates the speech from Socrates, and sends back to Alcibiades himself all that is going to be said. For he all but says plainly: you are without need, as you say, not as I say, and these things belong to you by your conceit, not by mine. So the young man praises himself, doing a vulgar thing; and the cause of this is double ignorance: being ignorant about himself, he does not know his own wretchedness, but thinks himself happy and self-sufficient. Second, the excess is a still greater charge against the speaker. For to need no one for nothing does not aim at human nature and the weakness of our race, nor does it consider this: "for light and winged words bring the heaviest penalty; for over all such things Nemesis, messenger of Justice, has been set as overseer". He ought not, then, to think so highly of himself as to overstep the bounds of human nature.
!note "Double ignorance": not knowing, and not knowing that one does not know. "For light and winged words": the Laws (717d), on words spoken against parents; Proclus gives "over all such things" for Plato's "over all who deal in such things".

Third, "what you have is great" shows him adorned with a greatness acquired from outside, but not with the greatness that belongs to souls, nor with the native one. For all apparent power has its greatness in mere seeming; but what is truly lofty, great and pre-eminent lies in the very life of souls; and this is single in form, simple, not multiplied, and native, not present in us from outside. Fourth, that the goods begin from the body and end in the soul makes plain how rotten and shadowy they are, and refutes the one who thinks himself happy and believes that the beginning of his own goods starts from the body. For this man puts the necessary before what is chosen, and what follows before what leads; but, as the Athenian Stranger says, the things of the soul are divine and the mortal things human, and the one should rule, the other follow. He, on the contrary, says his happiness begins from the things of the body; he is far, then, from being happy and without need.

@ch 104a.4 | Self-sufficiency, and where Alcibiades goes wrong
Self-sufficiency is first in the gods, who are the fullness of all things and give it to intellects, divine souls, daemons and us, the rest having it by sharing, the gods by being. So the common notion rightly marks happiness by self-sufficiency. Alcibiades reasons well in his major premise, that the happy man is without need, and badly in his minor, that he himself is happy; so too the lover of pleasure and the lover of money. The major premises come from common notions and reason, on which all agree; the minor from imagination, sense and passion, which divide us, as the Titans tear the intellect apart.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Ταῦτα μὲν οὖν ἐστι δι’ ἅ φημι
These, then, are the reasons why I say that Socrates praises in appearance but truly refutes the young man's ignorance. But in general we must ask this very thing: from what notion Alcibiades made this lack of need an element of the happy life. And we must say that the self-sufficient is first of all in the gods themselves. For the gods are good, beyond being in their goodness, and the fullnesses of all the things that are; and through them the self-sufficient belongs to all the others too, to the intellects, to the divine souls, to the daemons and to us; the others are self-sufficient by sharing, the gods by their very existence. For this reason, then, the common and undistorted notion marks happiness by self-sufficiency: for from whom comes the "well", from them comes the self-sufficient too.
!note At "the self-sufficient" Cousin gives Ficino's Latin in a footnote, which reads "the gods are good, and certain goodnesses beyond being" (superessentiales bonitates); Cousin's Greek prints ἀγαθότητος, "of goodness", where the Latin implies ἀγαθότητες; the English follows the sense of both.

@p
@greek Καὶ ὁρᾷς δὴ πάλιν ὅπως
And you see again how here Alcibiades is right in the major premise, but goes wrong in the minor. For he reasons in this way: through body, family, friends and wealth I am happy, without need; I am, he says, without need. So that the happy man is without need is true; but that he himself is happy is false. The conclusion, then, is false because of the minor premise; and in this way you will find both the lover of pleasure and the lover of money going wrong through it. For the one takes pleasure for the good, the other money; but that all that is desired is good is common to them. And, to put it shortly, each of them sets down the major premises putting them forward from the common notions and from reason, but brings forward the minor ones from imagination, from sense, from the passions without reason. That is why in these they differ from one another, but in those they agree. For the passions are the causes of division and standing apart for souls: they are Titanic, and pull apart and tear in pieces the intellect in us; but reason is common to all, and so is the putting forward of reason; and for this reason Hermes is "common", so that we may give the saying an ethical sense too.
!note "Hermes is common": κοινὸς Ἑρμῆς, a Greek saying used when one finds something and others claim a share; Proclus applies it to reason, which is common to all.

@treatise 104a-gifts | Beauty, family, friends, Pericles and wealth
Proclus comments on Socrates' list of what Alcibiades thinks he has: beauty and stature, the noblest family in the greatest of Greek cities, many good friends and kinsmen on both sides, the power of Pericles his guardian, and wealth. Going through them one by one makes the praise plainer and the refutation sharper: these goods are scattered and need something else to unify them, so they cannot make anyone self-sufficient, and each phrase of Socrates hides a charge. Then Proclus asks why souls prize such things at all: beauty and greatness, nobility, friendship and guardianship are first in the gods, and souls admire their images in bodies because they dimly remember the originals. Last he shows from history that Alcibiades really was handsome, nobly born and Pericles' rival.

@ch 104a.5 | The list, and why such goods cannot make a man self-sufficient
The true good is one, or comes from the one; these goods are offered heaped together and referred to no one thing, so they need something else to order and unify them. Self-sufficiency means taking in nothing from outside, but Alcibiades looks to body, family, friends and Pericles, and not to his own soul; so he who "needs no one" is shown to need many. A soul made like intellect seeks its good in itself; one like the body thinks its good lies in others.

@ch 104a.6 | What each phrase hides

Family and friends belong to the visible man and to many bad men too; a happy man would not need so many, and friendship needs goodness. Again, he did not say "you think truly that you are most beautiful and tallest", but "you are not wrong". For to be true belongs most of all to immaterial objects of knowledge; but not to be false belongs to sensible things, and especially when the forms master matter; since matter is the true falsehood and what truly is not, as the inspired interpreters of Plato are accustomed to say. And again, to begin the apparent praises from the body is a sufficient refutation of the ignorance of one who thinks highly of himself for these things. @ch 104a.7 | Why souls prize these things: images of divine goods
Beauty and greatness are first among the gods: beauty makes the divine lovely and is akin to limit, greatness makes them transcend and is akin to the unlimited; from there they come down to the visible cosmos, and souls, remembering, admire their images in bodies. Nobility too is first in the gods, as Hera says to Zeus; the rational soul is nobler than the irrational, made by the first maker, and souls' lasting nobility comes from the gods they follow, not from ancestors or cities. True friendship belongs to the gods and to good souls, as in Empedocles' Sphere; and our true guardians are the daemons set over us, not Pericles.

@greek Ταῦτα μὲν οὖν περὶ τῆς τῶν ἐλέγχων
So much, then, for the mixing of refutations in these praises. Beginning again from above, let us say from what notion souls think highly of themselves for beauty and stature of bodies, and for good birth, friendship and power; for these are images of the true goods held out to souls, which sensible souls look down on, and foolish ones cling to stubbornly. We say, then, that beauty and greatness appeared in the very first orders of the gods: the one making all things divine lovable and desired by what comes after, the other making them transcend the wholes and stand apart from their own offspring. The one is akin to limit, as form of forms and as blooming upon all the intelligible forms; the other to the unlimited, as what cannot be encompassed, encompasses all things and masters all. That is why Diotima gave beauty the first rank and greatness the second; for the object of love is the beautiful (and Love is a great daemon, the first to hang upon beauty). From above, then, from the very first principles, it goes forth through all the middle orders as far as the visible cosmos, and makes this the greatest and most beautiful of the sensible gods, as Timaeus says. Souls, then, having grasped beforehand by their native notions that these shine out in divine things, are amazed at the beauty and greatness in mortal bodies, as having a reflection of those; but through ignorance of those true things, they stay among these, and wonder at them alone.

@treatise 104c | "You have mastered your lovers": the divine lover and the vulgar
Proclus comments on the words "In all this, priding yourself, you have mastered your lovers, and they, being lesser, were mastered; and this has not escaped you. So I know well that you wonder what I can be thinking of, that I do not give up my love, and what hope I have that I stay when the others have fled." The vulgar lovers, lesser and rising as if from matter, were mastered and fled; the divine lover stays, steady like his god. The soul is a middle between intellect and body: its love is steady when it looks to intellect, changeable when it looks to bodies. The divine love is an activity, the insolent one a passion; and as justice, beauty and every form is threefold, so is love, and the insolent love clings as a by-product to the third, the love that shares in it only at times. Last, "this has not escaped you" lays the young man's passions open as a surgeon opens a wound, and "I know well" replaces the earlier "I think", now that the cause has been shown.

@ch 104c.2 | The two loves, and why love is threefold
The divine love is an activity, kin to intellect and divine beauty; the insolent love is a passion, kin to bodies, for making belongs to the bodiless and being affected to bodies. They are opposed as steady and changeable, active and passive, immaterial and material, inspired and insolent. Their opposition comes from the descent of things: as justice, beauty, equality and every form exist first in themselves, then in what always shares in them, then in what shares in them at times, love too is threefold, and the insolent love clings to the third.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Τοῦτο μὲν οὖν οὐκ ἂν ἄλλως
This, then, could not be otherwise. But we must say, from the same cause, that the divine love is an activity, and the insolent love a passion. For the one is ranked with intellect and divine beauty, the other with bodies; and what belongs to activity fits immaterial forms, what belongs to passion fits material ones; for making is proper to the bodiless, being affected to bodies. The loves, then, are opposed to each other, since the one is steady and the other changeable, the one active and the other passive, the one immaterial and the other material, the one inspired and the other insolent; for so Socrates in the Phaedrus named it, as the oracles too, I think, call it the choking of true love. What, then, is the cause of the opposition of these loves? The descent of things. For the procession that begins from above ends in these things, which can change, and can give rise to some swerving as a by-product in themselves. So with justice: one is first of all just, justice itself, and not such by sharing; another shares in that first, and shares always; another shares in it at times, and at times falls from sharing. For justice itself ought not to stand barren, giving nothing of its own character to anything; none of the first things is like that, but barrenness belongs only to matter, as the last of beings. Nor ought it, being shared by others, to be shared first by things that share at times; for to the simple and unchanging, the composite and changeable is foreign, and needs some middle bond to be joined to it. Nor ought it, shared by the things that can always be lit by it, to stop its giving at these, so that the last thing would not be what always hangs on the first principles. It is necessary, then, to see justice threefold; and not only it, but all the other forms too, beauty itself, the equal, the like, and each of the others; and to call some of them "themselves", and to see what shares in them, and the by-products, in the third rank from the truth. For nothing can arise as a by-product in the simple things, nor in what shares always: in the one, simplicity prevents the by-product; in the other, the everlastingness of sharing. So the worse must arise as a by-product in the third things, which share at times: with justice here, injustice; with beauty, ugliness; with the equal, the unequal. In this way, then, love too is threefold: one is love itself and first; one is shared everlastingly; one is shared at times. To this third, then, the insolent love clings as a by-product; that is why it is opposed to it, sets before itself the opposite ends, uses different aims, and grows in opposite lives.

@ch 104c.5 | What every sharing of goods needs
All things are joined to one another so: the causes that perfect hold out their gift ready, and those that will share are roused to receive it. Power is twofold, the maker's, mother of activity, and the receiver's, which takes in perfection; and what makes must overflow, what receives must swell towards sharing. Unmoved causes, like the intelligibles, are present to all alike, so that only the receiver's unfitness hinders; moved causes, like the sun, come and go. So there must be the giver's perfecting power, the receiver's utmost fitness, and their meeting, which is never by chance, but by nature, intellect, a daemon or a god. So here: Socrates' perfection as a lover was ready, and Alcibiades was fit; the daemon fixed the moment; and the right moment comes down from the gods to the last things.

@treatise 104d | "Slow to begin, slow to stop": how Socrates opens his speech
Proclus comments on the exchange "You will hear me gladly, then, as is likely, if, as you say, you want to know what I have in mind; and I speak to one who will listen and wait." "Certainly; but speak." "Look, then: it would be no wonder if, as I was slow to begin, I were slow to stop." "My good man, speak; I will listen." "Then I must speak." Socrates asks for the fewest and simplest starting points, only that the young man listen now and answer later, and his delays make the young man eager to hear. Proclus reads each phrase in turn, and explains why the true lover is slow to begin and slow to stop: judgment of the beloved takes time, and sympathy will not let go, as the providence of the gods is slow to begin punishment or reward and long to keep it up. Then he comments on "It is hard for a lover to approach a man who is not mastered by his lovers; but I must dare to tell you my mind": Alcibiades has a middle power, natural virtues and unformed notions, and Socrates dares to come down to him, as Heracles went down to Hades, to lead him up.

@ch 104e.1 | "Hard to approach one who is not mastered by his lovers"
This preamble holds in brief the causes of the whole meeting. Alcibiades, having mastered many lovers, is still imperfect as to the form of love, unable to tell its orders apart or what is against nature from what is by nature; had he been mastered by the many lovers, he would have lost all fitness, had he clear notions of the two loves, he would be most perfect: as it is, he is fit to receive knowledge. He has a middle power, natural virtues with unformed notions, which, as Plotinus says, have an imperfect eye and character. Socrates, full of the good, dares to come down to a lower activity, as all turning to the worse is daring for divine lovers, so that, like Heracles, he may lead the one he loves up out of Hades, to the intellectual life, where he will know himself and the divine.

@p
@greek Τὸ προοίμιον τοῦτο τῶν λόγων
This preamble of the speech is brief and compact, and holds in the fewest words the causes of the whole meeting. For Alcibiades, having mastered many other lovers, is imperfect as to the form of love, neither telling apart its different orders, nor seeing how what is against nature differs from what is by nature. That is why he needs the perfecting that comes from Socrates: for being not wholly perfect, he needed one who would lead him; and being not unfit for the divine love, he could take in help. If he had been mastered by his many lovers, he would have been shown to have lost all fitness. If he had held clearly articulated notions about the loves, the inspired and the insolent, he would have been most perfect. As it is, being ignorant of the one and having fled the other, he is fit to receive knowledge. Socrates, on the other hand, is full of good and beautiful things, and holds out to the young man a share in the virtues. But because he comes down to an activity directed towards another, and goes forth from the life within into a lower motion, for this reason he calls this going forth "daring", in the Pythagorean way; and he shows the young man his forethought for him, and unfolds the whole choice of his own life, and the lover's aim for the sake of which he does not give up his love.
!note "Alcibiades": Cousin prints Ἀλκιβιάδας here, and Σοκράτους for Σωκράτους, and ἐκφαίνε for ἐκφαίνει, "shows"; all three are misprints, and the English reads them so. "Daring, in the Pythagorean way": Proclus names no Pythagorean.

@p
@greek Διότι μὲν οὖν ὁ Ἀλκιβιάδης
In that Alcibiades is not mastered by his lovers, and that it is hard even for the true lover to approach him, since he has not yet told the loves apart, he shows that he has a middle power, having the natural virtues, but using unarticulated notions (for natural virtue is of this kind: it has "an imperfect eye and character", according to the divine Plotinus). In that Socrates dares to tell his mind, he comes down to an activity lower than the one that stays within him (for to divine lovers every turning towards the worse is daring); but still Socrates comes down, so that, like Heracles, he may lead the one he loves up out of Hades, and persuade him to stand away from the apparent life and run up to the intellectual and divine one, from which he will know both himself and the divine, the whole of it, set apart from all things, and existing before them as their cause.
!note "An imperfect eye and character": Plotinus, Enneads I.3.6: "natural virtue has both an imperfect eye and character, and the starting points are mostly the same for both". Proclus does not say which story of Heracles in Hades he means.

@treatise 104e-worthy | "If I saw you content with what is present": the nature worth loving
Socrates says he would long ago have given up his love had he seen Alcibiades content with what he has. Proclus draws from this the elements of a nature worth loving: not to rest in what is present but to seek something greater. Souls keep echoes of the ruling life they saw among the gods and, without a knowing guide, chase its images in power and tyranny; the souls that come down from heaven choose lives of rule, and ambition, the first step down from the best life, is the last tunic shed on the way up. So the true lover must know souls, their lives and their images.

@ch 104e.2 | "If I saw you content": the elements of a nature worth loving
As the Republic gives the elements of the philosophic nature, so these words give those of the nature worth loving: some visible, gifts of fate and nature such as beauty and stature; some hidden, seeds of the gods' providence, such as the ruling and lofty form of life. By the first Alcibiades masters the many lovers, by the second he is mastered by Socrates.

"For, Alcibiades, if I saw you content with the things I have just gone through, and thinking that you ought to live out your life among them, I would long ago have given up my love, so at least I persuade myself."
!note These are the dialogue's next words (Alcibiades I, 104e-105a). "I have just gone through": Cousin's note says that the editions have διήλθομεν, "we have gone through", and the Venice manuscript διῆλθον, which he prints.

@p
@greek Τίς ἐστιν ὁ ἀξιέραστος
In these words Socrates shows clearly who is by nature worth loving. And as in the Republic he has handed down to us the elements of the philosophic nature, so here too, I think, he hands down certain elements of the nature worth loving. These are twofold: some are visible, others hidden; some appear about the body, others are seen within, as motions of the soul itself; some are gifts of fate and of nature, such as beauty and stature, others are seeds of the gods' providence, given to souls for their salvation, such as the ruling, the commanding, and the form of life that lifts itself on high. By the natural advantages, then, those that come from fate, Alcibiades has mastered the many lovers: for through the high-mindedness that goes with that nature he looked down on the discordant and disorderly pestering that came from them. But by the motions of providence and of the soul he is mastered by Socrates: for it is because of these that Socrates persuades him that he is worth his zeal more than anything. And here it is shown what the superiority of the inspired friend over the vulgar lover is, and how great. For the vulgar lover falls short even of the natural advantages; these come second to the motions of the soul; and these are perfected by the inspired lover alone. By much more, then, as the geometers would say, may the vulgar lover be said to be defeated by divine love. Be that as it may, as we were saying, Socrates begins his words, himself persuaded that he is worth everything to the one he loves, and trying to persuade him too, exactly as is said in the Timaeus: by intellect persuading necessity to follow towards the best, and making the good in the proper sense the guardian of life.
!note "The elements of the philosophic nature": the Republic, Book VI (485a-487a). "With that nature": Cousin prints ἐπ’ αὐτῆς, "upon it", a feminine; the English takes it to mean the nature worth loving. "Intellect persuading necessity": Timaeus 48a, "intellect ruling necessity by persuading her to lead the most of what comes to be towards the best".

@ch 104e.3 | Why not rest in what is present: the soul's three lives
Every soul has seen the things that are, and keeps echoes of the true ruling and commanding seated in the gods; forgetting them, it longs for power, pleasure or money without knowing why. Souls stay with the gods, go forth among images, and turn back by knowledge; the followers of Zeus long for the ruling life, and become either saved or tyrants, as they meet knowing guides or not.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Τί δήποτ’ οὖν στοιχεῖον
Why, then, is this an element of the nature worth loving: not to be content with what is present nor stay among it, but always to seek what is greater than what is present? Because, he will say, it belongs to a soul that holds everything vulgar in no honour and is persuaded that it is worth nothing, and longs only to see what is great and precious; a soul that does not know what it has undergone, but imagines, by its native notions, some other greatness and a true height. For every soul, says Plato, has by nature seen the things that are. So it saw there what is truly precious, and the ruling and the commanding, seated in the gods themselves; and having seen them it was struck with wonder at them, and keeps some echoes of what is within. But through forgetfulness and ignorance it does not know what it is that it wonders at, nor by what notions it longs for power and greatness and rule, or for pleasure, or for money. But to the man of knowledge, who has examined the things themselves and the imperfection of nature, it is shown what true power is; and he makes plain in what kind of life the commanding and the ruling are seen, and perfects the imperfect motion of that soul.
!note "Every soul... has by nature seen the things that are": Phaedrus 249e. "It is shown": Cousin prints δείκνυται; one might expect δείκνυσι, "he shows", like the verbs that follow, but the English keeps the print.

@p
@greek Ὅλως γὰρ τριττήν τινα
For, in general, let us think of a threefold life of souls, if you like: one according to the activity that stays in the principles; one according to the activity that goes forth from them; and one according to the activity that turns back to them again. And we say that while souls stay with the gods they are active intellectually, and enjoy both the gods' good-like will and their unmixed giving; but going forth from there, through imagination and an indefinite motion, they meet with images of the things there, appearances instead of the true things; and beholding the likenesses and images of things, they dart wildly about them, longing for what they saw, but turned round towards what they do not long for. And turning back among these towards the principles, they pass again from the images to the original patterns, and here they need intellection and reason. For the ascent is through knowledge, and it is reason that carries the soul across to intellect, away from slipping and imagination. Now different souls are akin to different sights; so some run after some images, others after others: for of the things they saw there, they cherish the images and the shadows. But those who were followers of great Zeus, and went round with him as he holds the ruling rank among the twelve rulers of the universe, come here too after the form of their own god, and long for the ruling form of life; and they do not know what is really such, but chase after authority and power among appearances. If they meet guides who have knowledge, this fitness of theirs ends in their salvation and their leading up; but if they stay among the images, they become lovers of rule, are excited about apparent powers, and fill their lives with tyrannies and such evils.
!note "Of the things they saw": Cousin's note says that the manuscript has ὧ γὰρ ἴδιον; he prints ὧν γὰρ ἴδον, which the English follows. The followers of Zeus: Phaedrus 246e-247a (Zeus, the great leader in heaven, with the army of gods and daemons in eleven companies behind him, the twelve gods in all) and 252e (those of Zeus seek one who is "philosophic and ruling by nature"). At the foot of these pages Cousin prints a Latin version signed M. F. (Marsilio Ficino), not transcribed here.

@ch 104e.4 | The souls from heaven, and ambition the last tunic
The souls that come down from heaven, having seen the gods' powers, choose lives of rule, ambitious or tyrannical; content with the present is a small nature from under the earth. Ambition, the first step down from the best life, is the last tunic shed on the way up, so this desire belongs to souls in the porch of reason, and the true lover must know souls, lives and images.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Ἔστι μὲν οὖν καὶ ταῦτα λέγειν
This, then, is one thing that can be said about souls: how this ruling and great-working quality makes them worth loving, and for what cause. But one may also give, I think, the account Socrates hands down in the Republic about the souls that come down from heaven: that all such souls run after powers above all, and pursue lives of rule and command. For having gone round with the heavenly gods, and seen their powers that master the universe, and the kingdoms that govern from above down to the last things, and the chains held up by them, they too aim at a like life, and want to rule over very many and to stand above other men, as those gods stand above becoming, and to steer the affairs of all, as those gods steer all lesser things. And longing for this they leap at tyrannies, powers and honours; and some of them choose the ambitious life, others the tyrannical. Socrates knows, then, that this love of rule and of honour belongs to those souls which once shared the heavenly going round, and came from there to the region about the earth. That is why here he says to the young man that he would long ago have given up his love if he had seen him content with what is present: for this belongs to a small and ignoble nature, one that has come from somewhere below, from the places under the earth, and so is fond of what is present, and does not look up to the whole and to what stands above, nor, through greatness in its works, prove worth loving.
!note The souls that come down from heaven: the Republic's myth of Er (619b-d), where the first to choose, who had come from heaven, takes the greatest tyranny, and "not the fewest" of those so caught had come from heaven, untrained by toil; the souls from under the earth are at 614e-615a.

@p
@greek Ἐκ δὴ τρίτων λεγέσθω
In the third place, let it be said also that after the constitution according to reason comes the ambitious and timocratic one: for it comes next after that one. And the departure from the best life goes first into this one, as Socrates says in the Republic, and in the ascent it is the last to be purified away.
!note The Republic, Book VIII (545a-c and 547c): timocracy, the ambitious constitution, is the first to come after the best one.

@p
@greek Διὸ καὶ ὁ Πλάτων ἔσχατον χιτῶνα
That is why Plato called ambition the last tunic of souls. For contempt for money and disdain for pleasure are found even in the more moderate souls; but by honours, glory and power many even of those who are thought to be clever have been defeated. So as we go up, and strip off the passions and the tunics we took on in coming down, the last tunic to be stripped off is that of ambition, so that, having become naked, as the oracle says, we may set ourselves firmly in the god, becoming pure and unmixed reasons, and leaving all the passions about the earth, where they were set, and making ourselves like the divine lives.
!note "The last tunic": the saying is not in Plato's dialogues; Athenaeus (Deipnosophists XI, 507d) also reports it as Plato's. "Naked, as the oracle says": Proclus means the Chaldean Oracles, and gives no more. "Pure and unmixed reasons": Cousin prints the singular λόγος with the plural εἰλικρινεῖς, "unmixed"; the English reads λόγοι, "reasons".

@p
@greek Εἰ τοίνυν ἡ πρώτη τῶν ψυχῶν
If, then, the first descent of souls is the ambitious life, it is clear that this desire is not one of souls that have fallen into the depth, but of souls that turn about in the porch of reason. That is why Socrates took such a character to be worth loving, as standing above becoming and more akin to reason: for our spirit is nearer to reason than desire is. For these causes, then, Socrates took the life that loves rule and honour to be worthy of a lover's care. And from this it is plain that one who is genuinely the lover of anyone must both know the nature of the soul and have examined the different choices of lives, and be a trustworthy judge of images, able to see of what things each bears the reflections, and by what roads each soul is fitted by nature to reach its own end. For one who has not seen all this together exactly misses the goods that come from the inspiration of love.
!note "The reflections": Cousin prints ἐκφάσεις, "utterances"; the sense seems to require ἐμφάσεις, "reflections", "appearances", and the English reads it so.
