@treatise Prologue | Proclus' introduction to the dialogue
Proclus opens his lectures on the dialogue the school read first. Every being has its own good, fitted to what it is, so we must know what we are before we can know our good: knowing ourselves is the starting point of all philosophy, as the Delphic "Know yourself" teaches. The First Alcibiades is where Plato makes this his aim, so it comes first. Proclus then sets out what the dialogue is for, how it answers to the parts of the universe, why Iamblichus put it first of the ten dialogues, and how it divides: not by its manner of speech, nor into ten arguments, but, with Iamblichus, into three parts, every argument leading to self-knowledge.

@ch Pr.1 | Each being has its own good
Every kind of being has its own perfection, matched to the rank of its being: the good is given out by a fitting equality, more to the higher, less to the lower. So the good of intellect is in eternity, that of soul in time, that of body in being as nature made it.
@taylor 2 3

@p
@greek Τῶν Πλατωνικῶν διαλόγων
We hold that the most authoritative and most secure starting point of Plato's dialogues, and, one may say, of all philosophical study, is to discern what our own being is. Once this is rightly laid down, we shall be able to learn far more exactly both the good that belongs to us and the evil that fights against it. For just as each of the things that are differs in its being, so too, by nature, it differs in its perfection: one perfection for some, another for others, as their being falls lower.
!flag of the whole theory of that philosopher | The Greek says "of all philosophical study, so to speak"; it is not Plato's theory alone.
!flag and this according to a subjection of essence | The Greek word (hyphesis) means a lowering, a falling off: perfection differs as being falls away from the first. Taylor's "subjection" means this, in an old sense.

@p
@greek Εἴτε γὰρ ἀπὸ τῆς αὐτῆς
Suppose that being and the good come forth from the same hearth and the very first source, as Aristotle says. Then, surely, what is intellective in perfection must come to each thing by the measures of its being. Or suppose the good comes from one cause, older and holier, and being and existence come to things from another. Even so, as each thing shares in being more dimly or more clearly, so too it will share in the good: the things that are first, in a greater and more complete good; those set in the middle, in a second good, by their rank; and the last of the things that are, in the last. How else could things be, when gods and providence rule them, than with the good given out to each as it deserves? Intellect, surely, does not lead the things that are into order and give each the measure of being that fits it, while the good, which is older even than intellect, gives itself to things and channels its goods to them without order: giving the same goodness to things that differ in being, causes and their effects, say, or handing out to things that are the same in being the perfections of both the higher and the lower. For, as Timaeus says, it was not and is not right for the best to do anything but what is most beautiful and best proportioned. But what is best proportioned is not the same good for what is first and for what is second, nor, for things that are like one another, a perfection that differs in being. Rather, as the Athenian Stranger says, the most musical and best of all is the equality that gives unequal shares to unequal things: greater to the greater, less to the less.
!note Timaeus' words are in the Timaeus (30a), which has only "the most beautiful"; "and best proportioned" is Proclus' own. The Athenian Stranger speaks of this equality in the Laws (book 6, 757b-c).
!note "What is intellective in perfection" renders the Greek as printed (τὸ νοερὸν τῆς τελειότητος), as Cousin's Latin does ("id quod intellectuale est perfectionis"); the phrase is hard, and Taylor leaves it out.
!flag it is certainly necessary that perfection should be imparted to every thing according to the measures of essence | The Greek has "what is intellective in perfection" coming to each thing; Taylor leaves out the word. The modern version keeps it, and a note says it is hard.
!flag a cause more ancient and more characterized by unity | The Greek says "older and holier" (hagiōteras); "more characterized by unity" is Taylor's reading.
!flag For, how otherwise can things participate of deity and providence, and a distribution according to their desert? | The Greek says that, since gods and providence rule, things can be no other way than with the good given out to each as it deserves; things do not "participate of deity" here.
!flag but that the good, or the ineffable principle of things, which is more ancient than intellect | "Or the ineffable principle of things" is Taylor's; the Greek says only "the good, which is older than intellect".
!flag a distribution of inequality to things unequal, and of equality to things equal | The Greek has only the equality that gives unequal shares to unequal things; "and of equality to things equal" is Taylor's.

@p
@greek Κατὰ ταύτην δὴ οὖν
By this equality, then, the good too is different in different things, and the good in each thing is by nature matched to its nature. So the perfection of intellect is in eternity, and that of soul in time; the good of soul lies in living by intellect, and the good of body in being as nature made it. And again, the perfection of gods is one thing, that of angels and daemons another, and that of particular souls another. Whoever thinks that, where the being of these differs, their perfection is the same, misses the truth about the things that are. For, as Homer says, "never alike is the race" of gods and of men, nor of the kinds between them and the two extremes.
!note Homer's words are from the Iliad (5.441), where Apollo warns Diomedes that the race of the immortal gods and that of men who walk the earth are never alike.
!flag but of the rational soul in time | The Greek says "of soul", not "the rational soul"; and "the good of soul" is "according to intellect", with no word for Taylor's "energy".

@ch Pr.2 | Know a thing's being before its good
Perfection belongs to the being that shares in it, so in every rank we must know the being first: without parts or divided, eternal or in time, simple or composite. So the first principle of all philosophy is a pure knowledge of ourselves, held by reasoning from the cause.
@taylor 4

@p
@greek Εἰ δὴ ταῦτα ὀρθῶς
If this is rightly understood, then in every rank of the things that are we must know the being before the perfection. For perfection does not belong to itself; it belongs to the being that shares in it. So we must look first at the being: whether it is one of the beings without parts, or of those divided among bodies, or of those set in the middle; whether it is one of the eternal things, or of those that exist through all of time, or of those that come to be in some part of time; and whether it is one of the simple things, set up before all putting together, or of the composite things that are always held together by bonds that cannot be undone, or of those that can be broken up again into the things they were put together from. If we look at each thing in this way, we shall be able to understand its good as well, of whatever kind it is.
!flag such as intellectual natures, or to such as are divisible about bodies, viz. corporeal forms and qualities | The examples (intellectual natures; bodily forms and qualities) are Taylor's; the Greek names only the kinds of being.

@p
@greek Δῆλον γὰρ αὖ ὅτι
For again it is plain that, for those whose lot is a being without parts, the good is eternal; for those whose being is divided, the good too is by nature in time and in motion; and for those in between, perfection too is seen by the measures of their existence: it needs time, but the first time, which can measure the periods of things without body. So we may count this as the most authoritative starting point of all philosophy and of Plato's work, as we said: a pure, unalloyed knowledge of ourselves, marked out in the bounds of knowledge and firmly tied down by reasoning from the cause.
!note "Tied down by reasoning from the cause" recalls the Meno (98a), where true opinions become knowledge when they are tied down by reasoning out the cause.
!flag is to be considered according to the measures of subsistence and perfection | The Greek says that the perfection of these is seen "by the measures of their existence"; perfection is what is measured, not one of the measures.

@ch Pr.3 | The Delphic "Know yourself"
We must begin where the god at Delphi bids us. As the uninitiated were kept out of the inner shrine at Eleusis, "Know yourself" shows the way up to the divine: whoever knows himself can be joined to Apollo. Socrates too began philosophy from the inscription.
@taylor 4

@p
@greek Καὶ πόθεν γὰρ ἄλλοθεν
Where else should we begin our own purification and perfection, if not where the god at Delphi bade us? Those who went into the precinct of the goddesses at Eleusis found a notice telling them not to pass into the inner shrine if they were uninitiated and not yet perfected in the rites. In the same way, I think, the words KNOW YOURSELF, written up before the temple at Delphi, showed the way of being led up to the divine and the most effective road to purification. They all but say plainly, to those able to understand, that whoever has known himself, beginning from the hearth, can be joined to the god who reveals the whole truth and leads the life of purification; but whoever does not know who he is, being uninitiated and unperfected, is not fit to share in the providence of Apollo.
!note "Beginning from the hearth" is a Greek saying for beginning at the very start, at home: here, from oneself.
!flag if they are uninitiated in the highest of the mysteries | The Greek says only "uninitiated and unperfected"; "in the highest of the mysteries" is Taylor's.
!flag as being uninitiated both in the lesser and greater mysteries | Again Taylor's gloss: the Greek has "being unperfected and uninitiated", with no lesser and greater mysteries.

@p
@greek Αὕτη τοίνυν ἔστω
Let this, then, be the starting point of philosophy and of Plato's teaching: the knowledge of ourselves. For it is fitting, I think, that Apollo's companion should begin the perfecting of the imperfect from the very place where the god himself bids us begin. Socrates, too, who says he is a fellow servant of the swans, and who had the gift of prophecy from the god no less than they, is said to have begun his drive towards philosophy here, when he came on the Pythian inscription and took it to be a command of Apollo himself.
!note The Workshop takes "Apollo's companion" to be Plato, whose teaching this is; the next sentence turns to Socrates.
!note Socrates calls himself a fellow servant of the swans, sacred to Apollo, in the Phaedo (85b); he speaks of the Delphic inscription in the Phaedrus (229e).

@ch Pr.4 | Why the First Alcibiades comes first
No dialogue makes the study of our being its aim as this one does, so the reading of Plato begins here. Nothing is nearer to us than ourselves; we are all, like Alcibiades, ignorant of ourselves, and need the same help.
@taylor 4

@p
@greek Ἐντεῦθεν τοίνυν καὶ ἡμῖν
From here, then, we too must begin, following the god. And we must ask in which of the dialogues above all Plato has this as his aim, the study of our being, so that we may make it the very first starting point of Plato's writings too. Could we name any other before the Alcibiades, and the conversation of Socrates handed down in it? Where else shall we say that our being, what it is, is shown in this way? Where else are man and the nature of man looked into? Where is the Delphic inscription, whatever it means, fully tested? Or how could we look into anything else among the things there are, whether things that are or things that come to be, before these, when we have heard Socrates himself say: "It seems strange to me to look into other things while I do not know myself"? For nothing is nearer to us than ourselves. If we do not know what is nearest, what way is there to know what is further off, and what is by nature known through us?
!note Socrates says this in the Phaedrus (229e-230a), where Plato's words are "it seems absurd to me" (geloion); Cousin prints "strange" (alloion), and the modern version follows the Greek shown.

@p
@greek Εἰ δὲ δὴ μὴ ταῦτα
And if you consider not only that these things are worked out at length in this dialogue, but also that this is the first conversation Socrates begins with Alcibiades, and that it is he who says that the beginnings of perfection hang on the study of ourselves, you would no longer doubt that everyone eager to be perfected must begin here.

@p
@greek Καὶ γὰρ ἡμῶν ἕκαστος
For each of us, and of other people too, more plainly or more dimly, is subject to the same troubles as the son of Clinias. We do not know ourselves, held down by the forgetfulness that comes with birth into this world, and knocked off course by the turmoil of the unreasoning forms of life. And we think we know many things we do not know, because of the rational principles that are in us by our being. And we need the same help, so that we may stand back from our excessive conceit and find the care that is right for us.

@ch Pr.5 | The aim of the dialogue
Others gave the dialogue other aims; Proclus answers that its aim is what is most complete in it and runs through all of it: knowing our being. Exhortation, refutation, midwifery, praise and blame are the means, without which it cannot be reached, as purifications come before the mysteries.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Ἀλλ’ ὅτι μὲν προηγεῖσθαι
That knowing oneself must come before every other inquiry, and before all philosophical study, so to speak, has now been said. But someone may reproach us for so readily setting this down as the aim of the Alcibiades, since many other famous interpreters have written accounts of it, some giving it one purpose, some another. All the same, I should be ashamed of myself if, instead of what is most complete in the dialogue, which needs the greater knowledge and is the bound that takes in all its problems, namely what shows our being, what it is, and runs through all its main heads, I made the things that come second, less complete and more particular, the aims of the work before us.

@p
@greek Οὔτε γὰρ ἐπὶ μόνον
For we should not refer the dialogue's purpose to Alcibiades alone, as some have supposed: scientific study looks to what is common, and to what reaches to every like state. Nor should we put forward the instruments of dialectic, such as exhortation, midwifery or refutation, as the aims of the conversation; we should rather see to what end these too are directed. Nor should we leave the matters before us and carry the inquiry over to other beings, divine or daemonic, that have nothing to do with us (for the aim must be proper to the first foundation of philosophical study, since Socrates also says that he now approaches the young man for the first time). Nor, letting this aim go, should we contrive some other purpose for the dialogue, made up from outside it, when Socrates himself bears witness to this one.
!note Midwifery is Socrates' art of helping another bring his thoughts to birth, as he describes it in the Theaetetus (149a-151d).

@p
@greek Εἰ γὰρ παντὸς ἄλλου
If knowing ourselves must come before every other inquiry, what end of the first conversation could be more to the aim than to know ourselves? So exhortations and dissuasions, refutations and midwifery, praise and blame, count among the things without which the aim is not reached. Without them one cannot know oneself. We need exhortation towards what is really good, and dissuasion from what is really bad; midwifery to bring forth the reasons in us that are not twisted, and refutation to purify us of the double ignorance; praise to win over those being perfected, and blame to help them and cure them where they act badly.
!note "Things without which" is Plato's way of naming what a cause needs but is not the cause, in the Phaedo (99b).
!note The double ignorance is not knowing, and not knowing that one does not know; one of Taylor's footnotes explains it so.

@p
@greek Ὥσπερ οὖν ἐν ταῖς
As in the mysteries purifications come first, with sprinklings and cleansings, which are exercises for what is done in secret and for sharing in the divine, so, I think, the philosophical rite of perfection purifies and prepares beforehand those who set out towards it for the knowledge of themselves and for seeing our being as it shows itself.

@ch Pr.6 | What comes before the aim, what follows, and the dialogue as a universe
Some things in the dialogue lead to its aim, others follow from it; the aim is to know ourselves, and the good it brings is to become like the divine. Each dialogue has what the universe has: here likeness to God answers to the Good, self-knowledge to intellect, the arguments to soul, the style to form, the persons and setting to matter.
@taylor 6 8

@p
@greek Ὥστε σκοπὸς μὲν οὗτός
So this, and no other, is the aim of the dialogue. Of the things shown in it, some come before this purpose and others follow it, like conclusions tied to this end. Before it come the putting away of the double ignorance, the exhortation, and the like; after it come the showing of what virtue and happiness are, the condemnation of the many arts as knowing neither themselves nor what is theirs, nor anything at all, and whatever else of this kind is built up together at the end of the dialogue. But the most final and most central thing in the whole conversation is the study of our being. Indeed, if someone says that the end of the dialogue is the care of ourselves and the knowledge of it, he puts it rightly; but let him note that this belongs to us as the end, as the good, from what is proved, while the problem sought, for whose sake all the arguments are made, is to know ourselves.
!flag So that he will not err who establishes the care and knowledge of ourselves, as the end of the dialogue. | The Greek goes on to tell the end apart from the problem sought: the care of ourselves is the end, the good that comes from what is proved, while what the arguments seek is to know ourselves. Taylor stops before the distinction.

@p
@greek Ἄλλο γὰρ ἦν τὸν σκοπὸν
For the aim of the dialogue, to know, was one thing; the good, the one that comes from such a purpose, was another. As we have said elsewhere about the dialogues, each must have what the universe has: in each, something is set as answering to the Good, something to intellect, something to soul, something to form, and something to the underlying nature itself. Let us say, then, that here being made like the divine through care of ourselves answers to the Good; knowing ourselves, to intellect; the many proofs that lead us to this, the conclusion and, so to speak, all the reasoning in the dialogue, to soul; the character of the style, the weaving together of figures and forms of speech, and whatever else belongs to the power of expression, to form; and to matter, the persons, the occasion, and what some call the setting (hypothesis).
!note The first sentence is hard in the Greek. The Workshop reads it as the distinction just made: the aim is knowing ourselves, and the good is what that knowledge brings.
!flag and that which is called by rhetoricians the hypothesis, to matter | The Greek says "what some call the hypothesis" (para tisin); "by rhetoricians" is Taylor's.

@p
@greek Ταῦτα μὲν οὖν ἐν παντὶ
So much is in every dialogue. As for this one: the aim of this work is to know our being, and we are lovers of this knowledge so that we may reach our own perfection. And as among the causes themselves intellect hangs from the Good, so here what the dialogue seeks is yoked to the end of the conversation; and the end is what we have said.

@ch Pr.7 | The beginning of all philosophy
As knowing ourselves is the start of philosophy, this dialogue holds the seed of all of it: logic, ethics, the study of nature and of the divine. That is why Iamblichus set it first of the ten dialogues that hold Plato's whole philosophy.
@taylor 5

@p
@greek Ταῦτα καὶ περὶ τῆς προθέσεως
Let this be our account of the dialogue's purpose, now that we have shown first that one must begin one's own perfecting here, from a pure knowledge of oneself. This dialogue is the beginning of all philosophy, just as knowing ourselves is. That is why many logical theorems are scattered through it and handed down in it; many ethical ones, which help towards our whole inquiry into happiness, are cleared up; and many teachings that guide us towards the study of nature and the truth about the divine things themselves are sketched in outline. So, as it were, in this dialogue the common, single and whole outline of all philosophy is held, coming to light through our very first turning towards ourselves. And this, I think, is why the divine Iamblichus gives it the first place among the ten dialogues in which he thinks the whole philosophy of Plato is contained, the whole course of those dialogues being taken up beforehand in this one, as in a seed. Which the ten are, how they should be ordered, and how they are gathered up in the two that come after them, we have treated in its own place elsewhere.
!note Iamblichus' ten dialogues, and the two after them, the Timaeus and the Parmenides, are the curriculum the Workshop's Library follows (the Anonymous Prolegomena, ch. 26).
!flag It likewise contains information with respect to many things which contribute to physiology | "Physiology" is Taylor's word for the study of nature (physiologia), not the science of the body.

@ch Pr.8 | How others divided the dialogue
Some divided the dialogue by its manner of speech, into praise, refutation, exhortation and midwifery; others, more seriously, into ten arguments. Proclus praises the second, but finds both still busy with instruments rather than the matters themselves.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Ἀλλ’ ἐπειδὴ καὶ ταῦτα
Now that this too has been set out well enough, we must speak next of how the dialogue is divided. Some cut it by its manner of speaking, and have divided it into praise, refutation, exhortation and midwifery. The philosopher, they say, needs praise to win the young man over; refutation, to purify him of his excessive conceit; exhortation, to rouse him to share in virtue; dissuasion, to draw him away from becoming like the popular leaders, and from envy of those who have great power in the city; and midwifery, so that he may call him to the study of human nature and of the care that befits such a nature. They do say this when they divide the dialogue, but not as fully as could be done. For they spend their time on what is third from the truth, labour over the last things and lay hold of the instruments, but fall short of setting out the matters themselves.
!note "Third from the truth" is Plato's phrase for the imitation of an imitation, in the Republic (book 10, 597e).

@p
@greek Ἕτεροι δὲ αὖ τούτων
Others, more serious than these, let go of how the manner of speaking is divided, and go up to the reasoning and proving in the dialogue, and from there think fit to make the division. They count, first, an argument showing that Alcibiades does not know what is just; second, one declaring that the many are not good teachers of what is just; third, one concluding that, where there is question and answer, the one who speaks is the one who answers, not the one who asks; fourth, one in which it is shown that persuading many and persuading one are the work of the same knowledge; fifth, one reasoning that what is just is advantageous; sixth, another tied to it, which concludes that only the beautiful is good; seventh, one establishing that Alcibiades, in the double ignorance, does not know himself or the matters at hand; eighth, one proving that he has not even looked into who his real rivals are; ninth, one that shows him wholly ignorant of the way to take right care; and tenth, besides these, one in which our being is cleared up, with its three ways of being known and the kind of treatment that fits it.
!note The three ways are whether a human being is the soul, the body, or the two together (Alcibiades 129e-130c).

@p
@greek Οὐκοῦν καὶ τοὺς ταῦτα
We shall praise those who say this, then, as more refined than those before them and closer to the matters; but we shall not say that even they make the division as the matters require. They stand in the second rank, and have not wholly left off busying themselves with the instruments.

@ch Pr.9 | Iamblichus' division into three parts
The best division is Iamblichus', from the matters themselves: first, taking away the ignorance of reason; second, showing that natural gifts are not enough without complete virtue; third, recalling our true being and finding the right care. All the rest serves these three.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Ἐκεῖνος δὴ οὖν ὁ τρόπος
The way of dividing the dialogue that seems to me the most complete of all is the one the philosopher Iamblichus also approved. It starts from the matters themselves, gathers the whole plan of the work into three main heads, and refers to them both the methods of reasoning and the handling of the speech. For what comes second, and serves as an instrument, must always keep hold of the first and chief parts, and be handed down with its eye on them. How, then, do we say the dialogue is divided into its nearest and chief parts?

@p
@greek Πῶς δὲ ἄλλως ἢ
How else than this? Its aim is to bring to light the being of the human being, and to turn each of us towards himself, away from the drive that looks outward and from busying ourselves with what is not ours. This must come about as we purify our reason from what stands in its way and cuts off its turning, and train what is without reason and call it back to the perfection that accords with reason. So one main head comes first: the one that takes away the ignorance of reason, and clears away the obstacles to knowledge that arise in reason through birth into this world, with many arguments of every kind. Second after it is set the part of the dialogue which establishes that one must not be content with natural advantages and fall short of the practices that belong to complete virtue. Third after these is the part that gives the recollection of our true being, and the finding of the right care, and brings the fitting end to the whole purpose of the discussion. These, then, are the three parts of this dialogue. All the rest is taken up for their sake and helps towards them, both the proofs and what belongs to the expression.

@ch Pr.10 | Every argument leads to knowing ourselves
Proclus goes through the ten arguments and shows that each leads to self-knowledge: from our ignorance of what is just, through the soul's self-motion and learning as recollection, to the last, which shows that the human being is the soul.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Καὶ γὰρ τῶν συλλογισμῶν
For each of the arguments will be seen to lead us up to this one end: the study of what we are, and the knowledge of ourselves. If you will look at each on its own and examine the force it has, you will find that all of them go back to this one starting point. The first, which shows the ignorance of what is just that we all have from youth, blames nothing other than our own lack of knowledge. For though we have the rational principles of these things in our being, we do not turn to ourselves, nor take up a clear, articulated knowledge of them. This is the cause of our conceit that we know, and of the refutations we run into because of that conceit.

@p
@greek Ὁ δὲ δεύτερος ἀτιμάζων
The second, which dishonours the great crowd of people as no judge of what is just, draws us away from the whole multitude and from the life carried along by opinions, and leads each of us up to the one reason and the one knowledge, which exist in the soul itself by its being. The third, which shows that the one who answers is the one who speaks, makes plain the soul's power to move itself, shows that bringing forth reasons, and learning, are recollections, and does nothing but turn the one being perfected towards himself. The fourth, in which he concluded that persuading one and many belongs to the same knowledge, shows what the complete form of activity is, which does not stand outside itself but is turned back towards itself, as intellect is. For it fills everything that is less than itself, and is present alike to one and to many, neither divided among those that share in it nor altered by them, but staying one and the same in itself, and from itself perfecting the others. The fifth, after this, which proves that what is just is advantageous, places the advantage of each person in the soul, where justice also is, and persuades us to look for our being nowhere but in the soul, where the good also exists together with the just. For our advantage is not in one place and our existence in another.
!note The soul as what moves itself is from the Phaedrus (245c-e); learning as recollection, from the Meno (81c-d) and the Phaedo (72e-73b).
!note At "it fills everything that is less than itself", Cousin notes that both his manuscripts read differently; the modern version follows his printed text.

@p
@greek Ὁ δὲ ἕκτος, ὁ τὸ καλὸν
The sixth, which brings the beautiful and the good together into one, draws the soul away from the beauties that appear, leads it up to the beauty that belongs to intellect, to reason and to knowledge, and prepares it to turn towards itself and see these things in itself, not in what lies outside it. The seventh, which purifies away, with the double ignorance, everything that blocks our knowing ourselves and keeps us from turning towards ourselves, shows, by driving out the excessive conceit, that the greatest of evils is this: not knowing ourselves. Along with it comes to light the greatest and most complete of goods, which has its existence in knowing ourselves. The eighth argument shows him who his real rivals are. And this too helps towards the whole aim, since we do not know ourselves, whether in the power of our soul, or of our body, or in what we have outside us.

@p
@greek Ἵν’ οὖν μὴ παντελῶς
So that he may not, wholly ignorant of himself and of how he falls short of his rivals in everything, fail to notice that he has fallen into the greatest misfortunes, this argument too, I think, is necessary to the whole purpose of the dialogue.

@p
@greek Ὁ δὲ ἔννατος
The ninth, in which he is shown not to know the way to take care of ourselves, makes plain that the most authoritative starting point of this knowledge too is to discern our being. For on this hangs the judging of the right care; or rather, it is marked out by this very knowledge. For everything that moves itself perfects itself by turning towards itself. Whoever has known himself has also grasped, within himself, the care of himself; whoever does not know himself is without knowledge of the way of care too. Last of all, the tenth, which shows that the human being has his existence in the soul, brings to light at once the form of our being, and gives us the most complete knowledge of the life that acts of itself, taking together in one proof both our existence and our perfection. If, then, we say that each of the arguments in the dialogue, and all of them together, lead us round to knowing ourselves, we should perhaps hit on Plato's meaning.
!note The tenth argument is in the Alcibiades (129b-130e): the human being is the soul, so "Know yourself" bids us know the soul.

@treatise 103a | On the opening words: what the preface shows
Proclus comments on the dialogue's first words, "Son of Cleinias, I think you wonder that I, who was the first to love you, am the only one who does not leave you now the others have stopped." First he reads their wording. The preface is not there for show: it turns Alcibiades to himself, and through himself to Socrates' knowledge, as the whole dialogue will. Socrates says "I think" because he speaks of a changing thing, and because the young man is proud; he calls him by his father's name because the boy loves that name, because the father stands for the true father of souls, and because love comes from the Father. Of Socrates' three sciences, dialectic, midwifery and love, it is love that leads here.

@ch 103a.1 | The preface serves the aim of the dialogue
Plato's prefaces are neither ornament nor mere history: like the rites of a mystery, all their parts serve the one aim of the dialogue. Here the very first words show that aim, self-knowledge, by turning Alcibiades to himself and making him want to learn why Socrates alone still loves him.

@ch 103a.2 | Three kinds of turning
Everything that turns, turns to what is worse, to itself, or to what is better. The turn to the worse is the fall of a soul that has lost its wings; the turns to itself and to the better are found in the gods too, as the Parmenides shows. So the end of the dialogue says that whoever turns to himself will see the divine; and the preface already turns Alcibiades to himself, and from himself to love of Socrates' knowledge.

@greek Ἔστι μὲν οὖν τὰ εἴδη

@ch 103a.3 | Why Socrates says "I think"
A good man fits his knowledge to what he knows: intellect for what is intelligible, reasoning for the middle things, the senses for things sensed, conjecture for images, as the Republic divides them; the Timaeus says the same of accounts, and the Cratylus of names. Alcibiades' state of mind is a changing thing, so Socrates rightly says "I think": the word marks the changing object, not doubtful knowledge.
@taylor add001

@p
@greek Δεύτερον δὲ ἕτερον ἐπὶ
A second thing follows from this. A good man always brings to bear on each object of knowledge the knowledge matched to it. About things that can be otherwise and are carried now this way, now that, he does not claim a knowledge that is fixed, defined and unchanging; and about things that are necessary and always the same, he does not hold an opinion that is doubtful, undefined and unsteady. Rather, as Socrates himself divided them in the Republic, he looks at all intelligible things together by simple, single and intellective knowledge; he examines the middle accounts of the things that are by the scientific and reasoning power that unfolds the forms and puts them together; third from the truth, by perception, which touches its own objects through instruments, he judges all things sensed as is fitting; and lastly, by the power of conjecture, he knows the objects of conjecture, which are images of things sensed.
!note The four powers are those of the divided line in the Republic (511d-e): intellect (noēsis), reasoning (dianoia), trust in the senses (pistis) and conjecture (eikasia).

He says that the accounts of what truly is cannot be refuted and are fixed, while those of things not of that nature, but carried along in becoming, are likely and have a share of chance, as he says somewhere. The word marks the knowable thing as easily turned; it does not convict Socrates' knowledge of being undefined, mixed with ignorance and open to dispute. It is not right, says Aristotle, to accept a geometer speaking persuasively, or an orator claiming to demonstrate. So neither a man who speaks of necessary things by conjecture, nor one who speaks of things conjectured as if they were fixed, would ever be a sensible interpreter of things. This is why Socrates had to add "I think".

@treatise 103a-love | On love: its orders, and the two kinds of lover
Having said that the opening words show Socrates as a lover, Proclus turns from the wording to the matter: what love is. As other gods have their own powers, the whole order of love turns all things to divine beauty; it is the middle between beauty and those it leads up, which is why the Symposium calls Love a great daemon. Its ranks run from the hidden summit among the first gods down through the gods before the world to the many loves in the world: angels, daemons, heroes and the souls of men. Souls that choose the life of love come down to care for the well-born and lead them up with themselves; souls that fall from love's gift through bad upbringing chase the images of beauty and sink into matter. Then, from Socrates' own first words, Proclus draws five marks that part the divine lover from the vulgar one, and says why Socrates still calls the others lovers.

@ch 103a.8 | Love turns all things to divine beauty
As each kind of god has its own power, making, giving life, guarding or saving, so the whole order of love is the cause that turns all things to divine beauty, leading the lower up to it and filling them from it. It stands in the middle, between beauty and those it leads up, which is why the Symposium calls Love a great daemon: love holds among the gods the middle place that daemons hold between the divine and the mortal.

Some help by prophecy, some by the rites, some by divine medicine; souls that choose the life of love are moved by the god who watches over beautiful boys to care for the well-born, and from visible beauty they lead themselves and their beloved up to beauty itself.

And indeed the souls of men too have a share in such inspiration. Through their kinship with the god they are moved about the beautiful, and they come down into the place of becoming to do good to less perfect souls, in providence for those who need saving. For gods, and the gods' followers, stay in their own abodes and do good to all that is second to them, and turn it to themselves; but the souls of men, coming down and touching becoming, copy the good-like providence of the gods.

@ch 103a.11 | True love and its counterfeit
Souls possessed by love use visible beauty as a clean vehicle to be carried round to intelligible beauty, and light the way for others. Souls that fall from love's gift through bad upbringing, but are lovers by nature, take the images of beauty for beauty: they admire bodies, fall away from the divine into the darkness of matter, and, thinking they hasten to union, are carried off into scattering and the sea of unlikeness. Visible beauty, mixed with matter, can never be pure.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Αἱ μὲν οὖν κάτοχοι τῷ ἔρωτι
Souls possessed by love, then, and sharing the inspiration from there, use visible beauty as an undefiled vehicle and are carried round to intelligible beauty, and they set that up as the end of their activity. Kindling a light for the less perfect souls, they lead them all up too to the divine, and dance with them round the one source of all beauty.

@p
@greek αἱ δὲ τῆς ἐκεῖθεν δόσεως
But the souls that have fallen from the gift that comes from there, through bad upbringing, though they were given a lover's nature, fall upon the images of beautiful things through ignorance of the true ones. They make much of the beauties that are in matter and divided, and are struck with wonder at them, not knowing what has happened to them. @ch 103a.12 | Five marks of the two lovers, from Socrates' first words
Love's gift taken in badly makes the tyrannical, unbridled life, as Plotinus says an outflow of intellect makes cunning. Socrates' first words then show five marks of the true lover against the vulgar one: he gives wonder instead of feeling it; he watches the beloved from infancy and comes when the bloom is gone; he stays the same; he keeps apart until the time is right; and he is the one first and only lover, where the others are many.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Ἐπειδὴ τοίνυν ἀπ’ ἀλλήλων
So we have told apart the many forms of the orders of love, and the souls that share in it truly from those that turn aside the gift that comes from there. (For "an outflow of intellect makes cunning", says Plotinus, and a share of wisdom gone wrong makes the sophistry here below; so too love's light, when it finds a bad receiver, produces the tyrannical, unbridled life.) Since, then, we have told these apart, let us go through Plato's very words and look at the differences between the lovers, which Socrates sketches for us by his first words; let us consider who is the divine lover and what he gives the beloved, and who the common lover is, who follows the multitude in the soul.
!note Plotinus' saying is given as Proclus quotes it; the modern version does not name the place in the Enneads.

@p
@greek Ὁ μὲν τοίνυν δημώδης
The common and vulgar lover wonders at the beloved, pursues him, and is all aflutter about him; the true lover gives the beloved cause for wonder, and stirs him in every way to amazement at the life within him. And this is fitting. For the worse lover owns that he is the beloved's subject, and says he needs him; the other is sufficient in himself, full of power, and promises to give his own goods to the souls of those he loves.
!note Socrates' first words are "Son of Cleinias, I think you wonder" (103a): it is the beloved who wonders.

@p
@greek Δεύτερον τοίνυν
Second, the inspired lover watches over the beloved from his swaddling clothes, and comes to him when his bloom has ended, when the bodily tumults have ceased; for he is not a lover of the body, but of the soul, and when the body's flower is over, the beauty of the soul shines out. But the common lover reaches for the beloved's prime as for ripe fruit, and takes no trouble over him before or after: as soon as the bloom ends he leaves the boy, as befits his own principles. Being a lover of the body, he measures his love by the prime of visible beauty.
!note So the dialogue itself (131c-d): the lover of Alcibiades' body goes away when it stops flowering; the lover of his soul stays, "as the body's bloom ends".

@p
@greek Τὸ τρίτον τοίνυν
Third, the one is steadfast and always the same, since he is bound to something steadfast; the other changes easily, and while there is vulgar, but quick to tip and ready to leave the boy. For his friendship for what is in matter makes him like what he loves, and so he too, surely, becomes unstable, and readily changes his intimacy with the boy.
!note Pausanias' speech in the Symposium (183e): the vulgar lover "is not steadfast, since he loves nothing steadfast; as soon as the bloom of the body he loved ends, he flies away".
!note "Like what he loves": Cousin prints τῷ ἐραστῷ, "what is lovable" to him, that is the body; one of his manuscripts (A) has τῷ ἐραστῇ, which would make the beloved "like his lover".

@p
@greek Τέταρτον στοιχεῖον
Let the fourth mark of the difference between the men be this: the one, even when present with the beloved, is in a way apart from him, neither touching him nor talking with him, nor speaking to him at all, until the time for helping his soul has come; the other loves to be joined to him through the senses, and becomes a nuisance, ever changing, and inventing every kind of pretext to talk to him.
!note Socrates says at 103a that the other lovers "were a nuisance to you, talking with you", while he "did not even speak to you" in all these years.
!note "In a way apart from him": Cousin prints κεχώρισται, "is apart"; his manuscripts read κεχάρισται, "does him a kindness", and he refers to his annotations.

@p
@greek Πέμπτον ἀπ’ αὐτῶν
A fifth you could take from the very words: the divine lover is called one, first and only, as being akin to the one and the good, and as stretching up to the simple, one-formed pattern of beautiful things; the vulgar lover is many, and heaped together at random. For in calling the lovers of images "others", but the inspired friend "first and only", Socrates shows up their life as unbounded, divided and scattered every way, and honours his virtue as godlike, far from the multitude in matter, and set above all the passions of becoming.
!note At 103a Socrates calls himself "the first of your lovers", who "alone" does not leave, "the others having stopped".

@ch 103a.13 | Why Socrates still calls the others lovers
Much else parts the two kinds of lover. Socrates still lets the many be called lovers because it is not yet settled what a man truly is, and where Alcibiades himself is to be placed; once that is settled, Socrates will be seen as his only lover, and the others as lovers of what is Alcibiades', not of Alcibiades, wearing an image of love about an image of beauty. Each order of love goes with its own beauty, and the eyes of the many cannot bear to look at the truth, as the Eleatic Stranger says.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Πολλὰ δ’ ἂν καὶ ἄλλα
One could say much else, stirred by these words, about what separates these lovers from one another.

@p
@greek Ἐραστὰς γὰρ οὐκ ἀποδοκιμάζει
Socrates does not yet refuse to call the many lovers too, because it has not yet been settled what the true man is, and where Alcibiades himself is to be placed. @treatise 103a-first | Why Socrates loved first, and stays: the lover as a good daemon
Proclus turns to Socrates' own words, that he was the first of the lovers and alone stays when the rest have stopped. As the One is at work before, with and after all things, the divine lover cares for the beloved before the vulgar lovers, alongside them, and after they have gone, and speaks only when the crowd has scattered. Proclus compares the scene to the mysteries, where earthly daemons appear and distract before the god shows himself, and sets Socrates in the place of Alcibiades' good daemon: watching from childhood, silent, speaking only when the soul is ready. Read another way, Socrates is to Alcibiades what intellect is to the soul, and the vulgar lovers what the many-headed beast is; the two readings join, since the same being is a daemon to a man and intellect to a soul. Then he asks why Alcibiades wonders (the many measure care by time, not by perfection), why the vulgar lovers still bear the name of lover, and how Socrates, from his first words, shows himself the only lover.

@ch 103a.14 | First, with, and after the others, as the One is
Socrates begins before the other lovers, keeps on with them, and stays when they stop. So the more perfect embraces the work of its images, and is present to anything before the lower; so the One is at work before all things, with them and after them. The divine lover alone is called "first", while the many lovers are given the rank of the divided multitude; he waits until they have tired, and comes when the beloved is calm and fit to share his company.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Ταῦτα μὲν οὖν περὶ τούτων
So much for this. That Socrates begins before the others, carries his care for the young man along with theirs, and does not leave when the others have stopped, shows, I think, through such a life, that the higher patterns and the more perfect everywhere take in the activities of their images in every way, and that the lower things are present to nothing to which the more perfect have not given themselves long before. It shows too that in every order of the things that are, the One has taken in all the rest, and is at work before them, with them and after them.

@p
@greek Ἄντικρυς οὖν
So the text plainly gave the inspired friend the eminence proper to the One, calling him alone and first to care for the less perfect, and gave the many lovers the rank of the multitude that is divided in every way. And as there the One is at work before the others, with the others and after the others, if it is right to say so, so the divine lover attends to the beloved before the vulgar lovers, and while they are with him does not stand back from caring for him. It is then above all that he takes thought, lest the boy give himself up unawares to the image of love that lives in matter and belongs to the crowd. @ch 103a.15 | An image from the mysteries
Everywhere what is less perfect slips in ahead of the more perfect and draws unready souls away. In philosophy, the life of the sophist pulls the less perfect from the sight of what is to appearance; in the holiest rites, earthly daemons rush in before the god appears, so the gods bid us not look at them until the rites have armed us, as the Oracles say. So the vulgar lovers, like earthly daemons, draw the young man away from his ascent; when they are gone, the inspired lover appears, as the god does when the rites have cleansed away the daemons that drag souls down.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Καὶ εἴ με δεῖ τοὐμὸν λέγειν
And if I must say what I think, this too seems to me to give a wonderful image of what is done in the mystery rites. For everywhere the less perfect slips in ahead of the more perfect, draws round to itself the souls that are not yet set right, and pulls them away from what is better. So, just as in the intellective sights of philosophy the life of the sophist is a hindrance, and keeping company with it drags the less perfect away from the contemplation of the things that are to the appearance that goes with what comes to be and passes away, so too, in the ascent to divine love, the crowd of vulgar lovers stands in the way: it slips in under the guise of the true lover, and drags the young man's soul down from the Olympian goods above to what is earthly and dark.

@greek Κατὰ τὰ αὐτὰ δὴ καὶ ἐν ταῖς ἁγιωτάταις
In the same way, in the holiest of the rites, before the god's presence, onrushes and apparitions of certain earthly daemons appear, throwing those being initiated into confusion, tearing them away from the undefiled goods and calling them forth into matter. That is why the gods bid us not look at those daemons before we have been fenced about by the powers that come from the rites: "For you must not look on them before your body is initiated." And for this reason the Oracles add that they always charm souls and lead them away from the rites. Like the earthly daemons, then, are the vulgar lovers, who flow round the young man and lead him away from the ascent to the divine, and make him unfit for the company of the inspired friend. So, as there the divine shows itself after the cleansing away of every such daemonic thing that leads souls down into matter, so too the inspired lover appears after the flight of the many lovers bound to becoming, and gives the beloved a share in his company.

!note "Fenced about by the powers that come from the rites": the Greek (φραχθῶμεν) is "be fenced, armed".

@ch 103a.16 | Socrates in the place of a good daemon
Socrates holds towards Alcibiades the place of a good daemon. The daemon watches over us from youth, begins its care before our parents, stays with us through life as the pilot of the whole of it, and after death leads us to our judges, as the Phaedo says; so Socrates comes before all Alcibiades' friends, stays alongside the many lovers, and when they stop, calls him to justice. As the daemon is with us unseen and speaks only to those most ready, so Socrates follows the boy in silence and speaks when he sees his life loosened from matter and free of the crowd.

@p
@greek Ἀτεχνῶς οὖν μοι δοκεῖ
So it seems to me that Socrates simply holds the place of a good daemon towards Alcibiades: like it, he watches him from youth, guards him, oversees all his activities, and looks on his words and deeds. And as the daemon begins its care for us even before our parents, stands over us with our parents as the pilot, so to speak, of our whole life, and after our release from here takes thought for our journey to our judges ("for", he says, "it leads each one, the daemon whose lot he was while living"), in the same way Socrates is ahead of all the young man's kin and friends in caring for him, adds his own concern alongside many lovers, and when the others have stopped, he himself visits and keeps company with him, and calls his beloved back to justice as a whole and to the goods that truly belong to the soul.

@ch 103a.17 | Wonder, the start of philosophy
Socrates comes at the moment when the beloved is most awake to receive him, and says so: "You wonder what it is with me, and how I stay when the other lovers have fled." Wonder is the start of philosophy, as the Theaetetus says, which is why Iris is called the daughter of Thaumas; so release from the vulgar lovers and the flutter towards philosophy run together, for souls cleansed of the evils of becoming are made ready for the divine goods.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Πρόσεισιν οὖν ὁ Σωκράτης
So Socrates comes at this moment, when he saw the beloved most awake to receive his company; and surely he shows this himself, for "you wonder", he says, "what my case is, and how I stay when the other lovers have fled". This wondering is the beginning of philosophy, as he says himself in the Theaetetus; and for this reason, in the ancient story, Iris is called the daughter of Thaumas, Wonder. If, then, he comes to the young man just when the young man wonders at the philosopher's life and seeks the causes of what goes on in it, the young man would have the start of his impulse towards philosophy. And release from the vulgar lovers and the flutter of excitement about philosophy somehow run together: for souls purified of the evils that pour round them from becoming make themselves ready to share in the divine goods.
!note The Theaetetus (155d): "this feeling, wondering, is very much a philosopher's; there is no other beginning of philosophy than this, and whoever said Iris was the child of Thaumas did not get the genealogy wrong."
!note "What my case is, and how I stay": Proclus gives the sense of 103a-b in his own words.

@ch 103a.18 | Alcibiades between Socrates and the vulgar lovers
Socrates, as an inspired lover led up to intelligible beauty, stands to Alcibiades as intellect to the soul; the vulgar lovers, carried along by sense and imagination, are like the soul's many-headed beast. Alcibiades is the rational soul in between, torn from below by the passions as the Titans tore, held up from above by intellect as by Athena, the saviour. And as intellect is always at work in us but shows itself only when the soul has put off the crowd that grew on it from becoming, so Socrates is always present but speaks only when the lovers have gone.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Σκόπει δὴ καὶ κατὰ τὴν ἀναλογίαν
Consider too, by this analogy, what rank Alcibiades has towards his lovers, and how this also helps the study before us. Socrates, as an inspired lover led up to intelligible beauty itself, set himself in the place answering to the intellect of the soul (for what else is joined to the intelligible but intellect, and all that has an intellective life?); while the motley, vulgar tribe of lovers, swept along with sense and imagination and given over to the many-headed beast of the soul, copies the life that answers to it, the life by which it marks its own life too (for every soul is that part by which it lives, and has defined itself by it). Alcibiades is somehow between them: torn from below by the lovers ruled by passion, and dragged down to the worse lot; helped from above by Socrates, who is one, saved by him, and watched over, lest he suffer something at the hands of the lovers flowing round him. We must, then, by the analogy of the extremes, place Alcibiades too in the rational soul. On it the passions and the irrational powers still hang, plotting, as it were, against the rational life and trying to tear it in pieces like Titans; above it is set intellect, holding it up, as Athena does, from its leaning and its slide into matter. For it is Athena's to keep life undivided, for which Pallas Athena was called the saviour; and it is the Titans' to divide it, and call it forth into becoming.
!note The many-headed beast is the Republic's image of the appetites (588c-e).
!note Tearing like Titans recalls the story, told by the Orphics and read by the Neoplatonists, of the Titans who tore the child Dionysus to pieces; Proclus does not tell it here.

@p
@greek Ὥσπερ τοίνυν ὁ νοῦς οὐκ ἀεὶ
So, just as intellect does not always show itself to souls, but only when they put off the great crowd that grew on them later from becoming, as Timaeus says, so Socrates gives the young man a share in his company when he has become free of the many lovers who also later ranged themselves round him, and has leisure for philosophy and what can lead to it. And as intellect is always at work about us and always gives the light of thought, both before we sink into unreason, and while we live with the passions, and when the passions in us have been put to sleep, yet we are not always aware of it, but only when we are clean of the great surge of becoming and bring our life to anchor in a kind of calm (for then intellect shows itself to us and, as it were, speaks to us; then the one who was silent before, and present in quiet, gives us a share in its voice): so too the divine lover is present to the beloved before the many lovers, with them and after them, but silent, taming him, and only watching over him; and when they have stopped, he gives the beloved a share in speech, offers him a common life, shows him who he is, and that his love is provident, like the good, and leads upward, not like that of those many lovers, divided, needy, in matter and a thing of images.
!note The Timaeus (42c): the soul overcomes "the great crowd that later grew on it, from fire and water and air and earth".

@ch 103a.19 | Two ways of seeing it: daemon and intellect
The lover of such sights may go by either analogy: Socrates as the good daemon, the vulgar lovers as earthly daemons of matter, the young man as one being initiated who flees the daemons that drag him down; or Socrates as intellect. The two join: Alcibiades is both a soul and a soul using a body. As soul he has Socrates for his intellect, as soul using a body for his good daemon. The Symposium sets the daemonic between gods and men; the Timaeus sets soul between intellect and body. Since the dialogue asks what man is, Socrates will not speak of intellect but remind the young man of his daemon, calling himself now a daemon, now a god.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Ἔστι μὲν οὖν καὶ διὰ ταύτης
So the one who loves the sight of such things can travel by this analogy; and he can go by the one we spoke of before, likening Socrates to a good daemon, the vulgar lovers to earthly daemons of matter, and the young man to one being initiated, who runs from the tribe of daemons that drag down, brings himself to the good daemon, and hands it the whole steering of his life.

@p
@greek Εἰ δὲ δεῖ καὶ συνάπτειν
And if we must join both approaches, think of Alcibiades as twofold: as a soul simply, and as a soul using a body. These are not the same, just as the pilot and the man by himself are not the same, nor the charioteer and the person underneath the role; and in general what is not shared in differs from what is shared in, what is by itself from what is seen together with another, and what stands apart from what has been given a place beside something else. Since, then, Alcibiades is thought of in two ways, as a soul and as a soul using a body, in so far as he is a soul Socrates keeps towards him the place of intellect; in so far as he is a soul using a body, the place of the good daemon. And the same being is a daemon in relation to a man, and intellect in relation to a soul. For when we think of the daemonic middle, we see it between gods and men; when of intellect, we fasten soul to its highest point, and the body to soul. You have the one division in the Symposium, where he says somewhere that the daemonic is a middle between gods and men; and the other in the Timaeus, for soul stands in the middle between intellect and body. The same being, then, is daemon and intellect: intellect of the soul, daemon of the human soul. That is why here, since the inquiry is about the nature of man, there will be no mention of intellect, but Socrates will remind the young man of the providence of the daemon, calling himself sometimes a daemon, sometimes a god.

!note The Symposium (202e): "everything daemonic is between god and mortal". The Timaeus (30b): the maker put "intellect in soul, and soul in body"; soul is in the middle.

@ch 103a.20 | Why Alcibiades wonders: time, not perfection
Why does Alcibiades wonder at Socrates' long care? Less perfect people measure the efforts and activities of souls by the length of time, not by how perfect the activity is, as the many call a man skilled because he spent long with teachers. So Alcibiades wonders that Socrates has loved him longer than the others, and Socrates, knowing the young man's nature, guesses his wonder, though he himself began first, kept his love steadfast, and is only now beginning to show himself.
@taylor none

@p
@greek Ἀλλὰ τούτου μὲν ἡ αἰτία
But the cause of this will be told more clearly later. Let us go back to the start and ask what is the reason that Alcibiades wonders at Socrates' zeal for him, his constant attendance, and the lasting of his love when the others have stopped, and how Socrates guesses what the young man is thinking. We say that the less perfect among men are used to measuring the efforts and activities of souls by the length of time, and not by the perfection of the activity. Or have we not noticed that the many call the man who spent longer with teachers skilled or knowing in whatever he happened to spend that time on?

@p
@greek Καίτοι θαυμαστὸν οὐδὲν
Yet it is no wonder that the better-natured needs less time and work to be made perfect. Still, those who cannot judge the form itself, by itself, and the settled state, think that time has been given them by nature as a sufficient measure. Since, then, the many are used to judging each thing in this way, it was likely that Alcibiades too would wonder what Socrates' case was, who had spent longer in love for him than the other lovers; and that Socrates, knowing the youthfulness of his nature, would have guessed the wonder in him; even if he himself, for other reasons, gave himself first to caring for him, kept his lover's character steadfast, and has not even now ever left off his love. On the contrary, it is only now that he begins to make himself plain to his beloved.

@ch 103a.21 | Why the vulgar lovers are still called lovers
The licentious man never claims the name of temperate, nor the coward that of brave; yet vulgar lovers, who drag the beloved down, want the name of lover that the inspired lovers have. Proclus gives three reasons: all lovers share one end, kinship with the beautiful, and the lowest beauty too bears the name of beauty; all lovers are mad, though one madness is above self-control and the other has fallen below it; and what is more divine, by its overflowing power, gives even its fallings-away an image of itself, so the image shares the pattern's name.
@taylor none

@greek Ἔτι τοίνυν ἑξῆς
Next, then, let us look for the reason why the licentious man would never claim the name of a self-controlled man, nor the unjust or the coward the name of a just or a brave man; while those who are vulgar in their loves, and have fallen from the rightness of the pursuit, want to be called lovers and to share one name with the inspired, though they have not shared the same choice, but are even set against them. For the inspired stretch their beloved up towards the divine, the shining and the one in form; the others drag their souls down to the godless, the dark and the scattered.

@p
@greek Τάχα οὖν ἂν πρὸς τὴν ζήτησιν
Perhaps we might fairly meet this question by saying that the licentious and the self-controlled have not the same end: as their settled states differ wholly, so do their ends. But for all lovers the end is the same, kinship with the beautiful; it is forgetfulness and ignorance of what is beautiful first that makes the bad lovers flow down about the beauties in matter. So, just as the lowest beauty has the same name as what is beautiful first, though it has stood out of its own nature (for the beautiful is in form, and this is mixed with the formless and the ugly), so too the lowest lover claims to be called by the same name as the first, being of a rank with the lowest beauty.

@p
@greek Καὶ μὴν καὶ κατ’ ἄλλον τρόπον
And in another way too you could see that the names have shared in these things not unreasonably. The self-controlled and the licentious have been given the most opposite states, and act according to them; but all lovers, in so far as they are mad, are somehow affected in the same way, even if some are thought of by the better form of madness, others by the worse. For one madness is better than self-control, the other has fallen away from it. In so far, then, as both are disposed madly about the beautiful, they share the same name; but the manner of their madness is altered, and the beautiful, being different, makes some inspired lovers, others vulgar ones.
!note "Act according to them": Cousin prints κατὰ ταυτὰ, "in the same ways", but both his manuscripts have κατὰ ταύτας, "according to these" (the states), and his Latin translator reads so; the sense, opposite states and so opposite acts, asks for the manuscripts' reading, which the modern version follows.
!note The madness better than self-control is the Phaedrus' (244d): the ancients bear witness that "madness from a god is finer than self-control from men".

@p
@greek Ἔτι δὴ οὖν τὸ τρίτον
Third, then, we say that the more divine things, by their overflowing power, adorn even their own fallings-away, and give these too some image-like show of their own character. Self-control cannot do this for licentiousness; but the inspired art of the lover, being more divine than self-control, gives something even to its own image and sends down into it a dim trace, which is why it is called an image. In this way, then, it also shares the same name; for everywhere images aim to share the name of their patterns.

@ch 103a.22 | Socrates the only lover
The true lover is the inspired one, as Socrates will say, calling himself Alcibiades' only lover because he cares for the true Alcibiades, the soul; the others fought over an image, as Homer says, and fled when its bloom ended. From the first words Socrates shows himself the only lover: first before the others, alone after them, and different even while they were there, watching over the boy like a daemon or a god. For what stands apart in each kind is alone, even when a multitude comes with it. The young man will see this soon, when he has looked at himself and what is his, and can tell the pattern from the image.

@greek Ταῦτα καὶ πρὸς ταύτην
Let this be our answer to this question. It was said before, too, that the real lover is the inspired one, as Socrates himself will say further on, showing himself the only lover of Alcibiades because of his zeal for the true Alcibiades: he is the lover of the soul, while the others did not even pretend to love it. "They fought one another over an image", as the poem says; and so, when they saw the image's bloom ending, they were gone.
!note Socrates says it at 131e: "there was not, it seems, a lover of Alcibiades son of Cleinias, nor is there, but one only, Socrates". The verse is Homer's (Iliad 5.451-452), where Trojans and Greeks fight over the phantom of Aeneas.

@p
@greek Εἰ δὲ δεῖ τὰ τούτων ἀληθέστερα
If we must say something truer than this: straight from his first syllables Socrates shows himself to be Alcibiades' only lover. For if he began before the others, he was plainly then the only lover; and if, when they have stopped, he still tends the beloved, he would be the only lover now too; and if, even while they were there, the manner of his love was different, and they were a nuisance to the young man while he took thought for him from outside, like some daemon or god watching over him, then too, clearly, he was the only lover. The cause of this is that whatever stands apart in each nature of the things that are is alone, even if there is some multitude with it. For this multitude, if it has anything good, has it through union with that; and whatever is ranked with something else can neither be, nor be called, alone. So Socrates is the only lover, as standing apart from the multitude of vulgar lovers; for they have no rank or relation towards him, because of their unlikeness to him.

@p
@greek Ἔστι μὲν οὖν καὶ ἐν τῷ παρόντι
So even now Socrates has been shown by these words to be Alcibiades' only lover; but the young man will see it a little later, when he has looked at himself and what is his. For then he will be able to tell what is the pattern and what the image, what is feigned in love and what is true, and who is the lover falsely so called, decked out in a name not his own, and who belongs to the god whose name he bears. So much for these things. Now, if you like, let us go back to love and take up the more secret accounts of it.
!note "Himself and what is his": the dialogue's own distinction (128a-131a), between a man himself, his soul, and what is his.
